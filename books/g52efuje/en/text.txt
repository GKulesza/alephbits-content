**TAIWAN**

Chen Ching Ting stood before the automatic gate at the airport in Frankfurt am Main. Behind her was a long flight from Taipei, ahead of her the Frankfurt Book Fair — one of the most important events in her professional life. She was a publisher; she had brought catalogues, book samples and business cards in her suitcase. And a passport.

Golden letters on a burgundy cover: TAIWAN.

She pressed the passport to the reader. The gate flashed green — the facial scanner recognised her, the doors opened. She stepped forward. And then the system froze.

Red light. A sound that felt like a warning. The doors did not close, but an official in a navy uniform appeared in front of her.

– Please follow me.

Ching Ting did not understand. Everything had been in order — a biometric passport, a valid visa, return tickets. Except that the gate had not let her face through. Or perhaps it was not the gate — perhaps it was a system that looked at her and saw someone she was not.

In a small room without windows she sat down on a plastic chair. In front of her stood two officers. One of them was holding her passport and turning it over in his hands, page by page, as if looking for a flaw. The other looked at her, then at the photo in the passport, then back at her.

– Is this you? – he asked, pointing at the photo.

– Yes. It's me.

– You look different.

Ching Ting thought about the braces she had had fitted two months earlier. About the dental work that had slightly changed the shape of her jaw. About the fatigue after the long flight, which made her eyes a little more closed than in the photo.

– I wear braces – she said. – And I went to the dentist. My face has changed a little.

The officer did not reply. For a moment he looked at her in silence, then turned to his colleague and they exchanged a few words in German. Ching Ting did not understand, but she sensed something worrying in their voices — uncertainty slowly turning into suspicion.

– Who are you really? – one of them suddenly asked.

– I am Chen Ching Ting. I am from Taiwan.

– That is not a state.

– I am from Taiwan – she repeated calmly. – I have a Taiwan passport.

– Your passport is forged – said the officer. – The number does not exist in the system. We cannot find it.

Ching Ting felt her heart start to beat faster. For a moment she thought it was a joke, that someone would walk in at any moment and say: _a mistake, we apologise_. But no one came. Instead, the officer began to go through her phone — messages, photos, contacts. He looked at pictures of her family, her office, her books. And he kept shaking his head, as if he could not understand who this woman sitting before him was.

– Who are you really? – he asked again. This time louder. – Why did you come here?

Ching Ting tried to stay calm. She knew her passport was legal. She knew Taiwan met every criterion of a state — it had a permanent population, a government controlling its territory, its own legal system, its own army, its own passports, its own elections. She knew her country was a democracy, while China was a communist regime. She knew that between them lay 140 kilometres of strait that was at once a bridge and an abyss.

But the official in Frankfurt did not want to hear any of that. For him Taiwan was only a problem — a dot on the map that did not fit into any system. Her passport was forged because it did not exist in the German database. And if it did not exist in the database, it meant she did not exist either. Or that she existed as someone else — as a Chinese woman pretending to be Taiwanese, or as a fugitive who had stolen someone else's document.

Four hours. That is how long the interrogation lasted. Four hours in which Ching Ting repeated her first name, her last name, her address in Taipei, her work, her dreams. Four hours in which the officers asked the same questions, as if hoping that at some point she would grow tired and say something different.

And then — suddenly — the door opened. Another person came in, someone of higher rank, and said a few words in German. The officers looked at each other, then one of them turned to Ching Ting.

– We apologise. It was a misunderstanding. You may go.

Ching Ting stood up. Her legs were stiff, her hands trembling. She took her passport, her phone, her bag.

– It was a misunderstanding – the official repeated. – Please treat it as if it had never happened.

As if it had never happened.

Ching Ting left the room, passed through passport control and collected her luggage. In the arrivals hall a taxi driver was waiting for her with a sign bearing her name. He smiled and opened the door.

– Welcome to Germany – he said.

Ching Ting sat in the back seat. Through the window she could see the airport — planes taking off and landing, passengers hurrying to their gates. Everything looked normal. Only in her head those words kept echoing: _Your passport is forged. The number does not exist in the system._

She knew it was not the officers' fault. It was a problem of the system — a system in which China is the sole representative of all of China, and Taiwan is just a "province" to be conquered. A system that for decades has maintained the fiction that Taiwan does not exist as a state, even though it exists as a fact.

She remembered her country's history. How in 1947, on 28 February, an ordinary scuffle over cigarettes grew into an uprising that the KMT authorities drowned in blood. Tens of thousands killed. A White Terror that lasted almost four decades. 140,000 imprisoned. People disappeared at night, and their families never learned what had happened to them.

And how in 1971 Taiwan was expelled from the UN because its representatives refused "dual representation", which would have recognised them only as an insular part of China. They walked out of the chamber, not knowing that this would be their last step on the international stage. Since then Taiwan has been outside the UN. Only 12 countries recognise it — Haiti, Palau, the Holy See, the Marshall Islands and a handful of others that carry no weight in global politics.

And yet — Taiwan exists. It has its own democracy, its own army, its own passports, its own dreams. And one of the most important companies in the world — TSMC, which produces 90% of the most advanced chips on Earth. Without TSMC there would be no Nvidia, no artificial intelligence, no modern technology. And TSMC is in Taiwan.

Ching Ting took out her phone and opened the photos. She saw her family — her husband, her children, her parents. They all lived in Taipei, in a city that was the capital of a state no one recognised. Her children went to a school where they learned the history of Taiwan — a history that does not exist in Chinese textbooks.

She thought about what she had heard in that room: "Taiwan is not a state." And she understood that for the official in Frankfurt that had been only information from a system — a dry fact with nothing to do with her life, her dreams, her identity. For her — for Chen Ching Ting, a publisher from Taipei — Taiwan was everything. It was home.

The car entered Frankfurt. Past the window flashed tenement houses, shops, people. Everything looked the same as in any other European city. But Ching Ting felt different — as if during those four hours something inside her had cracked. Not anger. Not fear. Something deeper — the awareness that in the eyes of the world her existence was only a problem to be solved.

She got out in front of the hotel. She paid the taxi driver, took her luggage and went inside. At the reception she smiled at the porter and showed her passport. The same passport that four hours earlier had been declared forged.

– Welcome to Germany, Mrs Chen – said the receptionist. – We wish you a pleasant stay.

Ching Ting nodded. She took the key to her room and stepped into the elevator. When the doors closed, she leaned against the wall and closed her eyes.

She knew she would return to Taiwan. That she would keep publishing books, talking to authors, planning the next fairs. That her children would grow up in a country that is a democracy, but not a state. That the whole world would pretend Taiwan did not exist, even though everyone would keep using chips made in Taiwan.

And she knew it was not fair.

But she had no choice. She could only live, work, love and hope that one day — perhaps — the world would stop pretending.

The elevator stopped on the fifth floor. Ching Ting opened her eyes, took a deep breath and stepped into the corridor.

Outside the window of her room she could see the rooftops of Frankfurt. Somewhere far to the east lay Taiwan — an island that was a state, even though no one wanted to say so aloud.

And at the airport in Frankfurt, in the computer system that had not recognised her face, there was still a message:

_Passport invalid. Person unknown. Matter under investigation._

But Ching Ting knew it was not true. She was real. She was Taiwanese. And no system, no official, no One-China policy could change that.

She sat down on the bed. She opened her laptop. Work was waiting — catalogues to review, authors to meet, a fair to prepare. A normal life. A life that went on regardless, even though the world pretended her country did not exist.

She began to write an email to her husband in Taipei. In one sentence she described what had happened. In the second — that everything was fine. In the third — that she missed him.

Her husband replied five minutes later: _Come home quickly. We are with you._

Ching Ting smiled. She closed her laptop.

She knew she would return. And that then, at the airport in Taipei, the automatic gate would let her through. Because there, in Taiwan, no one had to pretend to be someone else.

She could be herself.
