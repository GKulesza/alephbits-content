**MARIA**

Warsaw, the year 1867. In an apartment on Freta Street, in the Skłodowski family, a girl was born who was to change the face of science. No one knew then that this small, dark-haired girl, whom her parents named Maria, would become the first woman to win a Nobel Prize, the first person in history to win two Nobel Prizes in two different fields of science. That her notebooks would be so radioactive that they are still stored in lead-lined boxes today. That her body, after death, would go on emitting radiation for decades.

But all of that would happen later. For now Maria is ten years old, standing over the grave of her mother, who is dying of tuberculosis, and she feels something inside her crack. That was the moment she stopped believing in God. Because if God exists, why does he take a mother? If he is good, why does he allow suffering? A certainty settled into her heart that stayed with her for the rest of her life: if you want to change something, you have to act yourself.

---

**The Flying University**

She was sixteen when she finished high school with a gold medal. Everyone saw a genius in her — a photographic memory, an unstoppable curiosity, an ability to focus that bordered on obsession. But in Warsaw, under Russian rule, women were not allowed to study. The tsar's regulations were clear: science was the domain of men.

Maria refused to be defeated, however. Together with her older sister Bronia she enrolled in the secret "Flying University" — an illegal school for girls whose classes were held at constantly changing locations to escape the watchful eye of the tsarist police. In cramped rooms, by flickering candles, Maria studied organic chemistry, physics, mathematics. Her head was like a sponge — it soaked up knowledge, but she knew this was only a prelude. She needed a diploma. She needed the Sorbonne.

But the journey to Paris was expensive, and the Skłodowski family couldn't afford it. Then Maria and Bronia made a pact: Bronia would go to France first and study medicine. Maria would stay in Poland, work as a governess and send her sister every grosz she earned. Then their roles would be reversed.

The plan was simple. Life — less so.

---

**A governess and an unhappy love**

Maria ended up in the house of the Żurawski family in the provinces, as a teacher to their children. She was poor, lonely and tired. But one day, on holiday, the oldest son of the household arrived — Kazimierz, a handsome, intelligent mathematics student. They fell in love, and the Żurawski family flew into a rage. Maria was too poor for them, too insignificant. Kazimierz, yielding to his parents' pressure, broke off the engagement.

Maria never forgot that betrayal. But instead of breaking down, she poured all her pain into her studies. "They'll be sorry" — she might have thought. And she was right: many years later, when Maria was already a world-famous scientist, Kazimierz used to sit thoughtfully before her monument. But by then it was too late.

---

**Paris**

At last, at the age of 24, Maria boards a train to Paris. She steps off at the station, where her sister is waiting for her. For the first time in her life she feels free. No one asks her for papers, no one checks her Polishness. There is only science.

The Sorbonne is the place she had dreamed of — lecture halls, libraries, laboratories. But life is hard. She rents a small attic with no heating. In winter the water in the basin freezes. Maria lives on bread and tea, often fainting from hunger during lectures. But she doesn't complain. "Physics is more important than food" — she thinks.

Within two years she earns two degrees — one in physics and one in mathematics. She is the first woman in her year to graduate with the highest distinction. Her lecturers are astonished. Someone in the corridor says of her: "That Polish woman has more in her head than all the Frenchmen put together."

---

**Pierre**

In 1894 Maria meets Pierre Curie — a shy, brilliant physicist whose record includes groundbreaking research on magnetism. He is eight years older than her, but that doesn't matter. From the very first meeting — when he shows her his laboratory and she asks questions that knock him off his feet — they know they were made for each other.

Pierre falls head over heels in love with her. When Maria says she plans to return to Poland, Pierre writes her a letter: "How beautiful it would be if we could walk through this life arm in arm, focused on our shared dreams of science." He offers to move to Warsaw, ready to learn Polish.

Maria returns to Poland for the holidays. She tries to find work at the Jagiellonian University. They refuse her — she is a woman. She returns to Paris. In 1895 she marries Pierre. There is no white dress, no rings, no priest. Only a navy blue dress that she will wear in the laboratory for years. As a wedding gift they receive two bicycles — their only luxury.

---

**The heroic time**

Maria is looking for a topic for her doctoral thesis. Everyone around her is fascinated by the X-rays discovered by Röntgen. But Maria chooses something else — the mysterious radiation of uranium salts, discovered by Henri Becquerel. In an old, dilapidated shed that had previously served as a dissecting room, Maria and Pierre begin their research.

They have only primitive equipment — an electrometer built by Pierre and his brother that can detect microscopic currents. Thanks to it, Maria discovers that uranium's radiation is proportional to its quantity — regardless of whether the uranium is pure or in a chemical compound. This means that the source of the radiation is the atom itself.

The breakthrough comes when Maria studies an uranium ore called pitchblende. It turns out to be four times more radioactive than its uranium content would suggest. The conclusion is shocking: the ore must hide another element, far more radioactive. Maria names it Polonium. A few months later they discover radium.

The name "polonium" is a tribute to Poland, which didn't exist on maps at the time. "I wanted the world to know that Poles exist" — Maria would say later.

---

**The first Nobel**

In 1903 Maria and Pierre receive the Nobel Prize in Physics. But at first the Nobel Committee wants to honour only Pierre and Becquerel. Maria is omitted — because she is a woman. Pierre writes to Stockholm: "If Maria doesn't get the Prize, I won't accept it." The Committee gives in.

Maria becomes the first woman to win a Nobel Prize. She uses the money to pay off debts and fund scholarships for Polish female students. And she buys better equipment for the laboratory.

---

**Pierre's death**

April 1906. Paris, rain, mud. Pierre is hurrying to a meeting, thinking about work, crossing the street. He doesn't notice the oncoming wagon. A heavy wheel crushes his skull. Maria receives the news when she gets home. She freezes. In her diary she writes: "My dear Pierre, your head rests in a coffin. I will not see you again."

The Sorbonne offers her her husband's position. She becomes the first female professor in the history of that university. Crowds come to her first lecture — journalists, students, the curious. Maria enters the auditorium, stands before the blackboard and begins the lecture from the point where Pierre had ended his last one.

---

**The scandal**

In 1911 a scandal erupts. The press learns of Maria's romance with Paul Langevin, a physicist and a student of Pierre's. Langevin is married and has four children. The French newspapers call Maria a "destroyer of families", a "Polish schemer". Cries can be heard beneath her windows. People say she should go back to Poland.

That same year Maria receives her second Nobel Prize, this time in chemistry. The Nobel Committee suggests she not come to Stockholm. Maria answers: "The Prize was awarded for my discoveries, not for my private life." She goes and accepts the award.

Albert Einstein writes to her: "I feel the need to tell you how much I admire your intellect, your determination and your honesty. If the rabble continues to rail against you, simply don't read that nonsense."

---

**War and the little Curies**

When World War I breaks out, Maria does not evacuate from Paris. She takes the radium to a bank in Bordeaux, then returns and asks how she can help. She wants to sell her gold Nobel medals to support the army. The bank refuses — the medals are too important.

Instead she creates mobile X-ray units — cars converted into field laboratories. She calls them "little Curies". With her 17-year-old daughter Irène she drives to the front, training women as radiology technicians. Thanks to her work, a million wounded soldiers avoid amputation and death.

The French government never honoured her for this. But the Americans, in 1921, awarded her a gram of radium — a gift from the whole nation.

---

**Death**

Maria's last years are a struggle with illness. Her body is destroyed by radiation. In December 1933, an X-ray shows only a large gallstone — the doctors don't know what is wrong with her. But the truth is cruel: her bone marrow has been destroyed by radium. She suffers from aplastic anaemia — her body stops producing blood.

On 4 July 1934 Maria dies in a sanatorium in eastern France. She is 66. Her body is placed in a lead-lined coffin to protect the surroundings from radiation. Her notebooks are still radioactive. In 1995 her ashes are moved to the Panthéon in Paris — the first woman in history to be honoured there.

---

**Legacy**

Irène, Maria's older daughter, continues her mother's work. Together with her husband Frédéric Joliot-Curie she discovers artificial radioactivity and also wins a Nobel Prize. The younger daughter, Ève, becomes a writer, author of her mother's biography, which to this day is considered one of the best.

Maria Skłodowska-Curie is a symbol of perseverance. She overcame poverty, a system that didn't want women, and her own body, which paid the price for her genius. Her notebooks are still radioactive — and after 1600 years they will still be radiating.

And in Poland, at the Maria Curie-Skłodowska University in Lublin, students still learn from someone who showed that science has no gender. And that you can be brilliant, poor, broken — and still win.
