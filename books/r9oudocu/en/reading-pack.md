## Text

**THE SHOES**

## INTRODUCTION

When he walked into the office, I didn't know yet. But I knew something was wrong – by the way he lowered his eyes when he shook my hand. By the way he sat on the edge of the chair, not leaning back. By the way he said "good morning" too quietly.

I had seven hours at the hospital behind me and three patients who didn't want to listen.

– Mr Tadeusz, right? – I asked, opening his records.

– Yes.

– Why did you come to me?

He shrugged.

– My wife told me to come.

I looked at his shoes. Leather, dark, lace-ups. They looked new. Then I saw him sliding his feet across the floor – as if checking whether they were still there.

– New shoes? – I asked.

– Something like that.

– Please take them off.

He hesitated. For a moment I thought he would get up and leave. But he stayed. He untied the laces. Slowly, as if doing something he hadn't done in a long time.

When he took off the left shoe, I saw a stain on the sock. Dark, dried.

– When did this start?

– I don't know. It hurt a little. But I thought it was from the shoes. New shoes, you know. For my son's wedding.

I took his foot in my hands. The skin around the toes was taut, shiny, reddish-purple. A sweet, rotten smell rose from the wound.

– Mr Tadeusz – I said. – When did you last have your sugar checked?

– Well... some time ago. But it didn't hurt, doctor.

_"It didn't hurt."_ I heard that every day.

– Please take off your sock.

He did. The sock stuck to the wound – he peeled it off with a quiet, wet sound. Underneath was a toe. Black. Dead.

– Mr Tadeusz – I said. – This is dead. If we don't do something fast, the rest of the leg will die too.

He looked at his foot as if seeing it for the first time. Then at me. In his eyes appeared surprise.

– But it doesn't hurt – he said.

---
## DEVELOPMENT

### Situation one: The decision

I called the surgeon. We scheduled a date for the next day.

– That long can't be waited – said the surgeon.

– It can't. But he needs time to understand.

I told him. He sat in the same place, on the edge of the chair, with a dead toe.

– Tomorrow – he said. – You say tomorrow?

– Yes.

– And if I had come earlier? If I had listened to my wife?

– Maybe we could have saved it.

– And now not?

– Now it's too late.

He sat in silence. He picked up the shoe – the new, leather one. For a moment I thought he would start to cry. But he only put the shoe back on, tied the laces, stood up and shook my hand.

– Thank you, doctor. For the truth.

He left, leaving behind him the smell of the wound and of fresh leather.

The next morning the surgeon called.

– He didn't come. His wife says he left. That he said he had something to take care of.

– Where did he go?

– I don't know. Tomorrow will be too late.

I closed my eyes. I imagined Tadeusz – in the new shoes, somewhere on the road, with a dead toe that didn't hurt. With the thought that it would pass.

---
### Situation two: The return

A week passed. Then two. The surgeon didn't call. I thought about Tadeusz at night, when I couldn't sleep.

And then, one evening, someone knocked on the door of my office.

He was there. He stood in the doorway. Thin, pale, with crutches under his arms. On one leg. On the other – an empty trouser leg.

– Doctor – he said. – I came back.

He sat on the same chair. This time he leaned back.

– What happened? – I asked.

– I went to my son's wedding. I was supposed to be in the new shoes. I sat on a chair and watched my son dance with his wife. And then I understood that this was no longer my life. That I had given it away. Piece by piece.

He took a shoe out of his pocket – the new, leather one. He put it on the desk.

– I'd like you to take it – he said. – To remember all those who don't come until it's too late.

I took the shoe in my hand. Inside, on the lining, I felt a stain – dried, dark. Blood.

– Do you know what's the worst? – he asked. – That if I had taken off those shoes... I wouldn't have had to. And I didn't take them off. Because I thought it would pass.

He left, leaving me with the shoe on my desk.

---
### Situation three: The shoe on the desk

Months passed. The shoe lay in the drawer. I didn't look in there, but I knew it was there.

More patients came. A man with an infection that was swallowing his whole foot – he had waited a week because he was "building a house for his daughter". A woman with sugar of 320 who "didn't want to be a bother" – she came because she had stopped seeing out of her left eye. A patient who had spent a month thinking "it's just a callus", and then lost two toes.

Every day the same. They came, sat on the same chair, looked at their feet as if seeing them for the first time. And always the same question:

– Could it have ended differently?

– Yes. If you had come earlier.

I didn't say it to punish them. I said it because they had to hear it.

---
Then a young man came. Maybe 24. Pale, with sunken cheeks. Tadeusz had sent him.

– He gave me a shoe – he said. – He said he'd lost a leg because he wouldn't take off his shoes. And that I should come to you.

– Why did you come?

– Because I'm afraid it will be the same. That I'll lose a leg. Or die.

– What's wrong with you?

– Diabetes. Type two. I've known for a year. I thought it would pass. And now I'm afraid it's already too late.

– When did you last have your sugar checked?

– Yesterday. 320.

– Glycated haemoglobin?

– 11.5.

I closed my eyes. 11.5. That's a lot. That's a very lot.

– Mr Krzysztof. It's not too late. Not yet.

– What should I do?

– Change your lifestyle. And start taking medication.

– I've tried. A thousand times. It never worked out.

– Now it's different. Because you have Tadeusz's shoe. And you know what you can lose.

He sat in silence. And then I saw something in him break.

– I don't want to die. I want to live. Really live.

I got up. I walked over to the cupboard. I took out an old, faded photograph.

– Who is that? – he asked.

– My father.

– What happened to him?

– The same as to you. Diabetes. He wouldn't treat it. He said he had time. And he ran out of time. A heart attack. He was 54.

Krzysztof took the photograph in his hand.

– You can take it – I said. – To remember that everyone has a choice.

He put the photograph in his pocket.

– Thank you, doctor.

He left. He left the door open.

---
## ENDING

The next week a woman came who had lost a leg. She walked on her own, with a prosthesis.

– Do you know what's strangest? – she asked. – That if I hadn't lost my leg, I would never have changed. I would have stayed the same bitter person. And now I feel like I'm really living.

– That's not a typical reaction.

– I know. I came to say thank you. For telling me the truth.

She picked up Tadeusz's shoe.

– Maybe you should give it to someone who doesn't yet know they have a choice.

She put the shoe back and left.

---
The next morning Tadeusz came. This time in a wheelchair.

– I want my shoe back.

– What for?

– I want to give it to the neighbour's son. He's 24. He has diabetes. He won't treat it. He says he has time. And I want to give him this shoe. So he knows what he can lose.

– Do you think it will help?

– I don't know. But I have to try.

He took the shoe in his hand, put it in his bag.

– Keep telling the truth – he said. – Even if they don't want to listen. Because maybe someone will listen.

He wheeled back toward the door.

– And remember: everyone has a choice. Even when it seems they don't.

He left.

---
## EPILOGUE

Years passed. Tadeusz's shoe kept travelling on. After a year, Krzysztof came back to me with his results: glycated haemoglobin 6.2. He had lost 20 kilos.

– Thank you, doctor.

– For what?

– For that photograph. For your father. For the choice.

I smiled.

– It was your decision.

– But without you, I wouldn't have known about it.

---
In the drawer there was still a shoe. Not the same one – that one had travelled on. But I found another. Leather, dark, with the imprint of a foot. Someone had left it, maybe forgotten it, maybe not wanted to take it.

Now it stands on my desk. In plain sight. Every patient sees it.

They no longer ask what that shoe is. They know. And most of them, before they leave, look at their feet. And think.

And I look at them and think about all the shoes still waiting in cupboards. About all the legs that can still be saved. About all the choices that can still be made.

This isn't my story. It's their story. I only listen to it. And I remind them that they have a choice.

Even when it seems they don't.

**THE END**
