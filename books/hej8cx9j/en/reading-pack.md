## Text

**THE CLOAKROOM ATTENDANT'S DILEMMA**


## I. Brussels, two years ago

When he walked into the chairwoman's office, he could smell freshly brewed coffee and a perfume that cost more than the salary of an average Pole. It wasn't his first time here. Over the years he had learned that in this part of Europe what matters is not what you say, but how you say it — and to whom.

— Donald — said the chairwoman, without raising her eyes from the documents. — Sit down.

He sat down. He knew it would be short. She didn't waste time on pleasantries.

— The situation in Poland is getting complicated — she began. — Your ratings are falling. They see it in Brussels too.

— I know — he answered.

— Do you need something from me? A position? Something that will protect you?

He hesitated. For a moment he thought about what he had left in Poland. A party teetering on the edge. People who had believed him when he said it would be different. And about the fact that he himself no longer knew what he believed in.

— Not for now — he said. — But it may turn out that I'll need... an emergency exit.

The chairwoman looked at him for the first time.

— There is always an emergency exit. The question is whether you'll want to use it.

He didn't answer. He stood up, nodded and left.

Behind the door his advisor was waiting.

— So? — he asked.

— As always — he replied. — Brussels is like a mother. It gives you a sense of security, but only when you are obedient to it.

---

## II. Warsaw, a year later

He sat in his office at the Chancellery of the Prime Minister, watching the television screen. The news was showing the scandal at the Southern Hospital. Doctors on contracts, fake shifts, patients outside the queue. And a name that kept appearing in the context of the Warsaw party structures.

He knew this was only the beginning.

He reached for his phone and dialled the number he knew by heart.

— Hello — said a voice on the other end.

— Did you know about this? — he asked.

— I knew something was going on there. I didn't know it was this bad.

— And now what?

— Do what you always do. Explain that you knew nothing. That it's the fault of the local structures. That you had nothing to do with it.

— And if they don't believe me?

— Then you'll have to play your Brussels card.

He was silent for a moment. He knew that card was his last resort. But using it meant admitting defeat.

— Give me a little more time — he said at last.

— You don't have that time, Donald. It's starting to heat up.

He hung up and looked at the photo on the desk. The smiling face of his wife, his children. A normal life, which he hadn't had for a long time. He stood up, walked to the window and looked at Warsaw. A city he loved, and at the same time a city that didn't want him. And then he understood that there was no turning back.

---

## III. Sejm corridor, the present

Anna Kulesza had been a journalist for fifteen years. She had seen everything – scandals, controversies, resignations. But what had been happening in recent weeks was different.

She stood in the Sejm corridor, waiting for the MP who had promised her an interview. Around her politicians, assistants and journalists milled about. Someone was talking on the phone about a bill, someone else about polls, and yet another about the fact that "Tusk already knows he won't hold on to power".

She had heard that sentence several times in recent days. It was said in the corridors, on talk shows, in conversations over coffee. It had become a mantra that, repeated often enough, began to sound like the truth.

— Ms Anna? — a young assistant came up to her. — The MP has five minutes for you.

She entered the office, where a portrait of Marshal Piłsudski hung on the wall. The MP was an older man, with grey hair and eyes that had seen more than one term of office.

— What brings you here? — he asked.

— I wanted to ask about the contingency plan. About what's being said in the corridors. That the prime minister is preparing a place for himself in Brussels.

The MP smiled.

— Rumours, madam editor. Brussels is not as eager as it used to be. Besides – he looked her straight in the eye – even if it were true, what would it change? People will vote for him anyway. They're afraid of the alternative.

— And if the alternative is better?

— The alternative is always worse. That's what his voters think. And that's why those who understand this will win.

Anna wrote it down in her notebook. Then she looked at the MP.

— And you? What do you believe in?

— I believe that politics is not a game about truth. It's a game about power. And power, madam editor, is like a drug. Once you try it, you don't want to stop.

She left the office with mixed feelings. She knew she would write an article about this, but she didn't yet know what its message would be.

---

## IV. Wrocław, a flat on a housing estate

Tomek was thirty-five years old, had two children and a mortgage that swallowed half his salary. He worked at a corporation as a data analyst. Every day he got up at six, came home after six in the evening, and at weekends he took care of the house and the children.

He wasn't interested in politics. Or rather – he was interested, but didn't believe anything could change. He always voted for the same option, because that's what his parents had done. But in recent months he had begun to have doubts.

He sat in the living room, scrolling through his phone. A headline appeared on the screen: _"Tusk is preparing a contingency plan – an escape to Brussels"_.

He read the article, then another, then one more. The more he read, the more lost he felt. On one hand he heard that the prime minister was betraying Poland, fleeing responsibility. On the other – that it was just political attacks, that there was no evidence.

– What are you thinking about? – asked his wife, coming into the living room.

– About the fact that I no longer know whom to believe – he answered.

– And do you have to believe someone?

– I don't know. Maybe not. Maybe it's enough that I believe in myself.

His wife smiled.

– That's already something.

Tomek took her hand and looked out the window. Outside it was raining. And then he thought that maybe that was exactly the point – to stop waiting for someone else to change his life. And to start changing it himself.

---

## V. Brussels, the present

He sat in the same office he had been in two years earlier. In front of him lay the documents he was supposed to sign. On the desk stood coffee, this time cold.

The chairwoman watched him carefully.

– Donald, it's time for a decision – she said. – Your party is falling apart. Scandals, falling polls, internal conflicts. You won't win the next elections.

– I know.

– You have to make a decision. Do you stay and fight to the end? Or do you take up the offer I made you?

He looked at the documents. On the first page was the heading: _"Advisor position for Eastern policy"_.

– This isn't what I wanted – he said quietly.

– Nobody gets what they want. You get what you need. And you need immunity.

He was silent for a long moment. Then he picked up the pen and signed the document.

– When does the next plane to Warsaw leave? – he asked.

– In three hours.

– Book me a seat. I still have something to take care of.

He stood up, nodded and left. He knew these were the last hours in which he was still someone. In three hours he would board a plane that would take him back. And then everything would end.

Or begin anew.

---

## VI. Warsaw, the next day

Anna Kulesza was writing an article. In front of her lay notes, interviews, quotes. But she knew the most important sentence had not yet been written.

She looked out the window at the city. Warsaw was teeming with life, people going to work, cars stuck in traffic. Everyone was living their own lives, unaware that somewhere out there, in the Sejm corridors, the fate of the country was being decided.

She began to write:

_"Donald Tusk has a contingency plan. That's no secret. But the question everyone is asking is: will he use it? Will he really flee to Brussels, leaving the party at the mercy of fate? Or is all this just a game meant to divert attention from the real problems?"_

She stopped. She knew this wasn't an article that would change the world. But maybe it would change the way people look at politics.

Because politics is not a game. It's responsibility.

And you can't run away from responsibility.

---

## VII. Epilogue

Tomek came home from work late in the evening. The children were already asleep, his wife was reading a book in the living room. He sat down beside her and was silent for a moment.

– You know – he said at last – today I was thinking that everyone keeps talking about politicians. About what they do, what they don't do, where they flee. And nobody talks about us.

– About whom?

– About ordinary people. About those who get up in the morning and go to work to support their families. About those who have no time for political games, because they have to pay the bills.

His wife put down the book.

– And what are you going to do about it?

– Nothing. Or everything. Maybe I'll start with myself. Maybe that will be enough.

He stood up and walked to the window. Outside, streetlamps were shining, and the sky was studded with stars.

– You know what's the worst thing about politics? – he asked.

– What?

– That we forget that we are the most important ones. Not them.

And then he understood that he didn't need to know whether the prime minister would flee to Brussels. He didn't need to know who would win the next elections. He needed only one thing: to know that he and his family were safe. And that he had the strength to fight for that.

And the rest – the rest is only theatre.

---

**THE END**
