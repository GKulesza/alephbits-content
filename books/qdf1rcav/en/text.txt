**The Hidden Depth**

Doctor Magdalena Cubała put down the phone and looked at the appointment calendar. In twenty minutes she was to see a new patient – a man in his forties, referred by Professor Czupryniak from Banach Street. "Mr Darek, this isn't diabetes. Go to Dr Cubała, she'll find the hidden depth" – she read in the professor's note and smiled to herself. She'd known him for years, from the time she herself had worked in the system, before she'd escaped to functional medicine. Czupryniak was one of the few academic doctors who didn't look at her like a quack when she talked about the gut-brain axis or the cortisol curve. "You look for causes, and I treat symptoms" – he'd once told her over coffee. "That's why I send you the ones my pills aren't enough for."

Darek arrived on time. He was a tall, stooped man with greying temples and deep shadows under his eyes that betrayed more than tiredness – they betrayed resignation. In his hand he clutched a folder of test results, thicker than the standard records of a diabetic patient. The professor must have given him a full work-up.

– Mr Darek – she began, once he was seated across from her. – Before we get to the results, please tell me your story. Not the medical one. The life one.

The man looked at her distrustfully. Then he shrugged.

– I got divorced. Six months ago. My wife... my ex-wife. She couldn't take it anymore. She said I'd changed. That I wasn't the same man I used to be. That I only work, and when I come home I'm like a zombie. – He swallowed. – She was right. Only back then I didn't know why. I thought it was laziness, burnout, maybe depression. I went from doctor to doctor, took antidepressants, nothing helped. Fog in my head, zero energy. I woke up in the morning exhausted, as if I hadn't slept at all. I drank coffee after coffee. In the evening my head was pounding. And here... – he pointed at his stomach – constantly bloated, gas, sometimes constipation, sometimes diarrhoea. I thought it was the stress at work. That it would pass on its own.

Magdalena listened without interrupting. When he finished, she opened the folder and spread the results on the desk.

– Professor Czupryniak ruled out diabetes, insulin resistance, thyroid disease, anaemia. All your basic tests are normal. Almost all. – She pointed at one of them. – He did a salivary cortisol curve for you. Do you know what that is?

– The professor said it's a test of stress levels. But I don't understand... Stress? Everyone has stress. You can't lose sleep and thinking and wreck your gut over that.

– You can. – She slid the printout with the chart toward him. – Take a look. In a healthy person, cortisol is highest in the morning – it's what helps us get up, feel energy. Then it slowly drops, to reach a minimum in the evening and let us fall asleep. In your case it's the opposite. In the morning your cortisol barely breathes, which is why you wake up tired. But in the evening, around five or six, it shoots up. That's when you get the headaches, that inability to relax. And at night, when you should be sleeping, cortisol rises again. That's why you toss and turn and can't fall asleep, even though you're exhausted.

Darek stared at the chart as if seeing it for the first time. Maybe he had seen it – the professor had probably shown him the same thing, but back then it hadn't sunk in.

– This is called HPA axis dysregulation – Magdalena continued. – The hypothalamus, the pituitary, the adrenal glands. When we're stressed — not for a week, not for a month, but for years — this system breaks down. Like an engine that keeps running at full revs. Eventually it seizes up. And then cortisol stops obeying the daily rhythm. There's too little of it when you need to get up. Too much when you need to sleep. And that's only the beginning.

She turned the page.

– You also had a microbiome test. And here is the second part of the answer. You have intestinal dysbiosis. To put it simply: an abnormal bacterial flora. Too many bacteria that produce toxins, too few of those that protect the gut. Under chronic stress, the permeability of the gut increases – meaning that what should stay in the intestines leaks into the blood. Toxins, undigested remains, fermentation products. And they go straight to the brain. Do you know what auto-brewery syndrome is?

– No.

– It's a condition in which candida fungi produce alcohol in the gut. Literally. A person is as if permanently slightly intoxicated. Brain fog, fatigue, concentration problems — it all fits. I don't know if you have that particular one, but the dysbiosis you have gives very similar symptoms. Add lactic acid produced by abnormal bacteria — that also hits the brain.

Darek was silent. His hands, clasped on his knees, had gone white from clenching.

– So... it's all in the gut? – he asked at last.

– In the gut and in the head. Or rather, between them. The gut-brain axis is a two-way highway. The vagus nerve connects the brain to the gut and carries signals in both directions. When you're stressed, the brain sends a signal to the gut: "Attention, danger!". The gut contracts, its permeability changes, the microbiome changes. And when the gut is sick, it sends signals to the brain: "Things are bad." And the brain gets stressed again. And so on in circles. A vicious circle.

– So that's why Marta...

He stopped. Magdalena didn't rush him. She knew that what was about to come was the most important sentence of this appointment.

– Marta, my wife, said I'd changed. That nothing makes me happy. That I'm cynical, indifferent. That we don't even argue, we just pass each other by. And I... I didn't have the strength to argue. I didn't have the strength for anything. I thought she was exaggerating. That if she'd just ease off a bit, it would be better. And now I see... – He raised his eyes to Magdalena. – I'm the one who broke. Literally.

Magdalena put down the results and looked him straight in the eyes.

– Mr Darek. This is not your fault. No one told you that the body has its limits. That stress isn't something you can ignore, because it writes itself into the body. Literally. Cortisol tells the thyroid: "We're slowing down the metabolism, because there's a threat." It tells the sex hormones: "We won't reproduce, because it's not the time." It tells the immune system: "We're turning off the defence, because we have to run from a tiger." Only you weren't running from a tiger. You were sitting in front of a computer, scrolling your phone, watching series until midnight, and every day hearing from your boss that the targets hadn't been met. Your body thought it was in a state of permanent siege. And besieged fortresses eventually fall.

Darek wiped his eyes with the back of his hand. He wasn't crying – he couldn't. But something in his face had cracked, as if for the first time in years the truth had reached him.

– What should I do? – he asked.

– Start with simple things. And there's no magic in any of this. First, rhythm. Regular meal times. Four a day, no snacks in between. Your gut needs a cleaning crew – it's called the migrating motor complex. When you snack, the crew has no chance to clean up the leftovers. And it becomes a mess.

– Second, eating without screens. No laptop, no phone. The first phase of digestion is in the head – when you see food, smell it, the brain prepares the digestive system for work. If you're scrolling your emails at that time, the brain doesn't know you're eating. Digestion goes haywire.

– Third, walking. Every day. At least half an hour. You don't have to run, you don't have to buy a gym pass. Do you have a dog?

– No.

– Then you'll walk a virtual dog. Imagine you have a labrador that needs to go out three times a day. Rain, snow, wind — it doesn't matter. A dog doesn't wait for nice weather.

Darek almost smiled. Almost.

– Fourth, sleep hygiene. An hour before bed, no screens. Dim light. You can listen to chillout, you can read a book — a paper one. The blue light from phones and laptops disrupts melatonin. And yours is already disrupted.

– And fifth, the hardest one. Emotions. They're biological too. When you hold on to resentment, when you can't let go of anger, your body is constantly producing cortisol. It's constantly in a state of war. Do you know that story? A woman calls her friend and complains that someone has wronged her. And the friend asks: "Why do you need those emotions? Who's hurting you now — those people or yourself? Because they forgot about you long ago, and with your anger and despair you're only hurting yourself. And the ones close to you."

Darek flinched. That sentence hit him harder than all the medical explanations.

– I... I think that's exactly what I was doing. For the last few years. I was hurting myself. And Marta. Because I couldn't let go. Of work, of pressure, of expectations. Everything.

– Letting go doesn't mean forgetting – said Magdalena quietly. – It means stopping feeding an emotion that's destroying you from the inside. The method is called the Sedona Method, you can read about it. But to begin with, one exercise is enough. When you feel the old anger, the old grievance coming back, ask yourself: "Am I willing to let this emotion go?". You don't have to know how right away. It's enough that you try. That you say: "Yes, I'm willing." And then do something completely different from what you usually do. Go for a walk. Breathe. Look at the tree outside the window.

– And medication? – asked Darek. – Will you give me something for all this?

– I will. Adaptogens to calm you down, probiotics for the gut, magnesium, because under stress you lose it like crazy. But these aren't medicines that will cure you. They're supports. You have to build the foundation yourself. Lifestyle. Everyday life. Without that, no supplement will help.

Darek took the prescription and stood by the door for a moment, as if he wanted to say something else. At last he turned around.

– Do you think I could... could I still undo it? Not the illness. The rest.

Magdalena looked at him with something that wasn't pity, but respect — the kind you have for patients who finally ask the right question.

– Mr Darek. Your body has been screaming for a long time. First with brain fog, then with your gut, then with fatigue. You didn't listen. And now it's speaking louder. But that doesn't mean it's too late. It means you have to start listening. And as for the rest... – She hesitated. – I'm a doctor, not a couples therapist. But I'll tell you one thing. The body remembers everything we do to it. If you repair it, if you regain your energy, clarity of thought, balance — you'll be a different man. And perhaps you'll be ready for a new relationship. With the same person or with someone else. But first you have to get yourself back. Because how can you give someone something you don't have yourself?

Darek left. Magdalena followed him with her eyes to the door, then sat down at her desk and noted a few things in the patient's chart. Then she went to the window. Outside it was raining, but in the distance, above the roofs of the blocks, the sky was clearing. She thought about her uncle, a surgeon who had died of a heart attack after thirty years of work in the national health service. About the young man who had climbed the corporate ladder and had a stroke at thirty-eight. About herself a few years earlier, when she couldn't get out of bed because of back pain and thought her life was over. Back then, in that pain, she too had had to learn gratitude — for the birds outside the window, for the cat sleeping on the pillow, for the glass of warm water in the morning, the only thing that didn't cause her stomach to cramp.

Health is worth more than millions — she thought. And she smiled to herself, because in an hour she had another patient. Another story. Another chance to find the hidden depth.
