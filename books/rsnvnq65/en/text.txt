**A TUNED HOME**

When Ewa came back from the workshop, her flat looked exactly the same as before she left. Which meant: terrible.

She stood in the hallway with a suitcase in her hand and looked at the pile of shoes that had spilled across the floor like lava. On the coat rack hung fourteen coats. Fourteen. She lived here alone.

– Well, here we are – she said quietly to herself. – Again.

For a week at the meditation centre she had felt light. She had plans, resolutions, a vision of herself as an organised person. She intended to come back and finally tame this chaos. And now she stood here, staring at the pile of shoes, and felt the energy draining out of her drop by drop.

She took a deep breath. Then another.

– Just one step – she said. – Just one.

But she didn't take any. Instead she went to the kitchen, put the kettle on and sat down at the table, on which papers from three months ago were piled up.

And then the phone rang.

– Ewa, I'm in your neighbourhood. Can I pop by for coffee? – Asia's voice, her former flatmate, sounded cheerful and unaware.

– No – she answered too quickly. – I mean... it's a mess.

– All the more reason. Be right there.

Asia arrived twenty minutes later. She stopped in the hallway, looked around and said:

– Oh wow.

– I know – Ewa pursed her lips. – I know, I know, I know. But I just came back from that workshop and...

– And?

– And I thought that when I got back I'd sort it out. But... I can't. I don't know where to start.

Asia took off her jacket and put it on the only free chair in the living room.

– Listen. I know someone. A woman who does this for a living. She helps people... well, exactly – she made a gesture that took in the whole flat.

– You think she'll help me?

– I don't know. But you can try. It can't get any worse.

---

Three days later Marta stood in Ewa's doorway. She had short hair, calm eyes and a smile that made Ewa relax a little right away.

– Ms Marta, thank you for coming – Ewa stepped aside to make room. – Sorry about the mess.

Marta came in. She looked around carefully, but Ewa sensed no judgment in her gaze.

– And what is mess? – asked Marta, sitting down on the sofa. – For you.

Ewa sat down opposite.

– It's... I don't know. Everything I can't manage to tidy up. Those things that lie around and gather dust. And remind me that I'm incompetent.

– So you're saying the mess is proof of your incompetence?

– Well... yes.

Marta smiled.

– And what if I told you that the mess isn't proof of your incompetence, but proof that something in your life needs attention? That it's a signal, not a verdict?

Ewa looked at her doubtfully.

– A signal of what?

– Of what's inside you. What you have at home is a reflection of what you have inside. And if you look at it, you can learn a lot about yourself.

Marta pointed at the pile of shoes in the hallway.

– Tell me – she asked. – When did you last wear those shoes?

Ewa looked at the pile. There were trainers, ankle boots, sandals, ballet flats. Some remembered her student days.

– Most of them... I haven't worn in years.

– Why do you keep them?

– Because... well, they might come in handy. Just in case.

– In case of what?

Ewa hesitated.

– I don't know. Maybe I'll need them someday. Maybe someday...

– Maybe someday you'll go back to being who you were? – Marta interrupted her gently. – To that younger version of yourself who wore those shoes?

Ewa felt something tighten in her throat.

– I don't know. Maybe.

– And who are you now?

– I don't know – she confessed quietly. – I thought I knew. But lately... less and less.

Marta nodded, as if that was exactly what she had expected.

– So maybe that's where we'll start. From finding the answer to that question: who are you now. And what you need to live in harmony with that person.

---

The next day they started with the wardrobe. Marta told her to pull everything out into the middle of the room.

Ewa looked at the mountain of clothes growing on the floor. There were things from the time she had worked at the corporation – elegant suits that had hung in protective plastic for five years. There were things from the time she had taken up running – tracksuits that were already too tight. There were things she had bought on impulse, at sales, because they were cheap. There were things her mother had given her that had never matched her style.

And there were things she had worn when she met Marcin.

– This dress – Ewa picked up a red floral dress. – I wore it on our first date.

Marta came closer.

– And what do you feel, holding it in your hands?

– Strange. As if... as if I had to go back to that day. I was younger then, stupider, happier.

– Was it a happy date?

– Yes. I thought it was the beginning of something big.

– And what happened?

Ewa lowered the dress onto her knees.

– It ended after three months. He... wasn't ready. He said it wasn't me, it was him. That he had his own problems. And that was it.

– And you've kept this dress ever since?

– Yes. For five years.

Marta sat down beside her on the floor.

– Does that mean you're still waiting for him to come back? Or maybe you keep it to remind yourself that someone once wanted you? That you were once able to charm someone?

Ewa looked at the dress. Floral, red, so cheerful. Completely out of place in her current life.

– I don't know. Maybe both.

– And maybe – Marta spoke gently – this dress is a symbol of your belief that you'll never have a moment like that again? That the best is behind you?

Ewa felt her eyes grow moist.

– And what if it is?

– If it is, then what would you do if you believed it wasn't? If you believed the best was still ahead of you?

Ewa was silent.

– I don't know.

– Well, then maybe let's start there – Marta stood up and held out her hand. – Maybe just let it go. Let go of the dress, let go of Marcin, let go of the thought that nothing is waiting for you anymore. Maybe make room for something new.

Ewa stared at the red dress for a long moment. Then, very slowly, she put it into a bag marked "to give away".

---

In the following days something strange began to happen. Ewa was getting rid of clothes, but with every bag she carried out of the flat she felt something changing inside her. She didn't yet know what, but she felt it was good.

One evening, when Marta had already left, Ewa sat down on the floor among the things spread out. She picked up an old, crumpled sheet of paper from a notebook. There was a drawing on it — her own, from her high school days. Back then she had drawn a house. Big, with a garden, with windows in which light was burning.

– You wanted a house like that – she said to herself.

And then something came to her mind that she didn't fully understand. Maybe that house she had drawn as a teenager wasn't just a house. Maybe it was a place where she could be herself. A place where she didn't have to pretend. A place she could return to after a hard day and feel safe.

And her flat wasn't such a place. It was a warehouse, a storeroom, a storage space for a past that didn't want to leave.

The next day she called Marta.

– I think I know what this is all about – she said.

– Tell me.

– I'd like to create a home where I really want to live. Not for someone, not to show off. For myself.

Marta smiled over the phone.

– That's the best thing you could have said. So let's do it.

---

Over the following weeks Ewa reworked her flat room by room. Every object was put on trial: "Does this serve me? Is this who I am now? Does this help me be the person I want to be?"

Many things failed the test. They ended up in bags, in boxes, in clothing containers. But some stayed. And those that stayed suddenly gained new meaning.

When she finished, she sat in the middle of the living room and looked around. The walls were brighter, the space was open. On the shelves stood only the books she really liked. On the walls hung the photographs she really wanted to look at.

– This is your home – said Marta, standing in the doorway. – And this is you. And now the question: what next?

Ewa smiled.

– I feel strange. As if I were... breathing for the first time in years.

– That's what a space that suits you does. It gives you breath. And now that you have breath, what do you want to do with it?

Ewa looked at her hands.

– I think I want to... start living. Really. Not waiting. Not putting it off. Just living.

Marta nodded.

– That's the best thing you could have said.

And then Ewa did something she hadn't done in years. She stood up, walked to the window, opened it wide and breathed in deeply. The wind carried the smell of spring, of freshness, and of something that could have been hope.

– Marta? – she said, without turning.

– Yes?

– Thank you. Really.

– Don't thank me. Thank yourself. You did all the work. I only showed you where to look.

---

Months passed. Ewa didn't become a minimalist. She had things she loved. She had memories she returned to with a smile. But they were no longer a burden.

One day, while going through an old box, she came across the drawing from high school. The same house she had drawn twenty years earlier. She smiled.

– It's not about the house – she said to herself. – It's about moving into it.

And then she understood that that was exactly what she had done. She had moved back into her own life.

**THE END**
