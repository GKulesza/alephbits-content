## Text

**GOLDEN HAIR AND THE SKY**

### The story of a pilot who wrote the world's tenderest fairy tale

## PROLOGUE

_Sahara, 1935_

The sand was everywhere. In his eyes, in his mouth, in every corner of a body that had stopped feeling thirst. Antoine de Saint-Exupéry lay on his back and looked at the sky. Above him stretched infinity – the same infinity in which he had spent so many hours, the same one that was now slowly killing him.

He had crashed four days ago. His plane, the Simoun, had struck a stony plateau at full speed. The roar of metal, a fountain of sand, silence. He and his mechanic, André Prévot, had survived. But the water ran out the next day. Now they had only two oranges, a few grapes, a small cake, half a litre of coffee in a broken thermos and a quarter of a litre of white wine.

The Sahara had fifty degrees by day. By night – zero.

Antoine closed his eyes. Images began to appear in his mind that he could not distinguish from reality. The castle in Saint-Maurice-de-Rémens, where as a child he ran through the attic with his younger brother François. His mother singing downstairs, her voice reaching him as a muffled echo. A green, lacquered throne, which he carried everywhere with him, because the household called him the Sun King.

And then the image that returned most often – a winter morning in 1917, a boarding school in Fribourg, the bed by which his fifteen-year-old brother was dying.

_"I wanted to talk to you before I died. I'm dying now."_

Antoine opened his eyes. The sand was still everywhere. But in the distance, on the horizon, a silhouette appeared. A Bedouin on a camel.

He did not know then that this meeting would save his life. And he did not know that a few years later, in New York, in the darkest period of his life, he would write a book about a pilot who crashes in the desert and meets a little boy with golden hair.

A boy who never grew up.

Just like his brother.

---

## CHAPTER I: THE SUN KING

_Saint-Maurice-de-Rémens, 1907_

The house where Antoine de Saint-Exupéry grew up was not wealthy. It was aristocratic, but without fortune. His father, Viscount Jean de Saint-Exupéry, died when Antoine was only four years old. A stroke took him in an instant, leaving a widow with five children and no means of support.

Marie de Saint-Exupéry, Antoine's mother, was a woman of extraordinary strength. A painter, a sensitive artist, she could turn any house into a warm place full of stories. She read Andersen and Verne to the children, taught them to draw, to love nature and animals. Her calm and devotion were the foundation on which Antoine built his childhood.

But the real paradise was the castle in Saint-Maurice-de-Rémens. A vast park surrounded it, densely overgrown with dark spruces and lindens. There was an attic swathed in secrets, full of costumes, hats and riding boots belonging to generations of great-grandfathers. Old clocks rasped hoarsely when touched. The children spent whole days there, playing knights, explorers and pirates.

And in the evenings, tucked into bed in the upper rooms, they listened to their mother singing downstairs. The song reached them as an echo, muffled and warm, as if coming from another, gentler world.

Antoine was a child of irrepressible vitality and a vivid imagination. He was constantly building something, inventing engines, designing machines. Once he had an old governess sew a sail out of a sheet, attached it to his bicycle and sped off across the park hoping the bicycle would lift off. It didn't lift, but the idea itself was telling.

The household gave him the nickname Sun King. Golden-haired, curly, of extraordinary beauty and temperament, he went everywhere with a green lacquered little chair – his throne. He would set it wherever his mother happened to stop and sit on it with the air of a monarch.

His only male companion was his brother François, two years younger. Fair-haired, delicate, calmer than Antoine, he was his shadow and ally. Together they explored the attic, together they fled from storms through the park, together they invented the rules of games. With all the constant moves, changing schools and friends, it was his brother who remained the one constant in Antoine's life.

Marie de Saint-Exupéry used to say that in that castle Antoine was stocking up on happiness. And indeed, observing his childhood, it is hard not to get the impression that the boy was gathering memories like a priceless material he would one day use.

He did not yet know that this material would serve him to write a book that would outlast the centuries.

---

## CHAPTER II: THE FIRST FLIGHT

_Ambérieu, 1912_

Antoine was twelve years old when he first rose into the air.

At the airfield in Ambérieu, a few kilometres from the family castle, stood a monoplane, and beside it the Polish pilot Gabriel Wróblewski, preparing for another takeoff. Antoine walked up to him and said that his mother had allowed him to fly. It was a lie, the first serious lie of his life, and the boy knew he was committing it.

Wróblewski shrugged, helped him climb into the cockpit and started the engine.

The ground fell away below. The houses shrank, the trees merged into a green stain, and Antoine felt something he could not yet name. It was not fear, it was not joy. It was the conviction that he had just found the place where he wanted to spend the rest of his life.

That twelve-year-old boy did not yet know that the sky would become both his calling and his curse. That he would crash so many times that his body would carry the marks of disasters to the very end. That on his final flight, over the Mediterranean, he would vanish without a trace, as if the sky had finally taken him forever.

But then, in 1912, he was only a boy who had felt, for the first time, what freedom is.

---

## CHAPTER III: THE DEATH OF A BROTHER

_Fribourg, 1917_

It was four in the morning when a nurse woke Antoine. She told him to come quickly. He dressed in a hurry, without turning on a light, and ran down the corridor to his brother's room.

François lay in bed, very pale, but calm. When Antoine sat down beside him, the boy spoke in an ordinary voice, as if it were the most everyday matter in the world:

_"I wanted to talk to you before I died. I'm dying now."_

Antoine froze. A fever attack shook François's body, forcing him into silence. During the crisis the boy shook his head, making a gesture Antoine could not understand. Only when the fever subsided and calm returned did François explain:

_"Don't worry, I'm not suffering. Nothing hurts. I can't help it. It's my body. I'll look as if I've died, but that's not true. Do you understand? It's too far. I can't take this body with me. It's too heavy. It will be like an old bark that one casts off. Old barks are not sad."_

For a moment he lay still, without crying out. Then he fell slowly, the way a tree falls.

François de Saint-Exupéry died at the age of fifteen. The cause was a complication after a streptococcal infection – an illness which, in those times, with no antibiotics available, could kill a young, strong man within days.

For Antoine, who was seventeen, his brother's death was a blow so deep that he locked it inside himself like a foreign body. His grief was so great that he could not express it. Twenty-five years were to pass before he managed to describe that winter morning farewell.

The words of François, remembered that day, would return in the least expected place. They would be placed in the mouth of a little golden-haired boy who says goodbye to the pilot in the desert:

_"It will be like an old bark that one casts off. Old barks are not sad."_

---

## CHAPTER IV: THE SKY

_Toulouse, 1926_

Antoine was twenty-six when he applied to the Latecoère airline. He was accepted, but not straight into a pilot's cockpit. First he had to pass a trial in the mechanical workshops, where he came to know from the inside the machines he was to fly. It was a period of learning humility. Antoine, an aristocrat and aesthete, had to dirty his hands with grease and understand that an aeroplane is not a winged Pegasus but a fragile construction of metal, wood and canvas.

Only after a few weeks was he allowed to fly on the Toulouse–Dakar route. That was when he met the people who changed his life. Henri Guillaumet and Jean Mermoz – two aces of the postal aviation – became more than colleagues to him. They were brothers, comrades in arms, the first real friends Antoine had had since the death of François.

A particular bond connected him to Guillaumet. An experienced pilot, calm and self-assured, he took Antoine under his wing. He radiated a sense of safety, like a lamp radiating light. One evening, in a shirt with rolled-up sleeves, arms crossed, smiling the kindest of smiles, Guillaumet told him simply:

_"Storms, fog, snow — all of this will sometimes get in your way. Then think of those who went through the same before you and tell yourself: 'What others managed, you can always manage.'"_

They were words as plain as bread. But Antoine remembered them forever, because they held a wisdom he found in no books.

Postal flights proved to be an extreme experience. The company lost many pilots – victims of unreliable machines and of the dangers lurking over the desert. Antoine carried out extremely risky missions and showed a courage that surprised even his experienced colleagues. In the air he became someone else – focused, determined, ready for anything.

But on the ground, in the staging towns, he was unbearably bored. The only occupation that gave him satisfaction besides flying was writing.

---

## CHAPTER V: THE DESERT

_Cabo Juby, 1927_

In 1927 Antoine was appointed head of the staging post at Cabo Juby on the southern coast of Morocco, at the edge of the Sahara. It was a key station for the postal line, the place where aeroplanes landed to refuel. It lay on territory controlled by the Spanish, but surrounded by the land of Mauritanian rebels, who regularly shot down postal planes and took pilots prisoner.

Antoine, an educated man of good manners, was sent as a kind of ambassador with the task of rebuilding dialogue between the Spanish and the Moors. He learned Arabic, invited Mauritanian chieftains to tea at the fort, and they repaid him with invitations under their tents two kilometres deep into rebel territory.

In a letter to his mother he wrote:

_"My dear Mama, it is complete bareness. A bed of a plank and a thin mattress, a basin, a jug of water. I forget the trinkets. A typewriter and airport papers. A monk's room. A marabout comes every day to give me Arabic lessons."_

Between the planes flying over every eight days, Antoine lived in a silence so deep that he could hear his own thoughts. And those thoughts began to form into something greater than letters. In the silence, in the heat, in the emptiness, where there was no one to admire him, flatter him or comfort him, Antoine discovered that he could bear being alone with himself.

In Cabo Juby things also happened that made him a legend among airmen. He performed a series of rescue feats – finding lost pilots, negotiating the release of hostages, flying over hostile territory in conditions in which more experienced pilots refused to take off. For this service, at the age of thirty, he received the Knight's Cross of the Legion of Honour.

But the real treasure Antoine brought back from the Sahara was not a decoration. It was a manuscript. In the silence of desert nights, by the light of an oil lamp, at the typewriter standing next to the jug of water, he wrote his first book: _"Southern Mail"_. It was a story about the airmen of the postal line, about the dangers they faced, about the friendship that bound them more strongly than blood ties.

The solitude of Cabo Juby turned out to be not a punishment but a gift.

---

## CHAPTER VI: THE ROSE

_Buenos Aires, 1930_

Consuelo Suncín was everything that the well-bred Parisian women Antoine had known until then were not. Small, dark-haired, of fiery temperament – she painted, wrote, sculpted and spoke so fast it was hard to keep up with her. She was full of fantasy, humour and unpredictability.

Antoine, accustomed to women from good homes, cautious and calculating, felt in her presence like an explorer who had suddenly come upon an unknown land. He offered her a ride in an aeroplane. What happened in the air, Consuelo later told with disarming frankness.

Antoine sat her beside him in the cockpit, closed the curtain to be alone with her. After three minutes of flight he said: _"Kiss me"_ and inclined his head toward her. Consuelo replied that she had known him for half an hour, and in her country one kissed only those one loved. Then Antoine switched off the engine. In the sudden silence, a few hundred metres above the ground, he said:

_"I know why you refuse, because I am ugly and bald."_

Consuelo saw his black, shining eyes and a child's tears running down his cheeks. She leaned over and kissed him gently.

_"And since then I still kiss him."_ – she said many years later.

The wedding took place on 22 April 1931. It was a marriage that was to last until the end of Antoine's life, although it was neither peaceful nor happy in the conventional sense. Their relationship was stormy, full of quarrels, partings and returns. But Antoine never abandoned her. He kept her by his side as his wife, although he knew he could not be the husband she needed.

In "The Little Prince" the rose is capricious, touchy, demanding, vain, yet unique. Antoine never hid that the rose was Consuelo. Their stormy relationship found in the story a gentle form, suffused with tenderness. As if only in a fairy tale could Antoine express what he could not say to his wife in life.

_"One must never listen to what flowers say. One must simply look at them and breathe in their fragrance."_

---

## CHAPTER VII: THE DISASTER

_Sahara, 29 December 1935_

The Simoun struck the stony plateau at full speed. The roar of metal, a fountain of sand, and then silence. Antoine and his mechanic André Prévot sat in the cockpit of the crushed machine, stunned, covered in blood, but alive.

Around them stretched the Sahara. This time not as a fort's host, but as castaways. Without water, without food and without radio. Between the two of them they had two oranges, a few grapes, a small cake, half a litre of coffee in a broken thermos and a quarter of a litre of white wine. The liquids lasted one day.

For four days they walked through the desert. They saw mirages, had auditory hallucinations. By day the temperature reached fifty degrees, at night it fell to zero. Their bodies were so dehydrated that they stopped sweating. Antoine later recorded:

_"I am very happy that I can sleep. My fatigue surrounds me with a manifold presence. I am not alone in the desert. My nap is populated with voices, memories, whispered confidences."_

On the fourth day, when they were already walking on their last strength, a Bedouin on a camel appeared on the horizon. He gave them water and took them in.

Their rescue became a great media event. Antoine, until then a well-known airman and respected writer, became a legend – a man who had twice cheated death, who had survived what should have killed him, and who could describe his own dying with poetic precision.

From this experience Antoine carried away an image that was to return in the most important work of his life. A pilot who crashes in the desert and there, in absolute solitude, meets someone who changes everything. For now it was a Bedouin on a camel. In a few years it was to be a little boy with golden hair.

---

## CHAPTER VIII: THE GREAT WAR

_New York, 1942_

America had finally entered the war. Antoine, cut off from his homeland, besieged by compatriots who saw him as an enemy, was living through the darkest period of his life. His body was a catalogue of injuries, fractures and scars. Old fractures made themselves felt with every movement. Military doctors categorically opposed letting him fly.

But Antoine would not accept refusal. He wrote applications, mobilised connections, asked, insisted, begged. The argument he used was simple and irrefutable: _"I cannot demand that others risk their lives while I sit in a safe place."_

In this state of suspension, between depression and creativity, between a sense of hopelessness and the need to create something beautiful, Antoine began to write and illustrate _"The Little Prince"_.

Every element of this seemingly innocent story had its roots in his life. The pilot crashing in the Sahara is Antoine himself, the airman and castaway. The number of planet B612 — in popular readings of Antoine's life linked to the serial number of a machine on the Toulouse–Dakar route — becomes in the fairy tale the address of a small world. The baobabs, sinister trees whose seeds must be pulled up by the roots before they take over the whole planet – written in the year when fascism, not uprooted in time, was tearing Europe apart.

The fox, who teaches the little prince that to tame means to create bonds – Antoine had seen desert fennec foxes in the Sahara when he worked in Cabo Juby. He wrote to his sister that he had tamed one and grown to love it.

And the Little Prince himself, the golden-haired boy who comes from nowhere and departs into the sky, was a figure built of many layers. There was little Tonio in him, the Sun King with the green throne. There were the boys Antoine met in life. But deepest, at the very core of the figure, was someone concrete – François, the younger brother, the fair-haired boy who died at fifteen and who never grew up.

When the publishers pressed Antoine to change the ending – claiming that children would not accept the death of the little prince – Antoine refused categorically. The death scene was written with words he had carried inside himself for twenty-five years:

_"For a moment he stood still. He did not cry out. Then he fell slowly, the way a tree falls. Slowly and silently, because sand muffles all sounds."_

These were almost literally the same words Antoine had heard and remembered on the winter morning of 1917, when François was dying at his side.

This was no coincidence. It was a deliberate transfer of the most intimate memory into the fabric of a fairy tale. The gesture of a man who had finally opened the chest of memories and taken out what had been too painful to speak of for a quarter of a century.

_"The Little Prince"_ was not a fairy tale for children. It was a farewell – to a brother, to childhood, to hope, to the France that Antoine had known and loved, and that had ceased to exist. It was the testament of a man who knew he would soon return to the war and might not come back from it.

## EPILOGUE

_Mediterranean Sea, 31 July 1944_

At 8:30 in the morning Antoine took off from Corsica on his ninth combat mission. He was to fly over the Rhône valley and return. The planned flight time was a few hours.

At 13:30 the fuel in the tanks of his Lightning P38 was supposed to run out. Antoine did not return. He made no radio contact, sent no signal. He simply vanished somewhere over the Mediterranean, between sky and water, in a blue so intense that one could not tell one from the other.

He was forty-four years old.

For decades nothing was known about the circumstances of his death. All sorts of versions were discussed – from engine failure, through being shot down by the enemy, to suicide. None of them had evidence, because neither the wreck of the plane nor the pilot's body was ever found.

Only in September 1998 did a fisherman pull from the sea a silver bracelet engraved with Antoine's name and surname. In May 2000, the remains of the aircraft were discovered on the seabed. Fragments of the fuselage were scattered over a strip a kilometre long. In the autumn of 2003, a fragment bearing a serial number was raised from the bottom, which unambiguously identified the machine as Antoine's aeroplane.

The mystery of his death remained unsolved. Perhaps the engine failed. Perhaps Antoine, unable to turn his head, did not notice the danger. And perhaps he simply flew too far, as he did every time in his life – playing truant in the sky, as he wrote to his mother as a twenty-year-old flying student.

Antoine left, but the Little Prince remained.

_"What is essential is invisible to the eye. One sees clearly only with the heart."_

---

## AFTERWORD

Antoine de Saint-Exupéry wrote _"The Little Prince"_ in the darkest period of his life – in exile, in New York, far from his homeland, in the shadow of a war that was tearing Europe to shreds. Every page of this book bears the traces of his life – a lost brother, the solitude of the desert, the stormy love for Consuelo, the longing for the sky.

Today _"The Little Prince"_ is among the most translated books in the world — it has been rendered into hundreds of languages and dialects. Its author vanished over the Mediterranean, but his work survived. And every evening, somewhere in the world, someone opens this book and hears the laughter of a golden-haired boy among the stars.

That is the greatest mystery of Saint-Exupéry. Not how he died. But that in dying he left us something that will never die.

Something that cannot be seen with the eyes.

Something that can be seen only with the heart.

---

**THE END**
