**THE SPARK**

From "The Shadow of Wings over the Ocean" – the moment when a two-year-old child calls out at night: _"A crashed plane! It's burning! A little man can't get out!"_ And then, when it asks its mother who Billy, Walter and Leon are – and they turn out to be real pilots who died in the same squadron.

**This is not a story about reincarnation. This is a story about what a child can know, and what it shouldn't.**

---

# THE STORY

## WHAT HE KNEW

When I first heard it at night, I thought it was an ordinary nightmare. Children have nightmares. My son was three years old, had only just begun to put together his first sentences, and already he was screaming in the middle of the night about things he couldn't possibly know.

"A crashed plane! It's burning!"

I ran into his room. He was lying on his back, pounding his fists into the mattress, kicking his legs in the air as if he were trying to free himself from something that held him captive. He was drenched in sweat. He was crying, but he wouldn't wake up.

— Dad! Help! The little man can't get out!

I grabbed him, held him. For a few minutes he didn't stop screaming. Then, suddenly, he fell quiet, opened his eyes, looked at me as if I were a stranger, and fell back asleep.

I thought it would pass. That it was just a phase.

It didn't pass.

---

Over the following weeks every night was the same. At the same time. With the same words. "A crashed plane." "It's burning." "The little man can't get out."

My wife Ewa began to be afraid of falling asleep. She sat in the kitchen with a cup of tea, waiting for the scream she knew would come. I couldn't sleep either. I tried reading books about night terrors, about children's nightmares. Nothing fit. These weren't ordinary dreams. This was something else.

— Maybe we should see a specialist — Ewa said one morning, when for the first time in a week we had both spent the whole night awake.

— What kind of specialist? — I asked.

— I don't know. A psychologist. Someone who works with children.

— He's three years old.

— I know. But something is wrong.

I went to work with a head full of questions. I sat in front of the computer pretending to work, and in my head I heard only my son's voice: "The little man can't get out."

I came home early. Ewa was in the kitchen, and our son was sitting on the floor in the living room. In front of him lay his toys – the planes we had bought him for his birthday. He played with them in a way that had always seemed strange to me. He didn't fly them like other children. He didn't imitate the sound of engines. He simply... crashed them. He banged one against another until the wings fell off. Then he left them on the floor and went to the next one.

I sat down next to him.

— Hey, little man.

— Hi, Dad.

— What are you thinking about?

He looked at me with that serious gaze of his that always surprised me. As if he were looking at something I couldn't see.

— About planes — he said.

— And what happens to the planes?

— They fall.

— They fall?

— Yes. And they can't get out.

I picked up one of the crashed planes. Plastic, cheap, from the supermarket.

— And who flies them?

— Men.

— What kind of men?

— The ones who won't come back.

Ewa stood in the doorway, white as a sheet. She had heard what he said. I saw her grip tighten on the door frame.

— How do you know that? — I asked.

— I see it.

— What do you see?

He looked at me. His eyes were clear, calm. As if he were talking about something obvious.

— I see them fall. And burn inside. And call out, but no one hears them.

Ewa left the kitchen. I heard her crying in the bedroom. I stayed with him on the floor, the crashed planes around us.

— Who calls out? — I asked.

— The friends.

— What are their names?

He looked at me. Then he pointed at the three crashed planes lying next to each other.

— Billy — he said. — Walter. And Leon.

— How do you know those names?

He shrugged.

— They're there.

---

I checked the internet. I don't know what I was looking for. Maybe some proof that it was only a coincidence. That three years old is too young for such things. That it was impossible.

I found a list of fallen pilots from VMF-221, the squadron that fought in the Pacific in 1943. The names: Piller, Devlin, Conner.

Billy. Walter. Leon.

I leaned against the desk and closed my eyes.

---

At night he screamed again. This time differently. Not "The little man can't get out". This time he called:

— Leon! Leon! Come back! We're waiting! Come back!

Ewa ran into his room. I stayed in the hallway, leaning against the wall, my hands trembling.

— Who is Leon? — Ewa asked, trying to wake him. — Sweetheart, who is he?

— They're waiting! — he screamed. — Billy and Walter! Leon has to come back! He can't stay!

And then, suddenly, as if someone had flipped a switch, he stopped. He opened his eyes. He looked at his mother. He smiled.

— Mum — he said calmly. — I'm thirsty.

Ewa picked him up and carried him to the kitchen. She gave him water. I watched them from the hallway, feeling that I was standing on the edge of something I didn't understand.

---

I went to the archive. I didn't tell Ewa where I was going. I said I had to take care of something for work.

In the military archive in Washington I found a folder labelled: "VMF-221 — combat losses, 1943". I opened it. Inside were photographs. Young men in uniforms, smiling, their hands on the wings of planes. Some of them had died. Some had come back. But they were all there.

Billy Piller. Walter Devlin. Leon Conner.

I remembered my son's words: "The friends. They're there."

---

I came home late in the evening. My son was already asleep. Ewa was sitting in the living room, a lamp burning, a book on her lap that she wasn't reading.

— You went to the archive — she said. It wasn't a question.

— Yes.

— Did you find something?

— I did.

— What?

I showed her the photographs. Three young men. Billy. Walter. Leon.

Ewa looked at them. Then at me.

— That's impossible — she said.

— I know.

— How does he know?

— I don't know.

We sat in silence. From our son's room came his calm breathing. I went inside, sat on the edge of his bed. I watched him, his face, on which there was no more fear. Only calm.

— Who is Leon? — I asked quietly.

He didn't answer. But then, through the open window, I heard the wind. And I remembered something he had said a few days earlier: "They're waiting."

---

In the morning, when he woke up, I asked him:

— Did they come back?

He looked at me. He smiled.

— Yes, Dad.

— Who?

— Everyone.

He pointed at the window. At the sky, which was clear and blue.

— They're there.

And from that night on, he never screamed again.

---

Sometimes, when I watch him sleeping peacefully in his bed, I think about all those nights. About the names he knew before anyone ever told him. About the wind that brought him images from places he had never been.

I don't know what it was. Maybe something I don't understand. Maybe something I'll never understand.

But I know one thing: every night when I hear the wind, I think of them. Billy. Walter. Leon. And of my son, who knew they were waiting. And that in the end they came back.

---

**THE END**
