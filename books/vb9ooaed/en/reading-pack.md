## Text

**SILENCE BEFORE THE STORM**

October 1932, Warsaw. In a small, dusty room at the back of the General Staff building on Ujazdowskie Avenue, Marian Rejewski saw the Enigma for the first time. Not the whole machine, because that lay safely in German barracks, but its spirit – the documentation that Polish intelligence had obtained thanks to their French colleagues, who in turn had got it from a German traitor who had sold the Reich's secrets for a handful of marks.

Rejewski was twenty-seven, fresh from his mathematics studies in Poznań, and even fresher from a cryptography course that he had been admitted to as one of the few. He was quiet, focused, with eyes that saw more than most people. When he looked at the Enigma diagrams, he felt that this wasn't just a machine. It was a challenge.

– There's no point analysing the texts – he said to his colleagues, Henryk Zygalski and Jerzy Różycki, who stood behind him. – We have to understand how the device itself works. It's not language, it's mathematics.

Zygalski, who always carried more scepticism than enthusiasm, shrugged.

– The machine has millions of combinations. You can't check them all.

– We don't have to check them all – Rejewski replied, already laying out the first calculations on the table. – We have to find their logic.

And he found it. Within a few weeks, working nights by an oil lamp, Rejewski discovered the key to the Enigma – the way the connections ran between the keyboard and the rotors. It turned out that the German designers, though brilliant at creating complex systems, were like most people – they chose the simplest solution. Alphabetical order.

– That's impossible – said Zygalski, when Rejewski showed him his results.

– And yet – Rejewski replied with a smile that didn't come to him often. – The Germans are systematic. And that is their weakness.


**The cryptologic bomb**

The years passed, and the Enigma became more and more complicated. The Germans introduced additional safeguards, changed settings, added new rotors. The Polish cryptologists had to constantly devise new methods to keep up with the enemy. So Rejewski designed a machine that was to speed up the process of breaking ciphers – the "cryptologic bomb", an electromechanical device that simulated the operation of several Enigmas at once and automatically checked the possible combinations.

The first bomb was built in 1938. It was big, heavy, noisy – but it worked. In two hours it could find the daily Enigma key. What used to take cryptologists weeks was now a matter of two hours.

– This changes everything – said Różycki, watching the machine process one combination after another. – Now we can read German orders before they reach their own units.

– If only we keep up the pace – added Zygalski. – The Germans don't sleep.

Indeed, the Germans were already working on an even harder version of the Enigma – with five rotors instead of three. The Poles knew their methods might soon become obsolete. They needed time. And money. Both resources were running out faster than they had expected.


**July 1939, the forest near Pyry**

The secret meeting took place in a villa in Pyry, near Warsaw. Documents and diagrams lay on the table, and in the corner stood a reconstructed Enigma and the Polish "bomb". Opposite the Polish mathematicians sat British and French cryptologists.

Alistair Denniston's face, the head of the British cryptographic section, was grey with shock. For years the British had tried to break the Enigma without much success. And now three Poles were showing them a ready solution.

– How is this possible? – asked Denniston, examining the bomb. – How did you manage to do it?

– Mathematics – Rejewski replied. – And a bit of luck.

– And German systematicity – added Zygalski.

Denniston nodded. He knew that what he was seeing changed everything. The Poles handed over everything – documents, methods, machines. And this on the eve of war.

– You're going to need it – said Rejewski. – The Germans will attack soon.

Denniston looked at him. In his eyes was a question that never came: "And what about you?"

Rejewski answered it before it was asked:

– We're staying. We'll fight. But you have to continue what we started.


**Bletchley Park, 1940**

While bombs fell on London and German U-boats spread terror across the Atlantic, a quiet war was being waged at Bletchley Park, about eighty kilometres north of the capital. In the buildings that had once been stables and ballrooms, thousands of people now worked. Mathematicians, linguists, engineers, and also women – who in those days were not often seen in such responsible roles.

Alan Turing was one of them. He was thirty-one, with an unruly mop of hair and a manner that made some consider him an eccentric. Others – a genius. And both were right.

Turing didn't start from scratch. When he arrived at Bletchley Park, he already had the knowledge the Poles had given the British a year earlier. He knew how the Enigma worked, he knew the Polish methods, he had seen the bomb. But he also knew that wasn't enough. The Germans changed the settings daily, and the war demanded answers in real time.

– We need something faster – he told his team. – Something that will test combinations automatically, without human involvement.

He began to design a machine that was to do what the Polish bomb did, but faster and more efficiently. He used Rejewski's ideas, but added his own – mathematical logic that allowed entire groups of settings to be eliminated at once.

– This isn't just a code-breaking machine – he told his colleagues. – This is the prototype of a computer. A machine that thinks.

Some looked at him with disbelief. But when the first "Bombe" – for that was what his machine was called – began to work, all doubts vanished. In a dozen or so minutes it could find Enigma settings that would have taken cryptologists days by hand.


**The mistake that saved millions**

The secret of success turned out to be not only machines, but also human mistakes. The German Enigma operators, though trained to be perfectionists, were like everyone – tired, distracted, sometimes lazy. Their weakness turned out to be the habit of repetitive phrases. "Heil Hitler" at the beginning of a report. "Keine besonderen Ereignisse" – "nothing special to report" – at the end. These predictable fragments of text gave cryptologists an anchor point.

But what helped them most was a procedure that was supposed to increase security. Every operator had to type the message key three times at the beginning of the transmission. That repetition, meant to prevent transmission errors, became the biggest mistake.

– They gave us the key to their secret themselves – Turing once told his team. – They repeat the same pattern, so we recognise it. It's as if they left the door open.

Thanks to this and other weaknesses, Bletchley Park became the heart of Allied intelligence. Thousands of German messages were decrypted every day and passed on to commanders at the fronts. Information about U-boat movements, planned attacks, supplies – all of it reached the Allies before the German commanders had time to use it.


**The price of victory**

The war ended. Germany surrendered, and Europe began to rise from the ruins. But for Alan Turing, the end of the war did not mean the end of his problems. His work at Bletchley Park remained a secret – only a few dozen people knew about it, and he himself could tell no one about what he had achieved.

In 1952, when the world was slowly forgetting the war, Turing was accused of homosexuality – at the time a crime in Britain. He was given a choice: prison or chemical castration. He chose the latter, but the price was terrible. He lost his sense of self, his identity, and ultimately – his will to live.

On 7 June 1954 Alan Turing died. He was found in his home, next to him a bitten apple soaked in cyanide. He was forty-one years old.

Only many years later did people begin to appreciate his contribution. In 2009 the British prime minister Gordon Brown apologised for the way Turing had been treated. In 2013 Queen Elizabeth II pardoned him. Today his name is synonymous with genius, but it also symbolises the injustice an individual can suffer at the hands of the system.


**What remains**

Marian Rejewski, Henryk Zygalski and Jerzy Różycki never received fame to match Turing's. After the war Rejewski returned to Poland, where he worked as an ordinary accountant. He never spoke about his wartime work. Zygalski emigrated to Britain, where he died forgotten. Różycki died in 1942 in a ship disaster off the coast of Algeria.

But their contribution to the Allied victory was beyond measure. They were the first to look at the Enigma not as a linguistic puzzle, but as a mathematical problem. They built the first tools that made it possible to break it. They passed their knowledge on to the British at the very moment they themselves stood on the edge of an abyss.

Without the Polish mathematicians, the war could have lasted two, three, or perhaps four years longer. Millions more lives would have been lost. What they did changed the course of history – even though for many years their role remained in the shadows.

Today, at Bletchley Park, there is a monument dedicated to the Polish cryptologists. It isn't big. It isn't grand. But it's there. And on it stand three names: Rejewski, Zygalski, Różycki.

And beneath them – an inscription that says everything: "Here began the story of victory".


**THE END**
