**RADIO IN THE HEAD**

When Kasia was seven years old, a radio played in her head. Not an ordinary one, with a single station. In her head, every station played at once. Someone was talking about the weather, someone else about politics, someone else was playing music, and in the background someone was reading the news. All at the same time. And no button worked to turn it off.

She sat at her desk, and the teacher was saying something about addition. Kasia heard the words, but in her head someone was just telling a story about dragons. A moment later someone else reminded her that yesterday she'd forgotten to pack her PE kit. And then the radio switched to a song she'd heard in the car. And suddenly the teacher looked at her and asked:

– Kasia, what's seven plus eight?

Kasia froze. The radio in her head suddenly went quiet, but in the silence there was no answer. Only emptiness. And shame.

– Well, Kasia? Seven plus eight?

– I don't know – she whispered.

The teacher sighed.

– But that's exactly what we were talking about. Maybe you could listen?

Kasia lowered her eyes. She knew she'd been listening. It was just that in her head it was too loud to hear.

---

At home it was similar. Kasia had her own room, but she rarely tidied it. Not because she didn't want to. It was simply that when she started tidying, the radio in her head told her about something else. About the book she wanted to read. About the drawing she wanted to finish. About the fact that dinner was coming soon. And suddenly it turned out that an hour had passed and she'd only managed to move one toy from one place to another.

– Kasia, tidy up that room already! – her mum would say. – How many times do I have to tell you?

– I'm doing it, Mum.

But Kasia couldn't. Not because she didn't want to. Her brain simply didn't know where to start. Toys on the floor, books on the desk, clothes on the chair. Everything shouted at her at once: "Start with me!". And Kasia froze, unable to take a single step.

---

At school Kasia was "the weird one". Sometimes she stood up in the middle of a lesson and walked around the classroom. Not because she wanted to disturb anyone. Her legs simply carried her by themselves. As if she had a battery inside that was constantly recharging, and the only way to discharge it was movement.

– Kasia, sit down – the teacher would say.

Kasia sat. But after five minutes she stood up again.

– Kasia, we don't stand up during lessons.

– But I can't sit still.

– You can, you just don't want to.

Kasia didn't know how to explain that it wasn't true. That she really did want to sit still. That she was trying. That sometimes she thought: "Now I'll sit, I won't get up, I promise." And then, before she realised it, she was already standing.

---

When Kasia was ten, her mum took her to a psychologist. They sat in the office, and the psychologist asked questions.

– Does Kasia have trouble concentrating?

– Enormous – answered her mum. – At school they say she's daydreaming.

– Is she hyperactive?

– Very. She can't sit still.

– Does she have trouble with organisation?

– She can't tidy her room. She loses things. She forgets her homework.

The psychologist nodded and looked at Kasia.

– Kasia, how do you feel about it?

Kasia thought for a moment.

– I have a radio in my head – she said. – Every station plays at once. And I can't turn it off. Sometimes when someone talks to me, I hear, but I don't listen, because something else is playing on the radio. And then I don't know what it was about.

The psychologist smiled.

– That's a very good description. And you know what? For many people with ADHD, the radio is exactly like that. Not because they don't want to listen. They simply have trouble turning down all the stations.

Kasia felt relieved. Someone finally understood.

---

After the diagnosis everything changed. Kasia got medication that helped her quieten the radio a little. It didn't turn it off completely — it still played. But now she could choose one station and focus on it.

At school the teacher seated her at the front, close to herself. Far from the window, so she wouldn't be distracted by what was happening outside. Kasia got a sensory fidget — a small, soft ball she could squeeze in her hand during lessons. It helped her focus. When she felt like getting up, she squeezed the fidget and it gave her some relief.

– You can stand up – said the teacher – but only if you don't disturb anyone. And only for a moment.

Kasia learned that she could stand up, stretch, walk over to the quiet corner, and then come back to her desk. It was better than fighting her own body.

---

At home her mum made changes too. Kasia's room was tidied up — not perfectly, but so there weren't too many stimuli. Toys were in boxes, books on the shelf. Kasia got a list of tasks for each day, written in big letters. She could cross off the tasks she'd already done.

– See – said her mum. – It's not that hard. First we get dressed, then we brush our teeth, then we pack the backpack. Step by step.

Kasia looked at the list and felt safer. She knew what to do. She didn't have to think about everything at once.

And before bed — no screens. No blue light to disrupt the daily rhythm. Instead — reading books. And silence. Slowly, very slowly, Kasia began to sleep better.

---

When Kasia was fourteen, she began to understand her ADHD. She knew it wasn't her fault. She knew her brain worked differently, not worse. She knew she had strengths — she was creative, she had great ideas, she could throw herself into the things that interested her with incredible force. It was called hyperfocus. And when she used it, she could do things others couldn't.

– I'm not lazy – she told her reflection in the mirror. – I'm not stupid. I just have ADHD.

And that was okay.

---

One day, in a homeroom class, the teacher asked whether anyone knew what ADHD was. No one answered. Kasia raised her hand.

– It means you have a radio in your head – she said. – Every station plays at once. And you can't turn it off. But you can learn to choose one station.

The class looked at her in surprise. The teacher smiled.

– And how do you know that, Kasia?

– Because I have ADHD.

Silence fell. And then someone from the back asked:

– So, does that mean you're sick?

Kasia shook her head.

– No. It means my brain works differently. Not worse. Differently. And sometimes it's hard, but sometimes it's great. Like when I get interested in something, I can think about it all day. And then I'm the best at it.

Someone laughed, but not unkindly. Someone else said:

– I'm like that too. Sometimes I can't focus. Maybe I have ADHD too?

Kasia smiled.

– Maybe. Or maybe you're just human. Everyone has trouble concentrating sometimes. But if it lasts all the time and gets in the way of your life, it's worth getting checked.

---

Today Kasia is twenty. She's studying psychology. She wants to help children like her. Children who hear a radio in their head and think they're bad, stupid, lazy. She knows it's not true. She knows they only need someone to tell them: "You're not bad. Your brain just works differently. And that's okay."

She still has a radio in her head. But she's learned to live with it. She's learned to choose the stations she wants to listen to. She's learned to ask for help when she needs it. She's learned that she doesn't have to be perfect. It's enough that she's herself.

Sometimes, when she's having a bad day, she thinks back to that psychologist's office. To the question that changed everything: "How do you feel about it?"

And she thinks about how sometimes it's enough for someone to ask. And listen. And understand.

The radio in her head keeps playing. But now it's music she likes.

**THE END**
