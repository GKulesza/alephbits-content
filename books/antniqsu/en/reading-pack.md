## Text

**THE ORDER**

Michał closed his laptop and stared at the ceiling. Beyond the window, on the 15th floor, Warsaw stretched out grey and tired, like him. Another project, another deadline, another week without sleep. It was Tuesday, and he could already feel Friday in his bones. Except that Friday brought no relief — just two days to catch his breath before climbing back onto the same carousel on Monday.

In the open-plan kitchen, Kasia from Finance was on the phone. Loudly. About how she couldn't take a holiday because the boss wouldn't sign it. And she had 18 days of leave piling up. "Then take sick leave" — Michał heard someone laugh from the other end of the room. Someone quipped: "You can just buy one online, can't you?"

He smiled to himself. But the words stuck.

---
That evening, sitting in his rented flat on Wolska Street, Michał opened a browser. He typed in the phrase that had been circling his head since the morning.

_sick leave L4 fast online_

The results appeared in a second. Sites, sites, sites. Colourful banners, big slogans: "SICK LEAVE ONLINE IN JUST 3 MINUTES", "NO QUEUES, NO UNNECESSARY QUESTIONS", "ORDER GUARANTEE OR YOUR MONEY BACK".

Michał kept scrolling. The next offer: "L4 in 5 minutes – convenience and speed. No unnecessary questions. Available around the clock." He mentally quoted a passage from the site that somehow moved him: _"You don't have to wait in queues. No unnecessary questions."_

No unnecessary questions.

That sentence hung in the air. After all, a doctor should ask questions. Thousands of questions. About symptoms, medical history, place of work, whether you can lift weights, touch keyboards, breathe cold air. Yet these sites were selling the _absence of questions_ as a product.

Michał picked the first one that came up: `zwolnienie.pl`. The form filled itself in — first name, surname, PESEL number, address. In the "symptoms" field he wrote: "cold, weakness, headache". He worked out how many days he wanted. He picked five.

He paid with BLIK. 79 zlotys.

After 15 minutes a certificate appeared in his inbox. A PDF file. Signed by a doctor, Marcin Z., from Centrum Zdrowia. No phone call, no chat, no examination. He was sitting in the kitchen drinking tea, completely healthy — and he had just received an official sick-leave certificate with the diagnosis "acute upper respiratory tract infection".

He didn't know whether to laugh or cry.

---
At work on Monday he sent his sick leave to HR. He got an automatic confirmation.

"Sick leave registered. We wish you a speedy recovery."

All week he slept in. He watched series. He read a book he hadn't had time to finish for a year. He went for a walk in Park Skaryszewski when the weather was good. He felt relief. Except that somewhere in his head a quiet, irritating sound kept sitting — like a warning light in a car that glows but you don't know what it means.

Was he sick? No. So he had cheated the system. But he wasn't the only one. The doctor who had issued the certificate without an examination had cheated it too. The company that automatically sent out certificates had cheated it too. And everyone pretended this was normal.

On Tuesday he returned to the thought that had been circling for days. His company pays him 80% of his salary for that week. That's around 2,500 zlotys net. Work stands still. Others on the team have more responsibilities. The project is delayed. The boss will be annoyed.

And him? He's lying on the sofa watching Netflix.

He started reading more about the scheme. He came across an article saying that last year Poles were on sick leave for 290 million days. Doctors issued 27 million certificates. An average L4 lasted 11 days. The cost to the Polish economy — 35 billion zlotys. These weren't just numbers — this was a swindle that bowed every company, restaurant, shop and factory under its weight. Every employer pays for someone who sleeps.

And it wasn't only people like him, Michał, tired of the corporate world. There were people who took sick leave for holidays, for weddings, for laying paving stones in front of their house. And they did it legally — because who would check them? ZUS? The prosecutor's office? The same people who closed their eyes, because the problem didn't hit the state budget, only private companies.

---
On Wednesday Michał went back to the websites. This time he wasn't looking for sick leave — he was looking for answers. He found an article about 56,000 certificates issued by three doctors in Greater Poland. ZUS brought charges against them. At worst they faced 12 months of being banned from issuing certificates.

He read on about a doctor from Lublin who issued 2,500 certificates a month. The medical court gave her a reprimand, a warning and a two-year ban on teleconsultations. _Mitigating circumstance_ — she had acted under pressure from her employer, who told her: "Your job is not to treat patients, but to issue certificates."

Michał closed the browser. An image was forming in his head: a system that assumes the doctor will be diligent — and a system that does nothing when the doctor stops being diligent. And in the middle of that hole — billions of zlotys and hundreds of thousands of workers who don't work even though they're healthy.

---
He thought about his father. He ran a small joinery workshop in the countryside. Three employees. Every sick leave — and there were quite a few — was a loss for him. He had to climb into the workshop himself, work overtime, make it up at night. "Son," he once said, "I don't mind when someone is really sick. But when they take sick leave to pick mushrooms on their plot, I'd like to have the right to check it. I don't. The state doesn't give me the tools."

Back then Michał thought his father was exaggerating. Now he knew he had been right.

On Friday, the last day of his bought sick leave, Michał got up early. He switched on his laptop and found the address for public notifications. He wrote a short email — to ZUS, to the Supreme Medical Chamber, to the District Prosecutor's Office. He described his story. He attached screenshots. He wrote: "Please check the doctor Marcin Z. from Centrum Zdrowia, who issued me a certificate of incapacity for work without any examination."

He sent it. He knows nothing will come of it. That officials have their tables, statistics, algorithms — and in practice they make you wait years for an answer. But he did it. Because someone had to start.

---
On Monday he returned to the office. Kasia from Finance looked at him with sympathy.

— Feeling better now?

— Yes.

— Good. You can't imagine what went on here. We had two people on sick leave in the team, everyone doing overtime.

Michał nodded. He sat down at his desk. He opened his inbox — nothing, no reply from the prosecutor's office. Of course.

In the kitchen someone was joking again: "Got some leave coming? Take a sick leave online." Someone laughed. Michał didn't. He grabbed his coffee and went back to the computer. Numbers circled in his head: 35 billion. 56,000 certificates. 19 seconds of conversation with a doctor who then issues a certificate. The law that requires a direct examination. Article 271 on certifying falsehood.

And that question nobody asked publicly: why don't doctors who certify falsehoods end up before the ordinary courts, but before boards that hand them reprimands?

As if someone had deliberately created a system in which nobody bears responsibility. As if a 35-billion-a-year fraud were... acceptable.

Michał looked at his calendar. In two months he'd have a holiday — a real one, 14 days by the sea. Maybe then he'd stop thinking about what he did last week. Maybe he'd stop thinking about the doctor who gave him an L4 without ever laying eyes on him.

Maybe he'd stop thinking that if his father had had the tools to check the truthfulness of certificates, he wouldn't have had to plane boards at night.

But one sentence stayed in his head, the one he'd read on the `zwolnienie.pl` website:

_"Order guarantee or your money back."_

An order.

It wasn't medical advice. It was an order. And Michał had just placed it. Now he was paying for it with something worse than money — with the feeling that he was part of something that killed honesty every day.

In the following days he couldn't shake the impression that everyone at work knew about it. Nobody said it outright, but they knew. When someone was on sick leave for a long time, the boss looked at him suspiciously. When someone brought in a Monday certificate, people whispered: "He was probably at a party."

Michał understood that this one purchase had undermined more than just the company budget. It had undermined trust. And in a world where any sick leave can be bought in 15 minutes, trust is a commodity that can no longer be rebuilt.

Late in the evening, as he sat in front of his monitor, he switched on Mentzen's podcast, which had just been released. He listened as the politician talked about 35 billion, about Germany tightening its rules, about Article 271.

– If the state doesn't want to check whether certificates are fake — the voice in the speakers said — then let the state itself pay for sick leave from the first day. If it shifts this burden onto the employer without giving him the tools to verify, then why should companies pay for it?

Michał nodded.

– Because we think it's not our problem — he said to the empty room. – Until we buy that sick leave ourselves.

He closed his laptop. In the kitchen he made tea. Beyond the window Warsaw flickered with lights, a million windows, a million stories. In some of them people were asleep because they were really sick. In others — asleep because they had bought themselves a few days off for 79 zlotys.

Michał knew he couldn't fix it. Not today. But he could stop being part of the problem.

He threw the printed sick-leave certificate into the bin. He didn't need it anymore.

He needed something else — for the system that allows such things to finally change. But that was a dream that, as he knew, would probably never come true.

At least not while this law is alive.

**THE END**
