**THE LIST**

The phone rang. Ewa looked at the screen – an unknown number. Usually she didn't answer, but today something told her to press the green handset.

– Mrs Ewa? – the voice on the other end was breathless, as if someone had been running. – My name is Tomasz. I'm a journalist from an independent portal. I don't know if you're aware, but you've been placed on the Myrotvorets list.

Ewa froze. She knew what that list was. A Ukrainian portal linked to the special services, which for years has published the names of "enemies of Ukraine". People on that list died. Not all of them, but some. A few months earlier a man who had been on that list was murdered in Poland. A few dozen kilometres from the home of Paweł Wyrzykowski.

– I'm on that list? – she asked calmly, though inside she felt something closing within her.

– Yes. Since this morning. It says it's for your speech in the European Parliament. For that flag.

Ewa thanked him and put down the phone. The phone in her hand was still warm. She sat down on the sofa and stared at the wall. Through the window the afternoon sun was streaming in – the same as always, peaceful, almost indifferent. Outside the window Warsaw was teeming with life. People walked to shops, rode trams, drank coffee in cafés. No one knew that a moment ago her world had changed.

She inevitably recalled that debate. The European Parliament, the plenary hall in Strasbourg, that peculiar silence before her speech. She stood up, walked to the podium, opened her folder. Inside, among the documents, lay a piece of cloth. A red-and-black flag. That was all. Only that much, and at the same time so much.

She spoke about Wołyń, about those who had died. About children dashed against walls. About women impaled on stakes. About the fact that the Ukrainian state still honours the symbolism of its executioners, giving their names to military units. She spoke about what Poland should never forget. And at some point she raised that flag. Not as an insult, not as a provocation. As evidence. Of what Ukrainian statehood is today, if it does not distance itself from the Bandera past.

The camera caught her gesture. The mainstream media in Poland kept silent or criticised. But Ukraine responded instantly. Her name appeared on the Myrotvorets list – one of the most sinister tools of Ukrainian intelligence. People placed on it are followed, harassed, intimidated. Sometimes – eliminated.

Ewa opened her laptop and typed in the address of the site. She found her name. A photograph. A short, venomous description: "Confederation MEP, for anti-Ukrainian activity, for promoting hatred". Beneath it – a message for anyone who wants to help the Ukrainian services: _"Please direct any information about enemies of Ukraine to the address provided."_

She closed the laptop.

In the kitchen she made herself tea. Her hands were cold, though outside it was over twenty degrees. She looked at her reflection in the window pane – the same woman as in the morning, but as if ten years older. She didn't regret it. If she could turn back time, she would do the same again. But the fear she felt was real. Not because she was afraid for herself. She was afraid for her family. For her children, her parents, her husband. They were on that list too. Not by name, but through her.

She thought about what had happened in the European Parliament on the last day of the session. A package of regulations known as Chat Control had been adopted – a mechanism for mass surveillance of internet messengers. In theory it was meant to protect children from paedophiles. In practice – it opened the door to scanning every message, every photo, every link we sent to our loved ones, friends, colleagues. The politicians who voted for it didn't know what they were doing. Or they knew, and it suited them. And those who protested – like Ewa – became targets.

Now her name appeared on the list next to Krzysztof Bosak, Anna Bryłka, Paweł Wyrzykowski, Zbigniew Bogucki. An ever longer list. More and more Poles who had spoken the truth. More and more people they wanted to silence.

Ewa picked up the phone and called Anna.

– You know? – she asked briefly.

– I know – answered Anna Bryłka. – I've been on it for a long time too. Welcome to the club.

– That's not a club I'd like to help create.

– But you're in it. And now you have a choice: either you back down, or you go on. I chose the latter.

Ewa nodded, though Anna couldn't see it. She was right. You can't back down. You can't let death lists decide what may be said. Especially when it comes to Wołyń.

She turned on the computer and opened the file with her speech. She read it once more. It was only a few minutes, but it contained whole generations – those who had been slaughtered, those who were saved, those who forgot, and those who remembered. _"The victims of the Wołyń massacre deserve dignity, they deserve remembrance and they deserve respect"_ – she had said then. She hadn't known those words would put her on a blacklist. But even if she had known, she would still have spoken them.

In the afternoon another call came. This time someone from the Polish foreign ministry.

– Mrs Ewa, we want to assure you that we take the Myrotvorets list matter seriously. We will send a diplomatic note to Kyiv.

– You've had that note ready for years – Ewa replied. – Bosak has been on the list since 2024. Nothing has changed.

There was silence on the other end.

– We understand your frustration, but...
– Don't talk to me about frustration – Ewa cut in. – Talk to me about action. If Ukraine wants to be in the European Union, let it stop keeping death lists of Polish citizens. It's not complicated.

She hung up. She knew the diplomatic note would land in the Ukrainian foreign ministry, where someone would read it, shrug, and throw it in the bin. That's how it had been every time.

Her thoughts returned to that day in Strasbourg. Just before her speech someone from her team had told her: "They'll try to intimidate you." She had answered: "They're already trying. And so what?" Now, sitting in her apartment, she knew that hadn't been boasting. It had been a declaration. And although she was afraid of what might come, she didn't regret a single word.

In the evening she opened her inbox. Hundreds of messages – of support, of insults, from people who thanked her for her courage, and from those who called her an enemy. She read a few. One in particular stayed with her.

_"Mrs Ewa, I am the daughter of a Wołyń survivor. My father never spoke about what he had been through. Only on his deathbed he told how he had watched his mother die. Thank you for reminding the world. Thank you for not letting it forget. Don't let yourself be intimidated. Those who know are with you."_

Ewa put down the phone and looked out the window. Beyond the glass dusk was falling. Warsaw flickered with lights, as if nothing had happened. And yet everything had changed. She – a member of parliament, a mother, a wife, a woman who had snatched the Bandera flag in the European Parliament – had become a target.

She didn't sleep that night. She lay in bed staring at the ceiling, thinking about what Rafał Ziemkiewicz had said in his commentary: "Wołyń was treated abominably. The victims of Wołyń were simple peasants. Settled there for many, many centuries."

And about the fact that today, after so many years, the Wołyń victims had been forgotten again. Forgotten by the world, by the Union that wants to accept Ukraine into its ranks even though it still honours its executioners. Forgotten by the Polish elites, who prefer to apologise rather than be apologised to. Forgotten by those who speak of symmetry – the Poles also did something, the Poles also once. As if genocide could be compared with anything.

At dawn she got up and turned on the computer. She played the recording of her speech. She heard her own voice: "Ukrainian nationalism is incompatible with European values." She smiled bitterly. In the European Parliament no one had protested. But instead – on Ukrainian servers – her name had made it onto a list.

She closed her eyes. She thought about the Biało-Czerwoni movement she was just creating. More than five thousand applications. People who wanted to act. Who no longer wanted to just watch. Who believed that Poland could be different – stronger, more aware, prouder. Maybe that was the answer to the Myrotvorets list. Not fear, not retreat. Organisation. Solidarity. Strength.

In the kitchen she put water on for tea. Through the window the sun was rising – the same as yesterday. The world hadn't stopped. Neither could she. She was on a list, but she was still alive. She had a voice, and she was going to use it.

When she sat down to record the next vlog, her hands were steady. She turned on the camera and said: "Dear friends, I'm on the Myrotvorets list. But that doesn't mean I'll stop talking about Wołyń. It means I'll talk about it even louder."

She didn't know what tomorrow would bring. Whether someone would knock on her door in the middle of the night. Whether someone would start following her. Whether her children would be safe. But she knew one thing – what she was doing mattered. Because if not her, then who? If not now, then when?

She closed her eyes and remembered the monument in Domostawa. The village where a monument to the victims stands. A simple stone that says more than a thousand words. Because if a nation remembers its victims, it means their suffering was not in vain. It means they are still among us.

Ewa opened her eyes. She turned on the camera and began to speak.

Somewhere in Kyiv, in the office of the Ukrainian services, someone had just noted her name in the system. But she no longer thought about that. She thought about Wołyń. About the children who never grew up. About the mothers who never saw their daughters again. About the father whose daughter had written her an email.

About Poland, which may not yet be lost.

**THE END**
