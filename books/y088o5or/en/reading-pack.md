## Text

**HOW TO BREW TEA**

When Patryk first saw the double Turkish teapot, he thought it was some kind of mistake. Two teapots, one on top of the other? One without a lid, because the bottom of the other covers it? That made no sense. But then he watched his Turkish friend Furkan pour water into the bottom one, put tea leaves in the top one, set the whole thing on the stove and say: "It'll be ready in fifteen minutes."

Patryk was a Pole, raised on tea from a bag – you put it in a mug, pour boiling water over it, wait three minutes and it's done. Simple. Fast. No fuss. But when he saw how Furkan prepared tea, he felt as if he were discovering a completely new world.

---

In Turkey tea is not just a drink. It's a ritual, a daily practice, a way of life. Turks drink more of it than anyone in the world – on average five glasses a day, and some as many as ten. In every café, in every shop, in every home – someone is always brewing tea. And always in a special pot called a _çaydanlık_.

Furkan opened the cupboard and took out the pot. It was brass, shiny, with a long handle and a distinctive shape that immediately revealed where it came from.

– This is a çaydanlık – he said. – The bottom part is for water, the top for tea. The water boils, the steam opens the leaves in the top pot, and then we pour boiling water over them. Then we put it back on the fire to steep. Simple.

Patryk looked at the pot with a mixture of admiration and disbelief. Simple? That sounded like complicated alchemy.

Furkan put four spoonfuls of tea into the top pot. The leaves were small, dark, smelling of earth and something sweet.

– This is tea from the Black Sea region – he said. – Kara Deniz. The best in Turkey. They say the closer to the sea, the better.

Patryk nodded, but didn't quite understand. For him tea was tea – leaves, water, done. But Furkan talked about it like wine, about terroir, about a flavour that changes depending on whether it rained on the day of the harvest or the sun was shining.

---

When the water in the bottom pot began to boil, Furkan took off the top one, poured boiling water into it and put it back on top.

– Now we wait – he said. – The tea has to steep. You have to wait until the leaves sink to the bottom. Then it's ready.

Patryk watched the small, dark pieces float in the water, and then slowly, as if tired, begin to sink. It took several minutes. An aroma hung in the air – intense, earthy, a little sweet.

– You can add sugar – said Furkan. – Or not. Many Turks drink tea without sugar. But there are also those who put in two spoonfuls. And some put a sugar cube on their tongue and drink through it.

– Through the sugar? – asked Patryk.

– Yes. Traditionally. You put the cube on your tongue and then drink hot tea. The sugar slowly dissolves. But I prefer to put it in the glass.

Furkan poured the tea into a small, tulip-shaped glass – _ince belli_, as the Turks called it. The shape resembled a woman's waist, and it was also practical: at the bottom the tea stayed hot, while at the top it cooled enough to drink.

– Drink – he said, handing Patryk the glass.

Patryk took it carefully. It was hot. He raised it to his lips and took a sip.

The aroma exploded in his mouth. The tea was strong, but not bitter. Sweet, but not too much. It had something in it that he couldn't name – maybe fruity notes, maybe something smoky. It was different from anything he had drunk before.

– Heaven – he said.

Furkan smiled.

– That's why Turks drink it every day. It's not just a drink. It's a way to slow down. To stop. To be with someone.

---

Patryk returned to Poland and tried to recreate that taste. He bought Turkish tea, a double teapot, and even tulip glasses. He tried brewing it – once, twice, ten times. But something was always wrong. The tea was too weak or too bitter. The leaves wouldn't sink.

In the end he called Furkan.

– What am I doing wrong? – he asked.

– Too little tea – answered Furkan. – And you steep it too briefly. Turks don't skimp on the leaves. And they wait until everything sinks. Don't rush.

Patryk tried again. This time he put in five spoonfuls. He waited longer. And then he felt that taste.

He understood that tea isn't just leaves and water. It's patience. It's ritual. It's a way of being here and now, instead of constantly rushing somewhere else.

---

A few years later Patryk went to Japan. There he saw something completely different – the matcha tea ceremony. The mistress in a kimono entered a small, modest room. A rolled mat lay on the floor, a scroll with calligraphy hung on the wall, and a single flower stood in a vase.

Patryk watched as the mistress cleaned the vessels in front of the guests – not because they were dirty, but to show respect. As she took water from the iron kettle, slowly, so it could cool. As she put the green powder into a bowl, poured water over it, and then whisked it in a zigzag motion until foam appeared.

– This isn't just tea – said the mistress, when she handed him the bowl. – It's harmony. Respect. Purity. Calm.

Patryk took the bowl in both hands. It was warm. He bent down and took a sip. The taste was intense, umami, green. Completely different from Turkish tea, but equally deep.

He understood that tea around the world is as varied as the people who drink it. In Turkey they drink it hot, strong and sweet, in small glasses, on every occasion. In Japan they drink it in silence, in concentration, treating it like meditation. In Poland they drink it with lemon and sugar, in large mugs, in the morning, to wake up.

Everyone has their own way. And every way is good.

---

Patryk went home and brewed himself some tea – Turkish this time. He poured water into the bottom pot, put the leaves into the top one. He waited for the water to boil, poured it over the leaves, put it back on the stove and waited again. He watched the leaves sink to the bottom.

Then he poured the tea into a glass, added a spoonful of sugar and sat down by the window. He watched the setting sun and thought that tea is not just a drink. It's a bridge between people, between cultures, between moments.

He took a sip. It was good.

---

And in Turkey, at that same moment, someone else was brewing tea in exactly the same way. And someone in Japan was whisking matcha. And someone in Poland was steeping black tea in a mug.

They were all doing it differently. And they were all doing it right.

Because tea has no single recipe. It has as many recipes as there are people who drink it.
