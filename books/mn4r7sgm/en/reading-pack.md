## Text

**The Legend of the Wawel Dragon**

Long, long ago, when the Vistula flowed even wider than it does today, and Kraków was just spreading out at the foot of Wawel Hill, there lived King Krak. He was a good and just ruler, with a long, grey beard and eyes that saw more than most people's. The city's residents loved him, because he cared for them as if they were his own children. And the children — there were plenty of them in Kraków — ran through the narrow streets, peeked into craftsmen's workshops, and listened to the tales of old merchants who arrived from distant lands.

The city teemed with life. In the market square, traders praised their wares, washerwomen sang over their tubs of soapy water, and cobblers in their workshops sewed shoes for townsfolk and courtiers with bowed heads. Above it all towered the castle — proud, made of stone, with towers reaching for the sky. There, King Krak sat every day in the throne room to settle disputes and hear his subjects' requests. In the evenings he strolled through the courtyard, watched the sunset over the Vistula, and thought about how much he loved his city.

And then one day, everything changed.

It was a warm summer afternoon. The king stood on the castle wall, gazing at the sky, when suddenly he spotted a dark shape in the distance. With every moment it grew bigger. The king narrowed his eyes. A bird? No. No bird could be that enormous.

"Radzimir!" he called to his advisor, who had just arrived with books tucked under his arm. "Have you ever seen a creature of such size in your books?"

Radzimir raised his eyes. And froze.

At that moment the shape took the form of a reptile — mighty, covered in scales, with a fire-breathing maw. The dragon flew over the castle, tracing a circle in the sky like a vulture over its prey. Its wings rustled, and a flame burst from its mouth that briefly blinded the king and his courtiers.

Panic gripped the castle. People fled in terror, screaming and praying. The king watched the dragon fly over Kraków's buildings, knocking off roof tiles and setting houses on fire. A great bell from one of the church towers crashed onto the cobblestones, clanging as it rolled through the streets. The dragon landed in the middle of the market square and began to sniff around — its enormous eyes peered into the windows of the tenement houses, and its tail knocked over stalls that, just a moment earlier, had been full of goods.

People vanished through the gates. The market square emptied. Only one child — a small boy named Skuba, who had accidentally peeked out of the workshop of his father, a cobbler — watched the beast with a mixture of fear and curiosity.

"We'll meet again, you monster!" he shouted after the departing dragon, though his voice was drowned out by the monster's roar.

From that day on, Kraków was never the same city. The dragon made its home in a cave at the foot of Wawel, and its appetite was insatiable. Every day it demanded fresh offerings — first sheep, then cows, and when those began to run out, it reached for people. Villagers were afraid to leave their homes, and Kraków's merchants closed their shops, because no one wanted to risk an encounter with the beast.

King Krak despaired. He watched his beautiful city fall into ruin, watched fear poison the hearts of his subjects. He summoned knights from across the kingdom, promising a sack of gold to anyone who slew the dragon. Bold men in gleaming armour came — brave and strong, but the dragon proved mightier. Swords dulled against its scales, and arrows bounced off its hide like peas off a wall. The knights fled screaming, and the dragon laughed in its lair, aware of its own strength.

Soon despondency gripped the whole kingdom. People stopped believing the dragon could be defeated. Even the king lost hope. He sat in the throne room with his head bowed, thinking about how much more he could lose.

It was then that Skuba arrived at the castle.

The young cobbler, who had first seen the dragon in the market square, had not slept peacefully since. He thought about the monster every night. But instead of fear, he felt something else — anger that the beast could so freely terrorize his city. And he decided to do something about it.

"Let me see the king," he told the guards, standing before the castle gate. "I know how to get rid of the monster."

The guards burst out laughing.

"You? Boy, none of the knights armed to the teeth managed it! What will you do, stab it with a needle and thread?" mocked one of them.

But Skuba would not be discouraged. He insisted until his voice was heard by Radzimir, the king's advisor. Though he was not a knight, he was a wise man — and he knew that sometimes cunning and courage matter more than a sword.

"Let's hear what he has to say," Radzimir decided. "We have nothing to lose."

And he led Skuba into the throne room.

When the king saw the boy, he was surprised. He had expected a knight in armour, not a cobbler in an apron.

"Come closer, my son," said the king in a kind voice. "Tell me your secret."

Skuba stepped forward and began to speak. The plan was simple: by trickery, not force. Instead of facing the dragon in direct combat, he wanted to exploit its gluttony and its foolishness.

"Your Majesty," he said. "The dragon is mighty, but also greedy. If I offer it something it cannot refuse, it will bring about its own downfall."

The king's face brightened as he listened. In the end the boy explained his idea, and the ruler clapped his hands.

"If you succeed, you shall receive a sack of gold!" promised the king.

"It is not gold I care about, my lord king," replied Skuba. "I dream of a knight's life. Of adventures. Of helping good people."

The king smiled. He knew this was an exceptional boy.

The next morning at dawn Skuba set to work. He bought the fattest ram he could find in the market square. Then, in his workshop, he filled its insides with sulphur and tar. He carefully sewed the hide shut so that no one — especially the dragon — could suspect the trick.

When dusk fell, Skuba headed towards the Dragon's Den. His heart pounded like a hammer, but he did not turn back. He crept quietly up to the cave opening, from which loud snoring came. He felt the dragon's hot breath and saw a flash of fire in the darkness.

Quickly he placed the ram before the entrance and hid in the bushes by the Vistula.

"Now we wait," he whispered to himself.

After a few minutes the cave grew noisy. The dragon woke up, moved its nose through the air and... saw the ram. Without thinking much, it swallowed it whole in a single snap of its jaws.

For a moment nothing happened. Then the dragon began to shake its head strangely. Something inside it had begun to burn. The sulphur and tar that had reached its stomach heated up until they glowed red. The reptile felt an unquenchable thirst.

"Fire!" roared the dragon. "Fire! It burns! It burns!"

And it hurled itself towards the Vistula. It drank. And drank. And drank. The water steamed in its mouth, but it did not stop. It drank until its belly began to swell. The roar of the river mixed with its own roaring and slobbering, and Skuba watched from the bushes, holding his breath.

The dragon was mighty, but foolish. It drank so long that in one moment — with a bang that shook all of Kraków — it burst.

The waters of the Vistula parted for a moment, then closed over the remains of the beast.

And silence. The dragon was no more.

Shouts of joy rang out from the castle walls. King Krak had seen everything, and he clapped and laughed as in the good old days. He sent messengers across the city — the dragon is dead, Kraków is free!

People poured into the streets. They celebrated for three days and three nights. Dancers in the market square, music, feasts. And at the centre of it all was Skuba — a simple cobbler who had defeated the beast with cunning, not a sword.

The next day a solemn knighting ceremony took place. King Krak placed a belt on his shoulders, handed him a golden spur, and announced that from then on he would bear the title "Dragon Bane". Skuba bowed his head, but his eyes showed something more than pride — joy at having saved the city he loved.

And then something happened that no one expected. In the castle courtyard appeared Princess Wanda — the king's daughter, known for her beauty and kind heart. A crown gleamed in her hand, and a smile graced her face.

"I declare a great wedding!" announced the king.

Skuba looked at the princess. And then he realized that this was only the beginning of his adventures.

Kraków breathed a sigh of relief. In memory of those events, a stone monument was erected at the entrance to the Dragon's Den, and the children who ran through the streets now pretended to be dragons and brave cobblers.

And so the legend of the Wawel Dragon survived the centuries. Passed from mouth to mouth, from father to son, from mother to daughter, it told of how courage and cunning can overcome the greatest beasts, and a true hero does not always wear armour — sometimes a good heart, skilful hands and a head full of ideas are enough.

If you ever visit Kraków, stop by Wawel Hill. Listen to the murmur of the Vistula. Perhaps you will hear a quiet rumbling underground — the echo of old times, when a dragon guarded its cave and a single cobbler changed the fate of an entire kingdom.

Because in a world full of magic, every legend still lives on. In the stories that ignite the imagination. And in those that teach that good and courage always prevail.
