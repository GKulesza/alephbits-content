## Text

**Controlled Hallucination**

**A short story**


When she opened her eyes, she didn't know where she was. A white ceiling, neon light, the smell of disinfectant. A hospital. Again.

The last thing she remembered was the fall. The stairs in the university library, slippery marble, books scattered like a deck of cards. And then darkness.

She tried to lift her hand, but she couldn't. Not because she was paralysed – she simply didn't have a hand. No, it was something else. The hand was there, but she didn't feel it belonged to her. As if someone had attached a foreign limb to her shoulder.

– Hello? – she heard her own voice, but it sounded distant, as if someone were speaking through a thick wall. – Is anyone here?

The door opened with a quiet creak. In the doorway stood a man in a white coat. He was about fifty, grey-haired, with tired eyes. In his hand he held a tablet.

– Ms Anna? – he asked. – My name is Dr Marek. Please don't move. You've had a serious head injury.

– Where am I?

– University Hospital. Neurology ward. You've been here for three days.

She tried to sit up, but something held her back. Not physically – mentally. As if someone in her head had said: _don't get up, that's not your body_.

– Something is wrong – she whispered.

The doctor came closer. He sat down on a chair beside the bed.

– I know. Please listen. You hit your head. The tests show... certain irregularities in the prefrontal cortex. Nothing dangerous, but... you may experience unusual sensations.

– What kind?

He hesitated.

– Some patients after this type of injury say they feel as if they were living in a simulation. That the world around them is... unreal. That their own body doesn't belong to them.

– That's exactly how I feel – she said quietly. – This is not my hand.

The doctor nodded.

– I know it. It's called _somatoparaphrenic disorientation_. The brain tries to interpret damaged signals and creates... an alternative version of reality.

– Will it pass?

– Probably. The brain is plastic. But you can help it. I'd like to propose something unusual.

He raised the tablet.

– I research consciousness. I cooperate with the philosophy department. We have a method that helps patients regain a sense of self. It's a kind of interview – we call it _micro-phenomenology_. Instead of examining your brain, we want to examine your _experience_.

– How?

– We'll talk about what you feel. At every moment. It's not about what you think you feel – but about the very structure of that feeling. As if we were zooming in to see individual pixels.

**_Day one_**

– Please describe what you feel right now – said the doctor.

She sat on the bed, propped up on pillows. Outside the window it was grey, it was raining.

– I feel... strangeness – she answered. – It's not a normal feeling. It's as if someone had switched on a different program in my head.

– What kind of program?

– Hmm... as if all my senses were delayed. When I look at your hand, I see it, but I _feel_ that I saw it a second earlier, before it reached me. Like watching a badly dubbed film, where the sound doesn't match the picture.

– That's fascinating – said the doctor, noting something on his tablet. – You're talking about a _desynchronisation_ of perception. Please try to focus on something simple. For example, on the fact that you're breathing.

She closed her eyes.

– I feel the air coming in... but it's not _my_ air. It's as if someone were breathing through me. I only observe.

– Is there any image that accompanies it? Any picture?

– Yes. I see myself from above. As if I were above myself. Small, hiding in the corner of the room, and that body on the bed is someone else.

– Who is that someone?

She hesitated.

– I don't know. Maybe... someone pretending to be me.

The doctor put down the tablet.

– Anna, what you're describing is a classic case of _damage to the connections between the areas responsible for integrating the senses_. Your brain can't reconcile information from different sources. That's why it creates separate "selves" – one that observes, and one that is observed.

– So I'm two people?

– No. You're one person who _experiences_ herself in an unusual way. Your consciousness – what we call the _self_ – is not a thing. It's a process. And that process has been temporarily disrupted.

She was silent for a long moment.

– And if... if this isn't a disruption? – she asked at last. – If what I feel now is the truth, and what I felt before was an illusion?

The doctor looked at her with curiosity.

– What do you mean?

– Exactly that – what does "I" mean? Who am I when I say "I"? Is it my body? My thoughts? My memories? Because now I see that all of this can be separated. My thoughts are like... like a radio playing in the background. I can turn it off. But then there's no "me". There's only silence. And in that silence... there is no "I" at all.

– So what is in that silence?

– Nothing. And that's terrifying.

**_Day three_**

A colleague from work came to see her. Marta, a psychologist, who had shared the office with her for five years.

– You look better – said Marta, sitting down beside the bed. – Less pale.

– I don't look – Anna replied. – I'm just a collection of atoms that happened to arrange themselves into the shape of a human. What you see is not me.

Marta laughed nervously.

– You're joking, right?

– No. Listen, since I've been here, I've been thinking about it all the time. What am I? Am I anything more than electrochemical processes in my brain? Because if not – if everything I feel is just a simulation created by neurons – then who am I really?

Marta leaned towards her.

– You're Ania. My friend. The person who loves reading books, who cries at films, who can listen for hours while someone talks about their problems. I don't know if that's "something more" – but that's you.

– And if all of it is just... a _controlled hallucination_? – Anna said quietly. – If my brain creates an inner model of the world, and I live in that model, not in reality? If what I call "myself" is just a prediction my mind made based on past experiences?

Marta looked at her with concern.

– Does the doctor know you think that way?

– He knows. He talked about it. He said it's normal with this type of injury. But that doesn't mean it's not true.

For a moment they were both silent.

– Listen – Marta said at last. – Maybe you're right. Maybe everything is a simulation. Maybe we're just holograms in someone's mind. But you know what? Even if that's true – I _feel_ that I'm here. I feel that I'm breathing, that I hear the rain outside the window, that I'm worried about you. Maybe it's an illusion, but this illusion is _mine_. And no one can take it away from me.

Anna looked at her hands. They seemed foreign, as if they belonged to someone else.

– And if... if this is exactly what consciousness is? – she whispered. – If consciousness is the ability to experience an illusion as reality? If what we call "I" is just the ability to tell a story about ourselves?

– I think that's a very good question – Marta replied. – But I don't know if it can be answered in a hospital room. Maybe you need to go outside. Watch the rain fall. Touch the wet grass. Then you'll feel that what you feel is real – whether it's a simulation or not.

**_Day five_**

Dr Marek came with news.

– The tests show improvement – he said. – The damage is smaller than I thought. In a week you'll be able to go home.

Anna nodded. For three days she'd had no more episodes of disorientation. The sense of her body had returned. But the questions remained.

– Doctor, may I ask you a question?

– Of course.

– Is it possible that consciousness – what we call the "self" – is really just a side effect? That the brain evolved to process information, and consciousness appeared by chance, as a by-product of complexity?

The doctor smiled.

– Philosophers have been asking that question for thousands of years. And there's still no good answer. But we do know one thing: consciousness isn't needed for intelligence. You can have a system that recognises images, solves problems, even holds conversations – and it doesn't have to be conscious. Intelligence is one thing. Consciousness, the capacity to feel, is something else.

– So AI can be intelligent but not conscious?

– Exactly. And that's one of the reasons we don't know whether artificial systems can suffer. We don't know what conditions are needed for subjective experience to arise.

Anna looked at her hands.

– And if one day we build a system that is conscious? And it suffers? Will we bear responsibility?

– That's one of the most important moral questions of our time – the doctor answered. – And that's why some scientists say we should slow down the development of AI until we understand what consciousness is. Because if we build a system capable of suffering before we manage to protect it, we'll make an enormous mistake.

Anna nodded.

– And the same applies to animals, right? Those that are conscious, but we can't prove it?

– Yes. Octopuses, crows, dogs – we know they're intelligent. We don't know to what extent they feel. But we know they can suffer. And that's enough to treat them differently.

– So moral progress is about expanding the circle of beings we recognise as capable of feeling?

– Exactly.

**_Epilogue_**

She went home a week later. The first thing she did was step out onto the balcony. It was raining. She placed her hand on the wet railing. The sensation was real – cool, damp, tangible.

And then she understood.

Maybe consciousness is only an illusion. Maybe the "self" is only a story the brain creates to make sense of the stream of experience. Maybe there is no "centre", no "ghost in the machine".

But when she touched the rain, she _felt_ that it was she who was touching. When she heard the hum of the city, she _knew_ it was she who was hearing. Maybe it was an illusion. But it was _her_ illusion. And that was the only thing that mattered.

She went back into the flat, sat down at her desk and opened her laptop. For a moment she looked at the empty screen. Then she began to write. Not about herself. About something else.

_"Consciousness is the greatest problem of modern science. Not because it's hard to define – but because in defining it, we define ourselves. And that is the hardest question of all: who are we?"_

She stopped.

She knew she wouldn't find the answer. No one would. But maybe the question matters more than the answer.

Outside the window it was raining. And she kept writing.


**THE END**
