## Text

**THE COMPUTER AND THE HAMSTER**

## I. THE BEGINNING

Marcin woke up at 5:47, before the alarm went off. For a moment he lay in the dark, listening to his own heartbeat and the hum of the radiator. Outside the window Warsaw was just waking up – the first buses, a distant dog barking, raindrops hitting the window pane.

He knew that today he had to finish the presentation. Deadline: 12:00. He had over six hours for it. That should be enough.

He got up, had some coffee and sat down in front of the computer. He opened the file. For the first ten minutes he stared at the empty screen, hearing four different voices in his head at the same time: the one that said this was important; the one that said it was impossible; the one that reminded him about the groceries; and the one humming a song from the radio that he hated.

— Pull yourself together — he said to himself. — You have to start.

But his brain didn't work that way. It worked like a plane that needs a runway to take off. Without the right momentum – or without adrenaline – it won't move.

He looked at the clock. 6:23.

He closed his eyes. A memory from childhood appeared in his head – a teacher talking to his mother: _"Marcin is talented, but lazy. If only he wanted to..."_

_"If only he wanted to"_ – he repeated to himself all his life.

He opened his eyes. 6:45.

He had to do something. He reached for his phone and opened the first game that came to hand. An hour later he was on level five, and the presentation was still empty.

— This isn't really happening — he whispered. — This isn't really happening.

---

## II. THE DEVELOPMENT

7:59. Marcin ran into the office with a cup of coffee that he spilled on the way. He had a stain on his white shirt, but he had no time to worry about that. The presentation was supposed to be ready in four hours, and he only had the title and three sentences of introduction.

— Did something happen? — asked a colleague from the desk next to him.

— Nothing — he replied, smiling artificially. — Everything's under control.

He sat down at the computer. For a moment he looked at the empty screen. And then, as if by magic, something happened. His brain switched into hyperfocus mode. For the next three hours Marcin didn't exist. He didn't feel hunger, didn't hear sounds, didn't see people. Only the presentation existed. Slides appeared one after another, perfectly designed, logical, persuasive. His fingers flew over the keyboard as if they knew on their own what to do.

At 11:47 he finished. For a moment he looked at the screen, unable to believe he had done it. Thirty-two slides. Graphics, data, analyses. Everything ready.

— Wow — said the colleague, peering over his shoulder. — That's amazing. How did you do it in such a short time?

— I just... — Marcin fell silent. He didn't know what to say. He didn't know how to explain that for three hours he had been in a state other people reach maybe once a week, and he reached every day, but he paid double the price for it.

The rest of the day was different. The energy that had driven him through those three hours was gone. He felt like a balloon that had been deflated. Every task – even answering an email – required ten times more effort than usual.

At 4:00 his manager asked him for a talk.

— Great presentation — she said. — Really great. But I have one small remark for you.

Marcin felt something tighten in his chest.

— I don't quite understand where the data in slide twelve came from. Could you justify it better next time?

— Of course — he replied, trying to make his voice sound normal. — Sure. I'll fix it.

He left the office and only in the bathroom did he allow himself to breathe. He felt as if someone had poured boiling water over him. He knew it was only a remark. A small, constructive remark. But in his head he heard other words: _"You're hopeless. You did something wrong again. Again you're not good enough."_

He remembered what his psychiatrist had once said: _"Rejection Sensitive Dysphoria. It's not your fault. Your brain reacts to criticism as if it were a threat to your life."_

But that didn't help. It hurt just the same.

---

## III. THE TURNING POINT

He came home at 8:00 in the evening. His wife was waiting with dinner – pasta with tomato sauce, his favourite. He sat down at the table and for a moment looked at the plate, unable to reach for the fork.

— Did something happen? — she asked.

— Nothing. I just... — he stopped. In his head appeared the memory from today's meeting, when the manager talked about that small correction. And suddenly he understood something he hadn't understood for years.

— I remembered something I once heard on a podcast — he said quietly. — The guy said that attention is like a muscle. That you have to train it. And we... all of us who are constantly scrolling, who can't focus, who start ten things and finish none... we simply forgot that this muscle has to be exercised.

His wife looked at him with surprise.

— And now what?

— I don't know. — Marcin picked up the fork. — But I think I have to stop looking for magic solutions. And start with small things.

The next day Marcin woke up at 5:47. This time he didn't reach for his phone. Instead he sat down at the desk with a notebook and a pen. At the top of the page he wrote:

_"What do I want to do today?"_

And below, in small letters:

_"1. Finish the presentation. (Start with the first slide, not with thinking about the whole thing.)"_

For the first five minutes he looked at that page. Then, instead of reaching for his phone, he took the pen and wrote:

_"2. Don't open the browser until I've done the first three slides."_

He smiled. It was silly. Childish. But it worked.

---

## IV. THE ENDING

A year passed. Marcin stopped looking for magic solutions. He stopped buying more planners, apps, productivity courses. He learned one thing:

_"Getting down to work looks like this: well, it just has to be done."_

He didn't wait for motivation. He didn't wait for the perfect moment. He didn't wait for his brain to switch into hyperfocus mode. He simply sat down and started with small things. Five minutes. Then ten. Then twenty.

He also began to notice that his biggest enemy wasn't a lack of time, but the fact that in every free moment he reached for his phone. He learned to take breaks that weren't absorbing. Instead of scrolling, he stood up, stretched, looked out the window. Sometimes he simply closed his eyes for five minutes.

One day, after a workshop, a woman with tearful eyes came up to him.

— I heard you today — she said. — About how you don't have to look for magic solutions. That it's enough to start with small things. And... — she stopped. — All my life I thought I was lazy. That I wasn't trying hard enough. And now I know... it wasn't my fault. I just didn't have the tools.

Marcin took her hand.

— I know. And you're not alone.

He left the room and looked at the sky. Somewhere in the distance, a plane cut through the clouds. And for the first time in years Marcin didn't feel that it was a metaphor for his life.

He felt that it was a promise.

---

## V. THE EPILOGUE

A few months later Marcin received a message from the woman from the workshop:

_"I stopped looking for magic solutions. I started with small things. And you know what? It works. Thank you."_

Marcin read the message three times. Then he put the phone in his pocket and went back to work. Ahead of him was another project, another deadline, another chance to show what he could do.

But this time he didn't feel pressure. This time he felt only determination.

Because he knew he didn't have to wait for hyperfocus. He could start with small things. And what seems impossible at first, over the course of a year becomes a habit.

He sat down at the desk. He opened the file. And he started.

Not right from the end. Not from a vision of the whole project. Simply from the first line.

And that was enough.

---

**THE END**
