**THE GODDESS AND THE NUMBERS**

A slate lay on the boy's knees, and he stared at it as if it were about to reveal the greatest secret of the world. It was late; everyone in the house was asleep, and he could not fall asleep. In his hand he held a stylus; beside him lay a thick, leather-bound book. Its pages held thousands of formulas, one theorem after another, with no explanation, no proofs.

– It has to be possible – he whispered to himself. – It must be.

He began to write. The stylus scratched across the slate. Numbers, letters, symbols that for others would have been a dry record were, for him, a language he had understood since birth. All his life he had felt that numbers are more than a tool for counting. They are alive. They have their own stories, their own personalities. When he looked at them, he saw the faces of friends he had known forever.

And then, for the first time, he felt that he was not alone.

From behind his back came a whisper, soft as leaves rustling in the wind. He turned around, but there was no one behind him. Only a shadow that seemed to move to its own rhythm.

– Look deeper – he heard. – To where the numbers meet the light.

The boy returned to the slate. His hand began to write on its own, as if someone were guiding it. Formulas that had seemed unclear before suddenly made sense. He saw them clearly, as if they were written across the sky.

– Who are you? – he asked into the darkness.

– I am what you see when you look beyond the numbers. I am what was always there, before anything began.

The boy did not know whether it was a dream or a revelation. He only knew that from that moment nothing would ever be the same.

---
Twenty years later, in the chilly study of Trinity College, Professor Godfrey Harold Hardy looked at the letter he held in his hand. He was tired. It was one more letter from someone who considered himself a genius and was, in reality, simply a madman. But something about this letter unsettled him.

The pages were densely covered with formulas. Hundreds of theorems, one after another, without a single proof. Some seemed familiar; others were so strange that he could not understand them. And yet there was something in them — an inner logic that would not let him put the letter down.

– Littlewood! – he called through the door. – Come here. You have to see this.

John Edensor Littlewood, his closest collaborator, entered the study. For a moment they looked at the letter in silence.

– That's impossible – Littlewood said at last. – No one could reach something like this on his own.

– And yet – replied Hardy. – Keep reading. The author claims that a goddess revealed these formulas to him in a dream.

Littlewood looked at him in disbelief.

– A goddess?

– Yes. And you know what? I think he is telling the truth.

Ramanujan stood on the deck of a ship, watching the shores of India disappear into the distance. Behind him lay everything he knew — his mother, his wife, the temples, the rituals that had filled his life since the cradle. Ahead of him lay the unknown. England, a foreign language, foreign food, a foreign world. And people who were to judge whether what he carried in his head was worth anything.

He knew that many Brahmins would condemn him for what he was doing. Crossing the ocean was a sin that could cast him out of his community forever. But his mother had told him that that night she had heard in a dream the voice of the goddess Namagiri of Namakkal. The goddess had commanded him to go.

– Do not stand between a son and his destiny – she had heard in her dream.

And now Ramanujan was sailing across the sea they called the black water, carrying three thick notebooks full of formulas that — he believed — were a gift from the goddess.

The ship rocked on the waves, and he thought about what Hardy had written to him in one of the letters: "Your theorems must be true. If they were false, no one would have the imagination to invent them."

Was it possible that a stranger who had never seen India understood him better than anyone in his own country? Ramanujan did not know the answer. He only knew that he had to try.

---
In Cambridge, two men met whom more than an ocean divided. Hardy was a rationalist who believed only in what could be proven. Ramanujan was a mystic who believed that numbers are the language of God. Hardy demanded proofs. Ramanujan saw the truth at once, without any derivation.

– How do you know that this formula is true? – Hardy asked once again, when Ramanujan showed him another theorem.

– It's obvious – Ramanujan replied. – Look at it. Do you see it?

Hardy looked. He did not see it. And yet he knew that the Hindu was right.

– This is not science – he said at last. – This is... something else.

Ramanujan smiled.

– This is mathematics – he replied. – Real mathematics. The kind that needs no proofs, because it is visible to those who know how to look.

Hardy did not know what to say. All his life he had believed that mathematics is an exact science, based on logic and reasoning. And now he stood before a man who overturned everything he believed in with a single glance at a slate.

– If this is true – he said at last – then mathematics is more than a science. It is something... sacred.

Ramanujan nodded.

– I have always thought so.

The illness came slowly, like a shadow that settles on the body and refuses to leave. Ramanujan tried to fight, but every day he felt weaker. The English climate was deadly for him — the cold, the damp, the foreign food that he had to prepare himself on a spirit stove at three in the morning, after a whole night of work on his formulas.

When he was admitted to the sanatorium in Matlock, the doctors forbade him to work. They said that mental exertion was making his condition worse. And he knew that was not true. It was mathematics that kept him alive. Without it, he would have had no reason to get up in the morning.

He lay in an unheated room, wrapped in a coat, with a slate on his knees. Outside it was raining. Inside it was cold. And he wrote.

– Mr Ramanujan – said the nurse, entering the room. – You must rest.

– I am resting – he replied without looking up. – For me, this is rest.

The nurse sighed and left. She knew there was no point in arguing. That man seemed not to be of this world. He wrote while he had the strength, and even when he no longer had it, he still wrote.

At night, when silence fell over the sanatorium, Ramanujan heard a voice. Quiet, clear, as if it came from inside his own head.

– Not yet – the voice said. – You still have something to do.

Ramanujan opened his eyes and stared at the ceiling. He knew that it was not his imagination. It was the goddess. The same one who had dictated formulas to him when he was a child in Kumbakonam.

– What should I do? – he asked into the darkness.

– Write – the voice answered. – Write while you can. Your words will survive.

And he wrote. Even when he could no longer sit up, when he lay propped against the pillows, his hand did not stop. His wife kept placing clean sheets under him, one after another, and he filled them with formulas that came to him from another world.

He was skin and bone – Janaki would say later. But his hand was still writing. Right up to the very end.

---
On 26 April 1920, at dawn, Srinivasa Ramanujan closed his eyes for the last time. He was 32 years old. He left behind three notebooks, about 3,900 theorems, and a question that for the next decades would torment mathematicians all over the world.

How did he know? How was it possible that a poor self-taught man from India, with no formal education, no access to libraries, had learned so much that his formulas work today in the physics of black holes?

Science could not provide an answer. Or perhaps it simply did not want to. Because the answer was too simple, too inconvenient, too far beyond what science could accept.

Ramanujan repeated all his life: "An equation has no meaning for me unless it expresses a thought of God."

And perhaps that was exactly the point. Perhaps mathematics is not only a science. Perhaps it is something more — a language in which God wrote the universe, waiting for those who can read it.

And he, Ramanujan, was one of them. Saved by the goddess, guided by her all his life, right up to the very end.

---
Today, in the Wren Library at Trinity College, sheets from the last year of his life lie under glass. The same ones that for 56 years were believed lost, that returned to the world thanks to chance and the persistence of one man.

There are formulas there that are still waiting to be discovered. Prophecies written by the hand of a dying man, which were to come true only years, decades, centuries later.

Hardy wrote in the obituary that he would compare him only with Euler and Jacobi, with the greatest who had ever lived. And he admitted that his acquaintance with Ramanujan was the only romantic episode of his life.

And he himself, Ramanujan, departed in silence, leaving behind a question that still remains unanswered. A question about the source of his genius, about the nature of his visions, about what lies on the other side of numbers.

Perhaps one day someone will find the answer. Or perhaps not. Perhaps some mysteries are simply too great to be fathomed.

Perhaps some truths can only be accepted — just as Ramanujan accepted the formulas that came to him in a dream.

With faith that they come from a place that cannot be proven. Only seen.

And written down, before it is too late.

**THE END**
