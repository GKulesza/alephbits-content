**Speech**

Marcin had always been afraid of the microphone. Not of the physical object – the metal grille on a stand that smelled of previous speakers' saliva. He was afraid of what the microphone does to silence. How it amplifies it. How it turns an ordinary pause between sentences into an abyss into which his whole confidence falls.

That's why when he stood in front of a room full of people – colleagues, managers, a few board members – and saw in front of him that small black transmitter clipped to his jacket lapel, he felt the familiar cramp in his stomach. He was to present his department's quarterly results. Ten slides, fifteen minutes. You'd think – nothing difficult.

– Thank you... thank you very much for letting me... for letting us meet today, so that...

He froze. Three seconds of silence that lasted a minute for him. Someone in the audience coughed. Someone else glanced at their phone. Marcin felt his hands growing damp and his breathing going shallow. The words he had prepared had vanished somewhere.

– Sorry – he said. – I'll... I'll get back to the point in a moment.

He didn't. The rest of the presentation was torture. The slides advanced, he read from his notes, avoiding eye contact with anyone. At the end the room clapped – out of duty, not appreciation. As he left the conference room, he knew something had to change. It wasn't about that one presentation. It was about all of them. About every meeting, every conversation with his boss, every speech during which he felt that his voice didn't belong to him.

His own voice. The one that at home, with his wife and children, could be warm, firm, funny. The one that among friends could tell a story so well that everyone wept with laughter. But the moment someone unfamiliar – a boss, a client, an examination board – looked at him, that voice vanished, replaced by a choked, uncertain whisper broken by nervous pauses.

Two weeks later he sat in a café with a notebook on his lap, watching an interview with Tomasz Kammel on his phone. He didn't believe in miracles. But he'd heard about this guy – a journalist, a speaker, someone who taught people to talk. If anyone could help him, maybe it was him.

---
When he walked into the recording studio where Kammel ran his trainings, he expected something grand. Instead, the room was small, soundproofed, with a few chairs and a camera on a tripod. Tomasz greeted him plainly – no stardom, no fake smile.

– What do you need this for? – he asked once they sat down.

Marcin hesitated.

– Because I can't speak – he said at last. – I mean... I can speak. But not when it counts. Not in front of people. Not when they're watching me.

Kammel nodded, as if he'd heard it hundreds of times. Then he leaned in slightly and said something Marcin remembered forever.

– You know what people are most afraid of when they step onto the stage? Not forgetting their lines. Not stumbling. They're afraid of being judged. They're afraid that someone will think: "What is he doing here? He shouldn't be here." And you know what? That thought – it's a thought only about yourself. And when you go out to people, you can't think about yourself. You have to think about them.

Marcin wrote it down in his notebook. Then he wrote down a few more things: _look into the listener's right eye – it works like an anchor, it gives a sense of contact without intimidating. Don't say "I'm nervous", say "I'm excited" – the biochemistry of the brain changes just from the naming. A pause is not an enemy, only a tool – thirty seconds of silence for you is an eternity, for the listeners it's a moment to breathe._

But the most important lesson came at the end, when they were already getting ready to leave.

– You have children, right? – asked Tomasz.

– Two. A son and a daughter.

– Then tell me about them. Not the way you talk about slides. Tell me the way you talk about them at home. As if you were at dinner, not on a stage.

Marcin looked at him uncertainly. Then he began to speak. About how his daughter rode her bike for the first time without training wheels. About how his son slept in a ski helmet for a week because he wanted to be ready for winter. About how his wife laughed at him when he came home from work pretending he wasn't tired.

When he finished, Tomasz was silent for a moment. Then he said:

– Now it was you speaking. Not that guy who's afraid of being judged. You. And that is your voice. The rest is just technique. Technique is a tool, not a goal. The goal is for you to stop pretending to be someone you're not. Because people don't want to listen to a perfect ideal. They want to listen to a human being.

---
Three months later Marcin stood in front of an even bigger room. This time it wasn't about quarterly results – it was the farewell speech for a director who was retiring, a man who had been his mentor for the past ten years. The room was full – not only of colleagues, but also of the director's family, his friends, old industry acquaintances.

When Marcin approached the microphone, he felt the familiar cramp. But this time he did something he had learned from Kammel. Instead of starting to speak right away, he took a breath. He looked at the audience – not all of it at once, but at one person in the front row. At the director's daughter, who was watching him with gratitude that it was him, not some stranger, who was saying goodbye to her father.

And then he understood. She hadn't come to judge him. She had come to hear something that mattered to her. He was only a tool – like the microphone, like the stand, like the words he had prepared. He wasn't there to be perfect. He was there to give something.

– When I first met Andrzej – he began – I mistook him for a security guard.

The room laughed. Marcin felt the tension in his shoulders ease.

– He stood by the door, in a jacket one size too big, and looked at me as if he wanted to check whether I had a pass. I didn't. But he let me in anyway.

He went on. He didn't read from a card. He didn't look at slides. He talked to people – to those specific people who had come to say goodbye to his boss. About how Andrzej taught him that a company isn't just results and goals. About how once, after a failed presentation, Andrzej told him over coffee: "You know why it didn't work? Because you spoke as if you were making excuses. And you have nothing to apologise for. You have something to say. So say it."

When he finished, silence fell. This time not a frightening silence, but a full one. Then applause broke out. But that wasn't what mattered. What mattered was that when the director's daughter came up to him afterwards and hugged him, she said:

– You spoke about Dad as if you really knew him.

And Marcin thought: _Because I did know him. And at last I could say it._

---
In the evening he came home. His wife was waiting for him with tea. The children were already asleep. He sat down at the kitchen table and for a moment simply said nothing, looking at his hands. They weren't trembling.

– So? – she asked.

– I think... – he began, but stopped. He looked at her and saw that she was smiling. Not because she expected an answer. Only because she was proud of him.

– I think I finally said what I wanted – he finished.

And then, for the first time in years, he didn't analyse every word. He simply drank his tea and told her everything – not as from a presentation, but as at dinner. Like a man who had found his voice. And he knew he would never lose it again.

**THE END**
