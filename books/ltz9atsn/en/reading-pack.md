## Text

**THE GRAVES**

## I. THE BEGINNING

Doctor Piotr Nowak stood in front of the building of the Central Archives of Modern Records and looked at his watch. 11:47. He had twenty minutes for a meeting that could change everything.

In his hand he held an envelope he had received a week earlier from an elderly man at a conference in Łomża. The man had approached him after the lecture and said only:

— You're looking for the truth about Jedwabne? Here you go. But be careful, because some people don't want you to find it.

The envelope contained copies of documents from 1945. Witness testimonies whose names had not appeared in any of Jan Tomasz Gross's studies. Accounts that said something different from the official narrative.

Piotr opened the door and went inside. He knew that what he was about to see could shatter everything he had believed for the last twenty years.

---
## II. THE DEVELOPMENT

The archive hall was cool, quiet, smelling of old paper and dust. Piotr sat down at the table and spread the documents out before him. For a moment he looked at them, unable to force himself to open them.

He remembered what his professor had told him at university: _"History is not propaganda. History is the search for truth, even if that truth is inconvenient."_

He took a deep breath and opened the first folder.

The documents were old, the handwriting illegible in places. But one thing was clear – the testimonies he found differed from those that had made it into the court files. The witnesses said that the Germans had been in Jedwabne on 10 July 1941. That they had given the order. That the Poles had not acted on their own initiative.

Piotr read it three times. Then he closed the folder and looked out the window. Rain was falling outside, and he felt something inside him cracking.

— What are you doing? — he heard behind his back.

He turned around. In the doorway stood an elderly man in glasses – the director of the archive.

— Professor Nowak, I didn't expect you here.

— I'm looking for the truth — Piotr answered.

— The truth? — the director smiled bitterly. — In this matter, the truth is like the Holy Grail. Everyone is looking for it, but nobody wants to find it.

— Why?

— Because the truth about Jedwabne is inconvenient for everyone. For the Jews, for the Poles, for the Germans, for the Russians. Everyone wants their own version. And we, historians, are only pawns in this game.

Piotr stayed silent. In his mind surfaced a memory from a few years earlier, when he had taken part in a conference about Jedwabne. Someone had told him then that there was no chance of the truth, because this case was too convenient for those who run public opinion.

— What should I do? — he asked quietly.

— What you're doing. Search. But be careful, because those who don't want you to find it are very powerful.

---
## III. THE TURNING POINT

A month later, Piotr was sitting in his office at the university. In front of him lay piles of documents, notes, articles. He tried to piece them together, but he was still missing one element.

The phone rang. An unknown number.

— Professor Nowak? — he heard a voice. — I have information for you. Please come to Łomża. We'll meet in a café on the square. Tomorrow at 5 PM.

— Who are you?

— Someone who knows the truth. And who wants you to know it too.

He hung up.

Piotr stood up and walked to the window. He looked at the city stretching out before him, and thought about all the people who, over the years, had searched for an answer to the question of Jedwabne. About those who had found it. About those who had died before they found it. About those who had stopped searching.

He took his coat and left.

---
## IV. THE ENDING

The café in Łomża was small, cosy, smelling of coffee and pastries. Piotr sat down at a table in the corner and waited. After a few minutes, a middle-aged man approached him – thin, with greying hair and eyes that had seen more than they wanted to remember.

— Professor — he said, sitting down. — My name is Marek. I'm a history teacher at the local high school. And for ten years I've been trying to understand what really happened in Jedwabne.

— And what have you found?

— I found what's not in the textbooks. Testimonies of people who were there. Accounts that don't fit the official version. Documents that show that what happened on 10 July 1941 was more complicated than we're told.

Piotr looked at him in silence.

— Why are you telling me this?

— Because I saw your lecture. I saw that you're looking for the truth, not for confirmation of your own beliefs. And I thought you might be able to help.

Marek took a thick folder out of his bag and put it on the table.

— These are copies of documents from the archive in Białystok. Testimonies of witnesses who were not interrogated in 1949. Here. Read it. And decide what to do with it.

Piotr took the folder. He felt its weight in his hands – not a physical weight, but that other one, which weighed more than all the books he had read in his life.

— Thank you — he said. — I'll do what I can.

Marek nodded and stood up.

— Good luck, Professor. And remember — the truth is like light. Even if someone tries to put it out, there will always be someone who lights another candle.

He left, leaving Piotr alone with the folder and the silence.

---
## V. THE EPILOGUE

A year later, Piotr published an article about Jedwabne. It was not a text that would please everyone. Quite the contrary – it met with a wave of criticism from both sides. But Piotr knew he had done what he had to do. He presented the facts he had found. And he left the readers to decide what they wanted to believe.

At the end of the article he wrote:

_"History does not belong to any one side. It is a search for truth. And the truth about Jedwabne is more complex than we have been told. Not because someone lied. But because each of us sees only part of the picture. And only when we put those parts together will we see the whole."_

The article did not change the world. But it changed Piotr.

Because he knew that he had finally found the answer to the question that had tormented him for years: not who the perpetrators were, not how many the victims were. But who he himself was as a historian. Someone who searches for the truth, even if that truth is inconvenient. And who is not afraid to show it.

---
**THE END**
