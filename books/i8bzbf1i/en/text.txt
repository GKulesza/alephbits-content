**YET**

When Julia was seven years old, she was the most curious child Tomasz had ever known. She asked questions from morning till night. Why is the sky blue? Where do clouds come from? What is in the middle of the earth? How does a car engine work? Sometimes Tomasz didn't know the answers, but Julia kept asking anyway.

– Dad, why do people have different skin colours?

– Dad, why don't fish sink?

– Dad, how do we know dinosaurs really existed?

Tomasz smiled and answered as best he could. Sometimes he looked for the answers on the internet, sometimes he made up funny explanations. What mattered was that Julia was curious about the world. And Tomasz was proud of that.

---
Five years passed. Julia was now twelve and in the sixth grade. Her questions had stopped. Instead there were sighs, eye-rolling and sentences like: "I don't feel like it", "That's boring", "What do I need this for?".

– Julia, do your homework – Tomasz would say.

– In a minute.

– Julia, an hour has already passed.

– OK, in a minute.

– Julia, what are you doing? I told you to do your homework.

– But it's boring, Dad.

Tomasz felt frustration building inside him. He didn't understand what had happened to his curious daughter. Where had the joy of discovering the world gone? Why was every lesson now a struggle?

---
One evening Tomasz was browsing the internet, looking for ways to motivate Julia to learn. He came across a video about the Singaporean method of teaching. Intrigued, he clicked play.

– Many parents observe a similar pattern – said the voice on the screen. – When a child is small, they constantly ask questions. They can be curious about the world from morning till night. Yet a few years later we hear more and more often: "I don't feel like it, it's boring, why do I have to learn this?".

Tomasz listened on. He learned three rules of the Singaporean approach. First: first awaken curiosity, and only then pass on knowledge. Second: treat mistakes as a natural part of learning. Third: help the child discover the answers on their own, instead of giving them straight away.

Rule number two interested him especially.

– A mistake is not a failure – said the voice. – It is information. It shows what still needs to be learned. Instead of asking: "Why did you make a mistake?", it is worth asking: "What can we learn from this?".

And then came the word that changed everything.

– When a child says "I can't do this", answer: "You can't do it yet". When they say "I'll never learn this", answer: "You haven't learned it yet". It's a small difference, but it completely changes the way of thinking.

Tomasz closed his laptop. He knew what he had to do.

---
The next day Julia brought home a maths test. She got a two – a failing grade.

– Dad, I'm good for nothing – she said, throwing the paper on the table. – I can't do maths.

Tomasz took a deep breath.

– You can't do it yet – he said.

Julia looked at him, surprised.

– What?

– You said you can't do maths. But that's not true. You can't do it yet. That means you can learn it.

Julia was silent for a moment.

– But I tried – she said. – And it didn't work.

– I know – Tomasz answered. – And that's all right. Everyone makes mistakes. What matters is learning from them. Let's see what went wrong.

They sat down together at the table. Tomasz did not give ready-made solutions. Instead, he asked questions.

– Where can we start? What do you already know? What solution comes to your mind?

Julia was reluctant at first. But slowly she began to think. She began to analyse. And in the end, after several failed attempts, she found the correct solution.

– I did it! – she exclaimed.

– You see? – Tomasz smiled. – You couldn't do it yet. But now you can.

Julia smiled. For the first time in many days.

---
Tomasz decided to apply another rule. Instead of asking Julia how school was, he started asking different questions.

– What surprised you most today?

– What new thing did you discover?

– What did you learn for the first time today?

– What's one thing you could teach me today?

At first Julia answered briefly: "I don't know", "Normal", "Nothing interesting". But Tomasz didn't give up. He kept asking. And in the end Julia began to open up.

– Actually, in biology class we talked about photosynthesis – she said one day. – And I learned that plants breathe differently than people. That was interesting.

– Really? – asked Tomasz. – Will you tell me about it?

Julia began to tell him. And the more she talked, the more animated she became. Tomasz saw in her eyes the same light he had seen when she was small. Curiosity. Wonder. The joy of discovery.

---
The next step was to show Julia that learning has practical applications. Tomasz decided to use everyday situations.

– Julia, will you help me make pancakes? – he asked one day.

– Sure.

– If we want to make a double portion, how much flour will we need?

Julia thought for a moment.

– The recipe says 200 grams. So 400.

– Great. And how many eggs?

– Four becomes eight.

– You see? – Tomasz smiled. – You just used maths.

Julia looked at him, surprised.

– That was maths?

– Of course. And you did it without any problem.

Julia smiled. Suddenly maths stopped being a boring subject from school. It became something that helps in real life.

---
Tomasz encouraged Julia to explain knowledge to others. Whenever she learned something new, he asked her to explain it to him or to her younger sister.

– Julia, imagine I'm a younger student – he would say. – Explain to me what you learned today.

And Julia explained. And the more she explained, the better she understood. Because when we can explain something in our own words, it means we truly understand it.

---
A month passed. Julia still had difficult moments. It still happened that she said: "I can't", "That's boring", "I don't feel like it". But now Tomasz knew how to respond.

– You can't do it yet – he would say. – But you can learn.

And Julia learned. Not always willingly, not always right away. But she learned. And more and more often, when she found an answer, the same smile appeared on her face that Tomasz remembered from her childhood.

One evening, while Julia was doing her homework, Tomasz heard her say to herself:

– I can't do this yet. But I can try.

Tomasz felt something tighten in his throat. It was the most beautiful thing he could have heard.

---
A few weeks later Julia brought home her report card. She had all good grades. But that wasn't the most important thing.

– Dad – she said. – You know what? I think I like learning.

Tomasz looked at her.

– Really?

– Yes – she answered. – It's not always easy. Sometimes it's boring. But when I understand something, I feel... good. Like I've gained something important.

Tomasz hugged her.

– That's what's called intrinsic motivation – he said. – And it's the best thing in the world.

Julia smiled.

– And you know what, Dad? Thank you.

– For what?

– For not giving up believing that I could learn.

Tomasz felt tears well up in his eyes.

– I always believed – he said. – And I always will.

---
That evening Tomasz sat down in his armchair and opened his laptop. He remembered the words from the video that had changed his approach to parenting:

"It's not about raising a child with the best grades. It's about raising a person who will want to learn for the rest of their life."

And he understood that that was exactly what he had done. He hadn't taught Julia maths or biology. He had taught her something more important – that learning is an adventure. That mistakes are natural. That it is worth trying. And that always, always, there is still "yet".

He looked at the door of Julia's room. Light was visible through the crack. Julia was reading a book. Not because she had to. Because she wanted to. And Tomasz knew that this was the greatest success of all.

**THE END**
