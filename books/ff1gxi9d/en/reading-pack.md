**THIRTEEN RULES**

When the American woman first set foot in Poland, she thought she knew everything. Before the trip she had watched a dozen YouTube videos, read travel blogs, and had even learned a few basic phrases – "dzień dobry", "dziękuję", "przepraszam". She was prepared. Or so she thought.

She was twenty-three years old, had fair hair tied up in a high ponytail and a smile that in California opened every door for her. In Poland it opened... nothing. People looked at her with a mix of curiosity and reserve, and when she tried to make eye contact with a passer-by on the street, he looked away, as if she had just committed a capital offence.

– What's wrong with them? – she asked her Polish friend, whom she had arranged to meet for coffee in central Warsaw.

– With whom? – he asked, without raising his eyes from his phone.

– With the Poles. Nobody smiles at me.

The friend raised an eyebrow.

– And do you smile at them?

– Well, yes! All the time!

– Then stop. You look like a psycho.


**Rule one: A smile has its price**

– In Poland a smile is not the default setting of your face – he explained, putting his phone down. – This isn't America, where a waitress asks how you're doing even if she couldn't care less. We are sincere. If we smile, it means we actually have a reason.

– So I should walk around with a face like this? – she asked, showing him her best "Polish smile" – a slight upturn of the corners of the mouth, without showing teeth.

– Exactly – he replied. – It's not a lack of kindness. It's saving energy. And authenticity.


**Rule two: Small talk is a waste of time**

She tried to strike up a conversation with a shop assistant in a grocery store. She asked whether she recommended any Polish cheese. The shop assistant looked at her as if she had asked for the recipe for an atomic bomb.

– The white one is good – she said dryly and turned to the next customer.

A few days later, at a meeting with a group of friends, she heard Poles talking among themselves. There was no "how's it going", no "nice to see you". They started with the concrete – with work, with problems, with what was really weighing on them.

– Why don't you do small talk? – she asked one evening.

– Because it's a waste of time – answered her new friend, Kasia. – If you ask someone how they're feeling, it means you really want to know. And if you don't, then why ask?

She was silent for a moment. In America "how are you" was just a polite opener, not an invitation to confide. In Poland it was exactly the opposite.

– And if someone asks me how I'm doing, what should I say?

– The truth – answered Kasia. – Or nothing. But don't pretend everything's great if it isn't. That's offensive.


**Rule three: Politics is a minefield**

The evening at the family dinner at Kasia's was supposed to be relaxing. It was. Until someone mentioned politics.

Within a few seconds the atmosphere thickened like Christmas borscht. The aunt threw out something about the ruling party, the uncle countered with an argument about the opposition, the grandmother chimed in with something about the old days, and everyone started talking at once, raising their voices as if the fate of the world depended on it.

She sat in the corner, holding a soup spoon in her hand and not knowing whether she should join in or pretend that her own fingernails had suddenly fascinated her.

– Don't worry – whispered Kasia, leaning toward her. – That's how we are. Poles love to argue. It's our national sport.

– But what's it about? – she asked quietly. – It's just politics.

– For you. For us it's history. It's identity. It's not an abstract subject. It's something our grandparents lived through first-hand.

After dinner, when the emotions had settled, everyone was kind to each other again. Someone offered tea, someone else cake. As if nothing had happened.

– And you're always like this? – she asked.

– Yes – Kasia smiled. – We argue, but we love each other.


**Rule four: Poland is not in Eastern Europe**

A few weeks later she met a new colleague from Germany at work. He asked how she liked living in "Eastern Europe". She answered truthfully that she liked it fine, but the rest of the room suddenly fell silent.

– Eastern? – someone from the other end of the table spoke up. – Poland is in Central Europe.

– Oh yes, sorry – said the German colleague, not quite understanding what he had said wrong.

– We're not Russia – added someone else, this time with a clear chill in their voice.

That evening she asked Kasia about it.

– Just don't say that – answered Kasia. – For us it's not only geography. It's a matter of identity. For decades we were lumped together with Russia. We don't want to go back to that.

– So what should I say?

– That we're in the heart of Europe. Look at the map. We are. And that's it.


**Rule five: Don't criticise Poland in front of Poles**

During a trip to the old town, accompanied by a Polish guide, she accidentally remarked that "in America such historic buildings would be better maintained". The guide made a face as if she had just insulted his mother.

– Madam – he said, stopping in the middle of the street. – We know that Poland is not perfect. We know there are things that could be better. But this is ours. And if someone says a foreign country is better, then... well, it's simply not nice.

– I'm sorry – she said quickly. – I didn't mean to...

– I know – he replied, walking on. – But it's us Poles who have the right to complain about Poland. Not visitors.

She understood that Poles have something that could be called "pride with a complex". They complain about their country more than anyone else, but if an outsider does it – they immediately rush to its defence. Like a family that loves each other, but nags.


**Rule six: Be a person of your word**

When her flatmate promised to fix the tap, he did it the next day. When Kasia said she would come at five, she appeared exactly on the minute. When someone said "I'll call you", they actually called.

– Words have weight with us – explained Kasia. – If you promise something, it means you'll do it. If you're not sure, better say nothing.

– And if someone says "maybe" or "we'll see"?

– That means "no", but they don't want to offend you.

– So just like in America.

– No. In America "maybe" means "yes, but not now". With us "maybe" means "no, but I don't want to hurt you".

She smiled. In Poland even politeness had its own logic.


**Rule seven: Don't flaunt your wealth**

One evening Kasia took her to a party at friends' place. One of the guests, an American who had come to Poland on business, was showing off his new watch and telling everyone how much it had cost. The Poles around him watched with embarrassment.

– What's he doing? – she quietly asked Kasia.

– He wants to impress – answered Kasia. – Only it doesn't work with us. If someone has money, they don't show it. That's inelegant.

– Why?

– Because Poles are rather modest. And they believe that a person's true worth is not in what they have, but in who they are.

The American, unaware that he had committed a faux pas, went on with his stories. The Poles around him exchanged glances that said everything.


**Rule eight: Respect the silence on public transport**

The first time she rode a tram in Warsaw she felt as if she had stepped into a library. People sat in silence, listening to music through earphones or staring at their phones. Nobody talked loudly, nobody laughed too boisterously.

– Peaceful, isn't it? – she asked Kasia.

– It's our rule – answered Kasia. – On public transport we don't disturb others. It's not a place for loud conversations.

– And if someone is loud?

– Then everyone looks at them. And that someone suddenly stops.

She remembered how in the American subway people talked loudly on the phone, played music without earphones, laughed their heads off. In Poland something like that was unthinkable.


**Rule nine: Don't cross on the red light**

She stood at a crossing in central Warsaw. Around her it was empty – not a single car, not a single bicycle. And yet the Poles stood on the kerb, waiting for the green light. She tried to cross, but Kasia grabbed her sleeve.

– Don't cross.

– But there's not a single car!

– It doesn't matter. It's red. We don't cross on red.

– Why?

– Because it's the rule. And we don't want to pay a fine.

– And if there's no police?

– We still don't cross. It's a matter of decency.

For a moment she thought Kasia was joking. But looking at the other Poles, who stood obediently waiting for the green light, she understood that this was no joke. It was part of their DNA.


**Rule ten: Take off your shoes at home**

The first time she entered Kasia's flat, she took off her shoes before crossing the threshold. She did it instinctively, because she had seen everyone doing it. But when she asked about it, Kasia explained:

– In Poland we don't walk around the house in shoes. It's dirty. And unhygienic.

– But back home in America...

– You're not in America now. Here Polish rules apply. And slippers are standard equipment in every home.

She received her first Polish slippers – soft, warm, with a wool lining. She put them on and felt... at home. Even though she was still far from home.


**Rule eleven: Learn a few words of Polish**

After a month in Poland she decided to learn Polish. Not fluently, not perfectly – but enough to be able to say "dziękuję" with the right accent. To pronounce "proszę" so it didn't sound like "prosze" with an American r. To say "przepraszam" with the right amount of "sz" and "cz".

When she first said "dziękuję" in a shop and the shop assistant answered "proszę", she felt she had taken a step forward. It was a small victory.

– It's not difficult – she said to Kasia. – You just have to want to.

– Exactly – answered Kasia. – And that's what Poles appreciate. Not that you speak perfectly, but that you try.


**Rule twelve: Don't impose your culture**

Once, during a conversation with a group of friends, someone suggested that Poland should have more cafés open until late, like in New York. The Poles around her shook their heads.

– We don't need that – said one of them. – We have our own opening hours, our own habits. We don't have to be like America.

– But it would be convenient...

– Maybe. But it's not Polish. And if you want to live in Poland, you have to adapt. Don't expect Poland to adapt to you.

At that moment she understood that the difference between a tourist and a resident is that a tourist demands that the country be like his own. A resident accepts it as it is.


**Rule thirteen: Be useful**

– Polish women want men who are useful – said Kasia, when they were talking about Polish relationships. – Not just men who look good and earn well. But men who can fix something around the house, change a light bulb, do something with their own hands.

– So handymen?

– Exactly. I have an uncle who can do everything. From fixing a tap to assembling furniture. Every aunt in the family admires him. Not because he's handsome. Because he's useful.

She smiled. In America men were judged for who they are. In Poland – for what they can do.


**Epilogue**

After a few months in Poland she had stopped being surprised. She had learned to smile the Polish way – slightly, without showing her teeth. She had learned that "jak leci" is not a question, but an opening to a conversation. She had learned that Poland is in Central Europe, not Eastern. She had learned that promises are not just politeness, but a commitment.

And she had learned that if you want to understand Poles, you have to look at their history. At the wars, the occupation, communism, at the suffering that shaped their character – tough, distrustful of strangers, but loyal to their own.

One day, walking through Warsaw, she smiled at a random passer-by. To her surprise, he smiled back.

– See? – she said to herself in English, but with a Polish accent that was not yet perfect, but was already hers. – It works.

And she walked on, knowing that in Poland – as in every country in the world – the most important thing is to understand that you don't have to be the same. You only have to respect what is different.


**THE END**
