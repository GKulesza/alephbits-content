**THE LIST OF THINGS I REGRET**

Anna was forty-two years old and had two grown children. When she looked back, she saw years that had flown by like a single day. And she saw the mistakes she had made. Not big, not dramatic – those everyday, small ones that add up to a whole life.

She sat in the kitchen with a mug of tea, looking at the old photographs she had just found in the wardrobe. In one of them her son Tomek was five years old and grinning from ear to ear. He was holding a plasticine dinosaur he had made himself. Anna remembered that day. She remembered that she was tired, that she had a mountain of work, that she had shouted at Tomek because he had spilt the plasticine on the carpet.

– You've made a mess again – she had said then. – How many times do I have to tell you to be careful?

Tomek lowered his eyes. The dinosaur landed in the bin. Anna didn't remember what happened later. She only remembered his eyes – sad, disappointed, lost.

She recalled the words from a film she had watched recently. They talked about the things parents regret. About scolding too often. About missed moments. About not hugging enough.

Anna put the photograph down and looked at her hands. She wondered how many times she could have done something differently. And whether you can turn back time at all.

---
Tomek was now twenty-two. He studied in another city, came home for holidays and birthdays. They talked on the phone, but Anna felt that something between them had broken. There was no longer that closeness she remembered from his childhood.

One day, during a Christmas dinner, Anna asked:

– Tomek, do you remember when you were little?

– What specifically?

– Well, the things we used to do together. The walks we took, the books we read.

Tomek smiled, but there was something in his eyes that Anna couldn't name.

– I remember you were always tired – he said. – And that you often got annoyed. I remember you shouting at me because I'd spilt the plasticine again.

Anna felt something tighten in her throat.

– I... I didn't mean to – she said. – I was just tired.

– I know. And I don't hold it against you. But I remember it. And I remember that once you promised we'd go to the cinema, but you didn't go because you had an important meeting.

Anna closed her eyes. She remembered. She remembered Tomek standing in the doorway in his jacket, ready to leave. And her, in a hurry, saying: "I'm sorry, sweetheart, I have to cancel. Mummy has something urgent."

– I'm sorry – she said quietly.

– You don't have to apologise – answered Tomek. – It was a long time ago. It's just... I think I always believed I was less important than your work.

Anna was silent. She knew it wasn't true. But she also knew that that's how it had looked from a child's perspective.

---
That evening Anna sat down at the computer. She opened a notepad and started writing.

_The list of things I regret._

_1. I scolded Tomek too often. For every little thing, for every mess, for every mistake. Instead of supporting him, I criticised. Instead of teaching, I punished._

_2. I missed the most important moments. His first performance at school, his first match, his first day at a new school. I always had more important things to do._

_3. We never spent time together on creative play. Once, when he was little, he loved to draw. And I said: "Leave that, do your homework."_

_4. I didn't take photographs. I didn't film anything. I thought I would remember everything. But I don't. Only hazy images remain._

_5. I didn't take his opinions into account. I said: "You're a child, you don't have an opinion." And then I wondered why he never said what he thought._

_6. I didn't enjoy being with him. Instead of delighting in him, I worried about chores, about work, about everything I still had to do._

_7. I didn't teach him about money. I said: "We can't afford it," but I didn't explain why. Later, when he started earning, he spent everything he had._

_8. I didn't care about his happiness. I thought the most important things were grades, successes, the future. I forgot that childhood is supposed to be joyful._

_9. I didn't hug him often enough. I thought that when he got older, he wouldn't want it. And I was right. He stopped wanting it before I even noticed._

_10. I was inconsistent. Sometimes I said "no", and then "yes". Sometimes I punished, and then I let it go. The child didn't know what to expect._

_11. I listened to other people's advice. The neighbour said you have to be strict. My aunt said you have to be tender. And I, confused, tried to please everyone. I forgot that I'm the mother._

Anna read the list. It was long. And painful. But she hadn't written it to blame herself. She had written it to understand. And to change something.

---
The next day she called Tomek.

– Sweetheart, I have a request – she said.

– I'm listening.

– I'd like us to spend a weekend together. Just the two of us. No work, no chores. I'd like to make up for lost time.

Tomek was silent for a moment.

– That's nice, Mum – he said at last. – But you don't have to. I know you're busy.

– I'm not busy – she answered. – And I'm not doing it out of duty. I'm doing it because I want to. Because I miss you. And because I want you to know that you've always been the most important thing to me. Even if I didn't always show it.

Tomek was silent longer this time.

– All right – he said at last. – I'll come.

---
They spent the weekend together. They went to the cinema, for a walk, to a museum. Anna didn't look at her phone. She didn't think about work. She was just with him.

In the evening, as they sat over tea, Tomek said:

– Mum, do you remember how you once said you regretted not taking photographs?

– I remember.

– I did something. I'd like to show you.

Tomek took out his phone and showed her an album. Photographs he had found in old boxes, scanned and organised. There were pictures from his childhood – the ones Anna didn't remember because she hadn't taken them. But there were also the ones she had taken. Smiles, laughter, moments that had survived.

– Where did you get these? – asked Anna, wiping away tears.

– I found them in your wardrobe – said Tomek. – And I thought they were worth keeping. They're our memories. Even if not all of them are perfect, they're ours.

Anna hugged her son. For the first time in many years.

– I'm sorry – she said.

– For what?

– For everything. For not being there when you needed me. For trying too hard to be the perfect mum instead of just being a mum. And for only understanding this now.

Tomek hugged her tighter.

– You don't have to apologise – he said. – You're my mum. And you always will be.

---
A few days later Anna returned to the list. She read it once more. And instead of feeling sad, she decided to do something with it. She started with number 6 – "I didn't enjoy being with my children."

She called her daughter, who lived abroad. They talked for two hours. They laughed, cried, remembered. Anna didn't hurry. She had no more important things to do.

Then number 9 – "I didn't hug my child often enough." The next time she saw Tomek, she hugged him as tightly as she could.

Number 2 – "I missed the most important moments." She decided that from now on she would be present. At every important event, at every meeting, at every conversation.

Number 11 – "I listened to other people's advice." She decided to trust herself. She knew that as a mother she had an instinct that wouldn't let her down.

And number 1 – "I scolded too often." She started speaking to her children differently. Instead of criticising, supporting. Instead of punishing, explaining.

Not everything could be fixed. Some mistakes remained. But Anna knew that the most important thing was to keep trying. And for the children to know that they are loved. Unconditionally.

---
One day Tomek wrote her a message.

_Mum, do you remember the list you wrote?_
_I remember._
_I read it. And you know what? I'm not angry. I'm grateful. Because what you wrote shows that you care. That you want to be better. And that you love me. That's the most important thing._

Anna smiled through her tears.

_I love you, sweetheart. Always._
_I love you too, Mum._

Anna closed her phone. She looked at the photograph standing on her desk – Tomek at five, with the plasticine dinosaur. And she thought that although she couldn't turn back time, she could make the future different.

And that what mattered most she already had – love that had survived all the mistakes.

**THE END**
