## Text

### 1. **The station that isn't there**

Marek was travelling by train from Poznań to Gdańsk. He was forty-seven, had a suitcase on wheels and a ticket in his pocket that he had bought online – contactlessly, no human word exchanged. He had been travelling this way for years. Boarding, getting off, changing trains, often without even looking at the station building in which he had just spent ten minutes on a platform.

But that day something was different.

Maybe it was the weather – grey, April, with rain that didn't dare fall and only hung in the air like an unspoken sentence. Maybe it was the conversation he had had the day before with his father, who had called from Tarnów and remembered how people used to travel by rail back then, when stations were still something more than shopping malls.

– You were at the station – his father said. – It meant you were in the city. Today it's just a corridor.

Marek had laughed then. But now, standing on the platform of Gdańsk Główny station, he understood what his father meant.

---

The Gdańsk station was beautiful. Marek had always known that – he had grown up in the Tri-City, had known the building since childhood, remembered his grandmother telling him that once architects from Japan had come to see it and then built a copy of it in their own country. Back then Marek hadn't understood why anyone would copy a Polish station. Now, looking at the neo-Gothic towers, the brick walls, the soaring windows and that incredible, fairy-tale silhouette towering over the city, he knew the Japanese had been right.

This wasn't a station. It was a cathedral of travel.

He went inside and immediately felt the difference. The Gdańsk station smelled of dampness, old wood and something else – maybe the scent of thousands of people who had passed through this hall over the decades. They had been here before the war, during the war, after the war. They had been here in the 1980s, in the time of martial law, in the 1990s, when the station became a gateway to the West. And they were here today – tourists with cameras, businessmen with suitcases, mothers with children, elderly people with shopping bags.

Marek sat down on a bench in the main hall and looked at the clock. It worked. It showed a minute past twelve.

And then he remembered something he had read in the comments under a video about the most beautiful stations in Poland. Someone had written: _"Gdańsk? A logistical failure. Beautiful outside, but nothing inside."_ Someone else had replied: _"But it has atmosphere. You can't buy that in a mall."_

Marek smiled to himself. Because he knew they were both right. The Gdańsk station was an architectural gem, but also a place that had been neglected for years. Inside it was empty, dirty, sometimes even gloomy. The platforms remembered better times, and the waiting room – if you could call it that – didn't invite you to stay.

But that was changing. A renovation was underway. And Marek could already see the first results – new benches, cleaner walls, more light. Maybe in a year or two the station would regain its glory. Maybe it would look again like in the old postcards. Maybe even more beautiful.

He stood up and headed for the exit. He had time. The train to Gdynia departed in forty minutes. Instead of waiting on the platform, he decided to do something he hadn't done in years – take a walk around the station. See what had changed and what had stayed the same.

---

He passed a coffee stand that used to be a kiosk with hot drinks. Now it looked like a small café with vegan croissants and coffee at twelve zloty. Nearby, in the place where the waiting room used to be, stood comfortable armchairs and people worked on laptops.

Marek felt as if he were in a lounge – but one that belonged to everyone. It was strange. And good.

Then he walked over to the notice board. Where hand-written notices about delays and changed platforms used to hang, a touch screen now gleamed. But next to it – someone had stuck a piece of paper. Written by hand, in marker pen: _"Gdańsk Główny. The most beautiful station in Poland. True or false? Vote!"_ Beneath it someone had added in pen: _"False. Wrocław is prettier."_ Someone else had replied: _"Gdańsk has atmosphere. Wrocław has a mall."_ And yet another: _"Both are beautiful. But I'm from Bydgoszcz and it's lovely there too."_

Marek laughed out loud. It was the same dispute that raged in thousands of comments under videos on YouTube. Wrocław versus Gdańsk. Modernity versus history. Mall versus atmosphere. And always somewhere in the background Poznań would appear – that unjustly criticised "bread box" that had landed in fourth place in the ranking, provoking an avalanche of outrage.

Marek came from Poznań. He had lived there for twenty years before moving to the Tri-City. And he remembered the old station – the one from before the redevelopment, with its great hall, with the clock standing at the centre, with the café where travellers met. He remembered how in the 1990s traders laid out VHS tapes on the floor – those with films recorded from German television, with stickers printed on the head, black-and-red, with titles like "Commando" and descriptions that promised more than the film could deliver.

Then the station disappeared. It became the "bread box" – modern, glassy, controversial. Part of the space was taken over by a shopping mall. The platforms moved away from the entrance. Travellers complained about the chaos, the distances, the lack of identity.

And yet – Marek liked the new station. It wasn't beautiful, but it was functional. And it had something the old station lacked – light. Space. Air.

Maybe that's why he defended Poznań in the comments. Not because it was the best. But because it was his.

---

He returned to the platform. The train was already waiting. He boarded, found a seat by the window, took out his headphones. In front of him, on the seat, lay a newspaper – someone had left it. On the front page: "Nowy Targ – the largest market in Poland. Hungarians, Czechs and Slovaks come. Cashmere for 270 zloty."

Marek smiled. A market. Cashmere. Hungarians. But that was a different story. Today – only the station.

The train pulled away. Outside the window poles, trees and rooftops flashed by. Gdańsk was disappearing in the rain.

Marek closed his eyes and thought about what he had heard in one of the comments: _"A beautiful station is one that stinks of piss."_

Brutal. But true. Because a station isn't just a building. A station is a place where people wait, return, say goodbye, cry, laugh, eat sour apples from paper bags and try to fall asleep on plastic benches. It's not a mall. It's not a lounge. It's the hallway of life.

And maybe that's why stations stir up so many emotions. Because each of us has our own – the first one, the last one, the one from childhood, the one of parting, the one of return. And each of us wants it to be beautiful. Even if only for a moment.

---

Marek opened his eyes. Another station appeared outside the window. Tczew. Small, modest, but well-kept. Someone in the comments had mentioned it – that it's also pretty, that it also has history.

Maybe someday someone would make a ranking of the fifty most beautiful stations. Or a hundred. Maybe then there would be room for all of them – for Tarnów, for Opole, for Bielsko-Biała, for Wodzisław Śląski, for Legnica.

Or maybe not. Maybe that's not the point.

Maybe the point is to stop at a station for a moment – not just to change trains. But to look, to listen, to feel. To remind yourself that every journey has its ritual. And that a station isn't just a corridor.

Marek took out his phone and opened the notes app. He wrote:

_"A station isn't a place. It's a threshold. And everyone who passes through it leaves a piece of themselves on it. That's why they're so important. And that's why we want them to be so beautiful."_

He stopped. Then he added:

_"Even if it stinks of piss inside."_

He smiled. The train slowed down. Outside the window appeared Gdynia. And another station, waiting for its story.
