## Text

**THE THRESHOLD**

Przemek woke up at 5:47. Not because of an alarm clock – it was just how he was. The hour at which he always opened his eyes, before the sun had even managed to peek through the window. He took a sip of cold tea that had been sitting on the bedside table since yesterday. He switched on his phone. On the neighbourhood group, someone had posted a link about the case at the University of Economics.

– Przemek, look – wrote Kamil. – They're renting out a space for 1200 zloty. The market rate is 3500. And someone there is legalising foreigners. On university grounds. Do you really want to go there?

Przemek scrolled through the comments. People wrote the same thing: "Someone should check this out", "Maybe someone should ask", "But I'm afraid they'll call the police on us". Przemek put the phone down. He thought about his father, who in the nineties had lost his job for asking his boss about the missing invoices. "Don't ask questions, son" – he used to say afterwards. "Questions are expensive."

Przemek got up. In the kitchen he made a sandwich and wrapped it in foil. Into his bag he put a notebook, a pen and his ID card. He didn't need any more authorisation than that. He wasn't a policeman, he wasn't a prosecutor. He was just a man who wanted to know why a public university was renting out its premises for a third of the market value. And whether that company was really operating within the law.

As he was leaving, his wife looked up from her book.

– Where are you going?

– To the university.

– What for?

– To ask about something.

– Take care of yourself.

– I always do.

---
The University of Economics in Poznań smelled of floor cleaner and eternal coffee. The stairwell was clean, hushed. On the third floor, on the door, hung a small sign: _Residence legalisation – no refusals, no stress, full effectiveness_. Przemek looked at Kamil. Kamil shrugged.

– Well, knock then.

Przemek knocked. The door opened immediately, as if someone behind it had been waiting. In it stood a young woman, smiling, with a phone in her hand – she was already recording.

– Good morning – said Przemek. – My name is Przemysław Grzegorek. May I ask you a few questions?

– Please do.

They didn't go inside. They stood in the corridor, as in polite conversations. The woman spoke nicely, fluently, but there was neither surprise nor indignation in her voice. She was prepared. She didn't ask who they were, didn't ask for a badge, didn't close the door. Przemek asked about the rental price, about the legalisation, about the legal basis. She answered with generalities. At one point Przemek asked about the banner and the UPA cult.

– This is not the place for political discussions – she replied.

– I understand. Thank you.

They left. On the stairs Kamil said:

– She knew we were coming.

– I know. She wasn't the least bit nervous.

– And she was recording everything.

– That's good. Let her record. I was recording too.

Przemek put his phone in his pocket. He thought that was the end. That he'd go home, make notes, write an email to the rector. Maybe the case would reach the media, maybe not. But he'd done his part. He'd asked. That was enough.

---
Seven days later, at 5:47, someone knocked on the door. Przemek opened his eyes before the knocking had even died down. He got out of bed and put the kettle on. He thought: maybe the postman. Or the neighbour upstairs, whose water always leaks.

Then the knocking came again – louder, more regular. And a voice: _Police, open up!_

Przemek didn't manage to touch the handle. The door opened by itself – someone had keys. Six men in blue uniforms stood in the doorway. One of them held a camera. Another – a video camera. The third reached for Przemek before he could take a single step back.

– Przemysław Grzegorek?

– Yes.

– You're under arrest. Please turn around and put your hands behind your back.

Przemek obeyed. He felt cold metal on his wrists – from behind, not from the front. Someone threw a jacket over his shoulders to hide the handcuffs. They led him down the corridor. In the kitchen doorway stood his wife. She looked at him as if she were seeing a stranger. In the upstairs window, a child's face pressed against the glass. Przemek wanted to say: "Everything will be fine", but he didn't manage. He was pushed toward the stairs.

In the police car it smelled of sweat and plastic. One of the officers was writing something in a notebook. The other was looking at his phone. Nobody spoke. Przemek tried to understand what was happening, but in his head only one question was working: _why?_

At the prosecutor's office they told him to sit down. He was still handcuffed. He gave his testimony in handcuffs. He dictated, and the official typed it into the computer. After an hour someone came over and unfastened one pair of handcuffs, refastening his hands in front.

– You need to sign – he said.

Przemek looked at his hands. They were blue. He signed.

– Article 212. Public defamation. Punishable by a fine or a year in prison.

– Is that a private prosecution offence? – asked Przemek.

The prosecutor looked at him carefully.

– Yes, it is.

– Then why did the police come to me at 6 in the morning?

Nobody answered.

---
He left after two hours. He went home by taxi, because they'd taken his phone and laptop. At the door his wife welcomed him. For a moment they stood in silence.

– You asked her about that company – she said. – You only asked.

– Yes.

– And for that they handcuffed you.

Przemek sat down in the kitchen. He poured himself some tea, even though it had gone cold earlier. In his head he was putting together a puzzle: the police shouldn't act on their own initiative under 212. It's a civil case. The injured party has to report it herself. And the injured party – that Ukrainian woman from the university – hadn't reported it. Yes, she had filed a complaint, but only after the police had located her themselves, found her and brought her to the station. The initiative had come from the officers. And then there were the photos – published on the station's Facebook page, showing him being led down the stairs as if he were a murderer. Photos taken for the media, for the politicians, for the viewers.

Przemek opened the laptop that had been returned to him. He started the browser. He found the profile of Paweł Wyrzykowski. Someone had told him about him. A journalist who had been the target of a witch hunt for years. A man who had also ended up on the Ukrainian death list. Przemek wrote: _I need help._

The answer came after ten minutes: _You've been arrested for asking questions. I know. Call me._

---
– You say you were handcuffed from behind? – asked Paweł over the phone.

– Yes. And during my testimony too. Only before signing the protocol did they cuff my hands in front.

– This is a scandal I haven't seen in years. Listen, I've got four kids already. Seventy kilometres from me, they murdered a man from the same list I'm on. I'll stop at nothing.

Przemek listened. Paweł's voice was calm, but in the background children could be heard – laughter, footsteps, normal life. And yet this man was talking about his own death as a possible scenario.

– You're not alone – Paweł added. – Behind you stand people who believe that in Poland you can still ask about something without handcuffs. You'll get legal help. Don't give up.

Przemek put down the receiver. The kitchen was quiet. Through the window the street could be seen – cars, people going to work, children going to school. Everything looked normal. Only inside Przemek something had cracked. Not from fear. From something else.

He understood that the state, which should protect, had come for him because he'd asked a question. That the line between legal and illegal had moved so far that he no longer knew which side he stood on. And that he could no longer pretend it didn't concern him.

He took a notebook and a pen. He sat down at the table. He began to write a list – names, dates, case numbers, company names. Every detail, every piece of the puzzle. When he finished, he looked at his wife.

– I'm going to fight for my rights.

– I know.

– Because if not me, then who? If we don't ask, no one will ask.

– Go to sleep – his wife said. – Tomorrow we start over.

Przemek closed the notebook. He lay down on the bed. Through the open window came the sound of a siren – somewhere far away, maybe at the other end of the city. He used to think a siren was a sign that someone needed help. Now he knew that sometimes it meant something completely different: that someone had asked about something they shouldn't have.

He fell asleep after 11 pm. He woke up at 5:47. With an alarm clock.

**THE END**
