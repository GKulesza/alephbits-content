**A NEW WORLD**

When Tomek was little, the world was simple. His parents spoke, he listened. Teachers spoke, he listened. Adults knew what the future would look like, and they prepared him for it the way you prepare a tree for winter – pruning the branches so it would grow straight.

– Children should be seen and not heard – his father used to say whenever Tomek tried to speak up. – You're supposed to listen, not talk.

And Tomek listened. He learned that his opinion didn't matter. That adults knew better. That if he was well-behaved and obedient, he would do well in life.

The years passed. Tomek was now forty, had two children – ten-year-old Zosia and eight-year-old Borys – and a job that consumed him more than he wanted. He came home tired, sat down in front of the computer and scrolled through social media to rest. And the children – they had their screens too. Phones, tablets, the TV. Everyone in one room, each in their own world.

And they were comfortable. Nobody argued. Nobody asked awkward questions. It was quiet.

Until one day Zosia brought home from school a report card with a two (a fail) in maths.

– What's this supposed to be? – asked Tomek, looking at the grade. – You did study, didn't you?

– I did study – Zosia answered quietly.

– Then why did you get a two?

– I don't know. I couldn't concentrate on the test.

– You couldn't concentrate? – Tomek felt the anger rising inside him. – You couldn't concentrate because you were probably sitting on that phone again. I told you, you use it too much.

Zosia lowered her eyes.

– It's not the phone, Dad.

– Then what is it?

– Things just get mixed up in my head. I can't focus on anything.

Tomek sighed heavily.

– That's not an excuse. You're supposed to study, not make things up.

Zosia got up and left the room. Tomek was left alone, looking at the empty chair. He knew he should have done something differently, but he didn't know what. The old pattern kept playing in his head: "She did wrong, so you have to tell her she did wrong. That's how you raise children."

---
A few days later Tomek was driving home from work. The car radio was on, but he wasn't listening. He was thinking about Zosia. About how she hadn't wanted to talk to him lately. How she locked herself in her room when he came home. How she didn't look him in the eye.

He turned on a podcast a friend had recommended. "U Sawickich" – the guest was Mrs Maria Berlińska, who was talking about raising children. Tomek listened with half an ear, but one sentence made him press pause.

"Children used to be expected to listen, and now they want to be in a dialogue."

Tomek stopped the car at the side of the road. For a moment he sat in silence, staring at the windscreen. And then he heard more words:

"There is no such thing as stress-free parenting. Today's world is one endless source of stress for children. Home should be a place where they are accepted, understood, supported."

Tomek thought about Zosia. About how she said she couldn't concentrate. And he remembered how he himself had felt as a child when adults told him he was naughty, instead of asking him why he was behaving that way.

– Maybe you're the one not listening, Zosia – he said to himself. – Maybe I'm the one not listening to you.

---
That evening Tomek went over to Zosia, who was sitting on the bed drawing.

– Can I sit down? – he asked.

Zosia shrugged. Tomek sat down next to her.

– I'd like to ask you something – he said. – About that test. What happened?

Zosia looked at him, surprised.

– I told you, I couldn't concentrate.

– But why? What was distracting you?

– I don't know. Everything. Thoughts. Dad, sometimes I have so many thoughts in my head that I don't know where to start. And then I can't do anything.

Tomek felt something tighten in his throat. It was exactly what he himself had felt as a child. Only then, nobody asked him. Nobody tried to understand.

– And how do you feel then? – he asked.

– Angry. At myself. Because I can't stop it. And then I get frustrated, and then it gets even worse.

– I understand – said Tomek. – I feel like that sometimes too.

Zosia looked at him in disbelief.

– Really?

– Really. And you know what helps me?

– What?

– When someone listens to me. When I can talk about what I'm feeling. And when I can take a break from all those thoughts.

Zosia smiled. For the first time in days.

– And how do I do that?

– We can try together. For example in the evenings, without phones, without the TV. Just talking. Or reading. Or going for a walk. What do you think?

Zosia nodded.

– Okay.

---
Tomek decided to change something. At first it was hard. Every evening he put his phone aside and sat with Zosia and Borys. At first the children didn't know what to do with themselves. They were used to evenings being screen time. But slowly they started to talk.

Zosia talked about school, about her friends, about what was bothering her. Borys talked about his dreams – he wanted to become a palaeontologist. Tomek listened. And he didn't judge. He didn't criticise. He didn't say: "That's stupid" or "You can't do that."

And then, one evening, Zosia said something that surprised him.

– Dad, why have we never talked like this before?

Tomek hesitated.

– Because I didn't know it was so important. I thought it was enough that you were safe, that you had food, clothes, school. I thought that was enough.

– It's not enough – said Zosia. – I need you to be with me. Not just physically.

– I know. And I'm sorry I didn't know that.

Zosia hugged him.

– You don't have to apologise. What matters is that you know now.

---
Tomek thought about what Mrs Maria had said: "We raise our children by who we are." And he understood that if he wanted his children to be present, he himself had to be present. If he wanted them to listen, he had to listen. If he wanted them to feel valued, he had to give them that.

He remembered how he himself had been raised. How his parents told him what to do, but never asked him what he felt. And how for many years he had thought that was what love looked like. That love meant providing safety, not being together.

But that wasn't enough. And Tomek knew it now.

---
A few months later, at parent-teacher night, Zosia's teacher told Tomek that Zosia had changed. She was more open, more self-confident, doing better at school.

– Did something change at home? – asked the teacher.

Tomek smiled.

– I changed.

On the way home Tomek thought about his own father. About the fact that he'd never had the chance to change. That he had grown up in a different world, where there was no room for conversation. And that he couldn't be blamed for it.

But he, Tomek, had the chance. He could be different. He could give his children what he himself had never received. And although it was hard, although he sometimes fell back into old habits, he knew he was heading in the right direction.

In the evening, when the children had gone to bed, Tomek sat down in the living room. For a moment he looked at the phone lying on the table. He could pick it up, switch it on, sink into a world that asked nothing of him. But instead he picked up a book he had wanted to read for a long time.

And he thought about what Mrs Maria had said: "Raising children is also raising oneself."

Maybe that was the point. Not being a perfect parent. Being present. And trying again every day.

---
The next morning Zosia woke up earlier. She went over to Tomek, who was drinking coffee in the kitchen, and said:

– Dad, today I'd like to go for a walk. Just the two of us.

Tomek looked at her and smiled.

– Okay. We'll go together.

They went out in front of the house. It was early morning, the sun was just rising. They walked in silence, but it was a good silence – the kind in which you can feel the other person.

– Dad – said Zosia. – Thank you.

– For what?

– That you're here. And that you listen.

Tomek stopped for a moment. He felt something break inside him – not from pain, but from relief.

– Thank you too – he said. – For giving me a chance.

Zosia smiled and took his hand. And they walked on, into a new world. A world in which children not only listen, but also talk. In which parents not only provide, but also are there. In which raising children is not training, but a relationship.

And Tomek knew that this was only the beginning. But it was a good beginning.

**THE END**
