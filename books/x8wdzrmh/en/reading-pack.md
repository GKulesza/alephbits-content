## I. Spring 2026, Bory Tucholskie

When Tomek turned twenty, his father said:

– You don't learn to read the forest from books. The forest is read by looking at it. Look, and it will tell you what is happening.

Tomek was the son of a forester, the grandson of a forester and the great-grandson of a lumberjack. The forest was in his genes. He knew that a pine needs eighty years to mature, that an oak needs even longer, and that a spruce grows quickly but also falls ill quickly. He knew that in the Bory Tucholskie forest the water lies shallow and the roots reach deep. He knew that once, when he was a boy, in spring people broke the ice on the ditches, and in summer birds bathed in the puddles.

That morning, when he got up at dawn, he needed neither a thermometer nor forecasts. He knew it was going to be hot. The air was dry, and the ground under his boots creaked like an old parquet floor. He went out to the clearing and looked at the trees. The pines, which once had been green like bottle glass, now had a yellowish-brown tint. The needles were falling prematurely. On the bark appeared cracks he did not remember.

He sat down on a stone sticking out of the grass. There was less grass. In the places where there used to be moist undergrowth, there was now sand.

The phone rang.

– Tomek, are you in the forest? – his father's voice, though old, was still firm.

– I am.

– Do you see it?

– I see it.

– Every year it gets worse. This is no longer a normal drought. This is a change. And I don't know how much longer these trees will hold out.

Tomek didn't answer. He looked at the horizon line, where the pines met the sky. Once he had seen green there. Now he saw dust.

– We have to do something – he said at last.

– What? Pump water from the depths? That's running out too. You pump it out, and in ten years there'll be nothing left.

– Then maybe plant different trees?

– What kind? These are sands, son. Pine has grown here for centuries. If the pine can't make it, then I don't know what can.

Tomek stood up and set off into the forest. He walked fast, as if he wanted to run away from the answers. But the answers were everywhere: in the withered branches, in the cracked ground, in the silence that once had been filled with birdsong.

---

## II. Spring 2026, Poznań

Professor Bogdan Chojnicki stood in front of the lecture hall and waited for the students to sit down. He was a climatologist. For the last thirty years he had studied changes in temperature, precipitation, evaporation. He knew more about the climate than most people knew about their own bodies.

– Good morning – he said, when the hall grew quiet. – Today we will talk about drought. Not the one from last year, and not the one that will come next year. The one that is happening now.

He turned on the projector. On the screen appeared a map of Poland with the areas affected by drought marked. They were everywhere.

– These are this week's data – he said. – 71% of rivers in Poland have low water levels. This is not a picture from the end of August. This is the beginning of June. What does it mean? It means that the water that should be in the soil, in the rivers, in the lakes, has evaporated. And it will not return until heavy rains fall. And the rains are not falling.

The hall fell silent.

– Professor, but there were droughts before too – a student from the back row spoke up.

– There were – Chojnicki admitted. – But not like these and not so often. In the 1980s, March was a winter month. Today no one thinks of it that way anymore. The change is real and measurable.

– So what should we do? – came the question.

– First: stop pretending the problem doesn't exist. Second: stop thinking only about yourself. Climate change won't end with us installing air conditioning. The climate system is not our home. It's the whole planet. And if we don't take care of it, there won't be anywhere left to bury us.

Silence fell. Chojnicki turned off the projector.

– That's all for today. Next time we'll talk about what we can do. But remember: there is no more time for debate.

---

## III. Summer 2026, Bory Tucholskie

August was the worst. Temperatures exceeded 35 degrees for ten days in a row. Fires broke out in several places at once. Tomek hadn't slept for three days. Together with other foresters he put out fires that appeared like mushrooms after rain — except there was no rain.

– The water in the wells has dropped by two metres – one of them said, handing Tomek a bottle. – In a month it may be dry.

– And in two? – asked Tomek.

– In two it will be even worse.

Tomek took a sip of water and looked at the forest. The pines stood like skeletons. The needles were brown and the roots stuck out of the ground. The trees he remembered from his childhood were dying.

He returned home late in the evening. On the radio there was news about yet another temperature record. Meteorologists warned of a "heat dome". Farmers talked about ruined crops. Politicians talked about subsidies. No one talked about what would happen in ten years. In twenty. In fifty.

He sat down at the table and opened his father's notebook. The old forester wrote everything down in it: the weather, the condition of the trees, the rainfall. The last entries were short, as if his father didn't have the strength for more:

_"2023: Dry spring. Trees weak. Water in the ditches at the bottom."_

_"2024: Drought. First dying spruces."_

_"2025: Forests in bad condition. I don't know if they will survive."_

_"2026: I don't know if I will survive."_

Tomek closed the notebook and went to bed. But he couldn't fall asleep. He thought about what Professor Chojnicki had said in the lecture he had watched on the internet:

_"The climate system can be compared to an organism. When the temperature rises by two degrees, it is like a human fever of 38.6. And no one says that's good."_

Tomek got up and walked to the window. Outside it was dark, but the sky was red from the glow of the fires. He knew that this forest, which he loved, was disappearing. And that there was no tree that could be planted in its place.

---

## IV. Spring 2027, Warsaw

The climate minister's presentation lasted an hour. There were charts, tables, forecasts. At the end the minister said:

– We must invest in retention. In reservoirs, in small-scale water infrastructure, in education. Without this we will not survive the coming years.

The hall grew loud. Journalists asked questions, politicians commented, experts argued over details.

In the last row sat a young man with a notebook in his hand. His name was Paweł. He was a journalist specialising in climate topics. On a daily basis he wrote about politics, but for a year he had been covering what was happening to Polish nature.

After the conference he walked up to the minister.

– Mr Minister, can we talk?

– Of course.

– What about the forests? What about the farmers? What about the water in the wells?

The minister sighed.

– We know it's a problem. We are working on solutions.

– What kind?

– Above all, education. We must change public awareness.

– And if awareness doesn't change? – asked Paweł. – If people still think the climate has always changed, that it's not our fault, that somehow it will work out?

The minister looked at him carefully.

– Then... then it will be worse. But we hope it won't come to that.

Paweł wrote it down in his notebook. He didn't believe those words. He had seen too much to believe.

---

## V. Summer 2027, Bory Tucholskie

Tomek stood on the clearing where three years earlier he had sat with his father. Now the clearing was bigger. The trees that had surrounded it had withered and been cut down. In their place stood young seedlings that looked like children in a war — fragile, uncertain, too weak to survive.

His father was no longer alive. He had passed away in winter, after a serious illness that had come from weakness. Before he died he said:

– Don't let the forest disappear. If you can't manage, then let someone else. But don't let it disappear.

Tomek looked at the seedlings. He knew that most of them would not survive. They were too young, too weak, and the ground was too dry. But something in him told him he had to try.

He took a bucket and walked toward the ditch in which there used to be water. Now there was only mud. He poured some water into the bucket and began to water the seedlings. One, two, three.

He knew it wouldn't be enough. That all the water from the whole region would be needed to save this forest. But he had no other choice. He had to start with something. Even if it was just one tree.

---

## VI. Autumn 2027, Poznań

Professor Chojnicki received an award for the popularisation of science. In his speech he said:

– When I started working as a climatologist, I thought the hardest part would be developing mathematical models. It turned out the hardest part is convincing people that the problem is real. That there is no more time for debate. That we must act.

In the audience sat students, scientists, journalists. In the last row sat Paweł, who was writing about the award. Next to him sat Tomek, whom he had invited to tell his story.

After the ceremony Tomek walked up to the professor.

– Professor, thank you for speaking about this out loud.

– You're thanking me? You're the one watering trees in a dying forest.

– I do what I can.

– I know. And that's the most important thing. Each of us does what he can. And if we all do even a little, maybe that will be enough.

Tomek looked at his hands. They were chapped, cracked, dirty. But he felt this was not the end. That there was still hope.

– Professor, do you think we'll make it?

Chojnicki was silent for a moment.

– I don't know. But I know that if we don't try, we certainly won't make it. And if we do try, at least we'll be able to say that we did everything in our power.

Tomek nodded.

– Thank you.

– Thank you. And thank you to everyone who doesn't look away.

He left the hall and looked at the sky. It was grey, but not because of clouds. Because of the dust rising from the dried-out ground.

He knew that a long road lay ahead of them. That it would get worse before it got better. But at that moment, looking at the city where people were still living, working, loving and dreaming, he felt that it all made sense.

Because if you don't believe in the possibility of change, then why live at all?

---

## VII. Winter 2028, Bory Tucholskie

The snow fell only in January. There wasn't much of it, but Tomek welcomed it with gratitude. He knew that every drop of water was worth its weight in gold.

He stood on the clearing and looked at the seedlings. Half of them had survived. Some were already taller than him. He knew this was only the beginning. That he would have to water them for years before they became self-sufficient.

But that didn't bother him. Because in the end he understood that the forest is not only trees. It is also the people who take care of it. And that if he, Tomek, devoted his life to making sure this forest survived, then maybe others would do the same.

And maybe, in a few generations, someone else would stand on this clearing and say:

_"There used to be a desert here. And then someone planted trees."_

And that was more than he could have done alone.

---

## VIII. Spring 2029, Poland

The new government announced a programme for rebuilding water retention. Hundreds of reservoirs were built to hold back the water. Farmers began planting crops more resistant to drought. Cities began greening their squares and streets.

But that was only the beginning. The fight for the climate was to last for years. And every day was precious.

Tomek still worked in the forest. Professor Chojnicki still lectured. Paweł still wrote. Each of them did what he knew how to do. And although it wasn't easy, they felt they were heading in the right direction.

Because if something changes now, maybe it will be possible to stop what is inevitable. And if not, at least they will be able to say that they tried.

And that was the most important thing.

**THE END**
