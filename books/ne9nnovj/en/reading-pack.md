## Text

**HOLY WARRIORS**

When I was twelve years old, I first heard about the Templars. My father, fascinated by medieval history, used to tell me about them in the evenings when I couldn't sleep. He talked about white mantles with red crosses, about holy war, about how you could be a monk and a soldier at the same time. Back then I thought it was the coolest thing in the world.

Now, twenty years later, I'm sitting in my Warsaw flat, listening to a podcast by Ator and Artur Wójtowicz. The green glow of the screen, two voices interweaving in a discussion about the holy warriors of different cultures. And I feel something trembling inside me.

---
Artur talks about the Templars. About how the order was founded in 1119, after the crusaders captured Jerusalem. About how at first they were meant to protect pilgrims, and then became one of the most powerful institutions of the medieval world. Bankers, warriors, monks. All in one.

– For a modern person this may seem like a paradox – says Artur. – How can you be a monk and a soldier at the same time?

I listen and think about myself. About how strongly I believed that the world is simple. That there are good people and bad people. That you can be only one. And then I grew up and saw that everything is more complicated. That every warrior has his own demons. That every saint has his dark side.

Ator talks about relics. About how medieval people believed that saints still intervened in people's lives. That they help in illnesses, that they influence the outcomes of battles. And he laughs about the relic of the Holy Spirit in Bytom, about the seven skulls of Saint Anthony, about the forest you could make from the pieces of the Holy Cross.

– It was the most powerful weapon – says Artur. – Of course, there was production. But it was meant to give incredible protection.

And then I remember how, as a child, I wore a medallion of Our Lady. I believed it protected me from everything. That I was safe. And then I stopped believing. And suddenly I stopped feeling safe.

---
Artur talks about the samurai. About Bushido, the way of the warrior. About loyalty, honour, courage, readiness for death. About how a samurai, before battle, wrote a death poem that expressed not despair, but acceptance of passing.

– It was an acceptance of fate – he says.

I listen and think about my grandfather. He was a soldier in the Home Army. He never talked about the war. Until one day, when I was older, he told me a story. About how he saw his friends die. How he had to kill. How after the war he couldn't sleep for years. And how in the end he found peace, not in religion, but in acceptance. In the fact that he had done what he had to do. And that there's no point in despairing over the past.

– They had their rituals too – says Artur about the samurai. – Prayers in temples, visits to shrines, offerings to the Kami, Zen meditations.

And I think that maybe we all need rituals. That maybe the warriors of all times and cultures needed something to help them cope with the terror they felt before battle. That maybe it was that very need that created the Templars, the samurai, the Vikings, the mujahideen.

---
Ator and Artur move on to the Vikings. To the berserkers, who flew into a battle frenzy. They felt no pain, ignored their wounds, fought with superhuman strength.

– The berserkers helped themselves with infusions and smoke – says Artur. – They were among the first warriors in the European tradition to go into battle high.

And then Ator tells the story of the most high soldier of the Second World War. About the Finnish sniper who gulped down a whole ampoule of tablets and in two days made 200 kilometres on skis, killing several dozen Russians on the way.

– Actually, the first historically documented form of getting high were the jaguar warriors – says Ator. – The Aztecs mixed coffee seeds with fat and ate that before battle.

I listen and think about how much a human being is capable of deceiving themselves. That they need faith to survive. That they need something that will give death a meaning. And that this need is so strong that it creates whole religious systems, whole codes of honour, whole cultures.

---
Artur talks about Islam. About how warriors believed that the fate of every man is known to God even before birth. That if their hour had not yet come, they would not die, even in the fiercest battle.

– In Islamic culture, a person's life was seen as part of a divine plan – he says. – And many warriors went into battle convinced that if they were to die, they would die, and if not, they would survive.

And I think about how comforting that faith must have been. That you don't have to be afraid. That everything is planned. That you have no influence over it. That you can simply go and fight, and the rest doesn't depend on you.

---
Finally Artur talks about something that moves me the most. About the fact that in all these cultures, thousands of kilometres apart, similar rituals appeared. Amulets, sacred objects, the cult of the dead.

– A warrior didn't always disappear from the world after death – he says. – He became a new saint, a new hero. He was given guardianship. People returned to him.

And then I think about my grandfather. About how maybe he too was a holy warrior. Not in the religious sense. In the human sense. A man who did what he had to do to survive. And who left behind a story that still lives in me.

---
Ator ends the conversation, thanking Artur. I switch off the computer and look out of the window. Behind it, the lights of Warsaw flicker. Millions of people, each with their own wars, their own demons, their own saints.

And I think that maybe we don't need faith in gods to be holy warriors. Maybe faith in ourselves is enough. Faith that what we do has meaning. That we can be better. That we can survive.

I stand up, walk to the window. In the distance, against the night sky, I make out the first stars. And Artur's words come back to me about the samurai who looked at the stars before battle and wrote poems about the fleetingness of life.

Maybe that's exactly what it's about. Not about winning. Not about surviving. But about giving your life meaning. Even if that meaning exists only in your head. Even if it's only a faith that helps you through the darkest nights.

I turn away from the window. The room is quiet. Somewhere in the distance a siren sounds – maybe an ambulance, maybe the police. Someone is fighting for their life. Someone is dying. Someone is being born.

And I think that we are all holy warriors. Each in our own way. Each with our own amulets, our own rituals, our own faith. Each with the hope that what we do has meaning.

And that is enough.

**THE END**
