## Text

**THE DESKTOP ICON**

## I. THE BEGINNING

Adam sat in the waiting room of the therapist's office and looked at his hands. They were 47 years old, worn out, but still strong. In them he held a phone with a time-management app open. Today's plan: 9:00 – meeting with the therapist. 10:30 – back home. 11:00 – talk with his wife. 12:00 – decision.

— Mr Adam? — he heard a voice from behind the door. — Please, come in.

He stood up, went into the office and sat down in a comfortable armchair. Across from him sat a woman in her fifties with warm eyes and a notepad in her hand.

— Tell me what is going on — she said.

Adam took a deep breath.

— My wife and I have been together for fifteen years. We have two children, a house, a mortgage, everything it should be. And I feel that... that something has broken.

— What do you mean, "something has broken"?

— I don't know. — Adam looked at his hands. — Before, when I came home, I was glad to see her. Now... now I have the impression that I know what she will say before she even opens her mouth. And it's not that she says something bad. It's just... I know. And it wears me out.

The therapist nodded.

— What you're describing is very typical of long relationships. The longer we know someone, the more we start to assume we know what they think. And we often assume the worst.

— And what am I supposed to do about it?

— Maybe let's start with what you feel when your wife says something you've already heard. What appears in your head?

Adam closed his eyes. An image appeared in his head — an icon on a computer desktop that someone had clicked. And right away a file opened that he knew by heart.

— I think she's starting again. That she'll complain about work, about the children, about me not helping. And I know that what she says will sound like a reproach. Even if she doesn't mean it that way.

— And what do you do then?

— I shut down. Or I answer with something that hurts her. And then I apologize. And so on, over and over.

The therapist leaned forward.

— And what if you tried something different? What if, instead of assuming you know what she'll say, you asked her what she really thinks? And what if you tried to believe her?

Adam looked at her in disbelief.

— Is it that simple?

— No — she answered. — It's very hard. But maybe that's exactly what you need.

---
## II. THE DEVELOPMENT

Adam came home at 10:30. His wife, Magda, was standing in the kitchen cooking dinner. On the counter lay the ingredients for chicken broth — her favourite soup, which she cooked when she wanted to relax. The smell of broth reminded him of the beginning of their relationship, when everything was new, exciting, unpredictable.

— Hi — he said as he came in.

— Hi — she answered without turning around. — How was it at the therapist's?

— Good. She said we should try to listen to each other more.

Magda turned around and looked at him with a mixture of curiosity and unease.

— So... what? Do you want to tell me something?

Adam sat down at the table. For a moment he looked at his hands, then at her.

— I want to ask you something. And please, don't think it's a reproach. I just... I want to understand.

— What is it?

— Why have you been cooking chicken broth so often lately?

Magda looked at him in surprise.

— Because I like broth. And I thought you liked it too.

— I do. But... — Adam hesitated. — It seems to me that you cook it when you're sad. Or when you want to tell me something but don't know how.

Magda was silent for a moment. Then she walked over to the table and sat down across from him.

— You're right — she said quietly. — I cook it when I feel lonely. When I think about how our life has become so... so predictable. That I know what you'll say before you even open your mouth. And that frightens me.

Adam felt something break inside him. Not anger, not regret — something else. The realization that all along they had been doing the same thing: assuming they knew what the other was thinking instead of simply asking.

— I feel the same way — he said. — I think I know what you'll say, and I shut down. And then you shut down. And so we go around in circles.

— What are we supposed to do? — she asked.

— Maybe try to stop guessing. And start asking.

---
## III. THE TURNING POINT

A week later Adam and Magda were sitting on the sofa watching a film they had both already seen. Adam was holding the remote and suddenly, for no reason, pressed pause.

— What happened? — asked Magda.

— I wanted to ask you something. And please, don't think it's another reproach.

— All right.

— Do you ever think about separating?

Magda looked at him with a mixture of surprise and pain.

— Do you think I want to leave you?

— I don't know. That's why I'm asking.

Magda was silent for a long moment. Then she took his hand.

— I've thought about it. A few times. Not because I don't want to be with you. But because I'm afraid we'll stop understanding each other. That our life will become so predictable that there will be no room for us in it.

Adam felt like crying. Not out of sadness — out of relief that she had finally said what they had both been afraid to say out loud.

— I thought about it too — he said. — Not because I don't love you. But because I was afraid we would never change.

— And now? — she asked. — What now?

Adam looked at her and for the first time in years saw her as she had been at the beginning. Not as someone he knew by heart, but as someone he could still discover.

— Now I think maybe it's worth trying. Not pretending everything is perfect. But simply being. And asking. And listening.

Magda smiled.

— That sounds like a plan.

---
## IV. THE ENDING

A year later Adam and Magda were still together. Not everything was perfect — they still argued, they still had days when they couldn't stand the sight of each other. But they had learned one thing: to stop guessing what the other was thinking and to start asking.

One evening, when Adam came home from work, he saw Magda in the kitchen. On the counter lay the ingredients for chicken broth.

— Are you cooking broth? — he asked.

— Yes. — She turned around and looked at him. — Not because I'm sad. Because I had a good day. And I wanted to celebrate it.

Adam smiled and walked over to her. He hugged her from behind as she stirred the soup.

— That's good — he said. — Because I had a good day too.

— Will you tell me about it?

— Yes. But first I'd like to ask you something.

— What?

— Do you believe we can still change?

Magda turned around and looked into his eyes.

— I do — she answered. — Because we already are.

---
## V. EPILOGUE

A few years later Adam wrote a book about relationships. Not about how to be a perfect partner, but about how to stop pretending we know everything about the other person.

On the last page he wrote:

_"The biggest mistake I made in my marriage was thinking that I knew my wife inside out. That I knew what she would say before she even opened her mouth. Only when I stopped guessing and started asking did I see that all along she was someone I could still discover. And that was the most beautiful discovery of my life."_

When Magda read these words, she smiled.

— It's true — she said. — And I'm still discovering you.

Adam took her hand.

— And I you. And I hope I never stop.

---
**THE END**
