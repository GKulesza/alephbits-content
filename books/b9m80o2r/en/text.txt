**AUTHORITY ON APOLOGIZING**

When Anna closed the door behind Marek, she sank onto the sofa and let herself have a moment of weakness. Tears spilled from her eyes — not the first that day. Dirty lunch plates sat in the kitchen, scattered building blocks lay across the living-room floor, and upstairs her daughters — seven-year-old Marysia and three-year-old Ala — were arguing about who would get the first bath.

– Marysia! Ala! – she called out without getting up from the sofa. – Stop it! I'm coming up!

The voice in her head sounded tired. Tired, because that was exactly what she was. A whole day at work, then the shopping, then collecting the children from nursery and school, then lunch, then the cleaning, then the argument with Marek about how he had come home late again and how she couldn't cope. And about how he treated Marysia too harshly — a girl who was only just learning to read and had every right to make mistakes. And about how she was too soft, how she was raising "snowflakes," how Marysia couldn't stand losing, how Ala threw a tantrum over every single cup.

– You spoil them! – Marek had said as he left the house. – There's no consistency in you at all. I can't watch this.

Anna hadn't answered then. She didn't have the strength. She didn't have arguments either, because somewhere deep in her heart she felt that something was wrong, but she didn't know what. After all, she was a good mother. She loved her children. She gave them all her time, all of herself. So why did she feel she was failing?

---
Her phone buzzed in her hand. She glanced at the screen — a notification from YouTube. The channel she had recently started following had posted a new podcast. "How to raise a child so it grows up happy?" the title read. The guest was a psychologist who had run a family children's home for years and helped parents in crisis.

Anna pressed play, too drained to do anything else.

Mrs Maria's voice was calm but firm. Anna listened, sipping her lukewarm tea, her eyes never leaving the screen. And word by word, sentence by sentence, something inside her began to crack.

– Authority isn't built on rules and prohibitions – Mrs Maria said. – It's far sooner built on "I'm sorry." Because a parent who can say "I'm sorry," who can admit to a mistake — that parent is great.

Anna held her breath. She remembered how yesterday she had yelled at Marysia, calling her clumsy when she spilled juice on the carpet. She hadn't apologized. She had decided it wasn't necessary, that a child needed to know when she had done something wrong. And yet she had seen the tears in her daughter's eyes, seen Marysia shrink into herself as if expecting a blow.

– A sense of self-worth is built in a parent's loving eyes – Mrs Maria went on. – It's about a parent looking at their child and the child knowing it is loved, even when it isn't perfect. No matter whether it brings home an A or a D, whether it spills the juice or not.

Anna started to cry. She cried quietly, so the children wouldn't hear. She cried for herself — for the little girl she had once been, who had also wanted to be loved unconditionally. Who heard "Oh, Anna, that's fine," while her sister got smiles and warm looks. Who to this very day, even though she had finished university, had a good job and a loving family, felt she wasn't good enough.

– How should we praise? – the host asked.

– Descriptively – Mrs Maria answered. – We don't say "You're beautiful." We say "I see you chose lovely colours for that dress." We don't say "Great job." We say "I see you worked on this for a long time and you did it. It wasn't easy, but you made it."

Anna kept listening. She heard about nurseries — that until the age of three a child needs one main caregiver, because that is when attachment patterns form. She heard that conflict in a marriage is not bad, that what matters is how it is resolved. She heard that if we want to raise our children well, we have to work on ourselves and on our marriage, because that is the model our children grow up with.

– What we think about ourselves has its beginnings in our family – Mrs Maria said. – We carry our parents with us — their fears, their fascinations. We know little about ourselves until we enter a relationship and start discovering who we are.

Anna thought about Marek. About how he, too, carried his own fears. About how his father had been stern and demanding and had never once praised him. And how Marek, wanting to be a better father, had become exactly that — stern and demanding, afraid that if he loosened the reins his children would grow into "softies." And that he, too, needed to hear that he was good enough.

---
When the children finally fell asleep, Anna sat down in the living room. Marek had come home but had shut himself in his room. Anna took a deep breath and went to him.

– Marek – she said softly. – We need to talk.

– I don't have the energy for another fight – he replied without looking up.

– I don't want to fight. I want to apologize.

Marek looked at her in surprise.

– Apologize? For what?

– For not listening to you. For not trying to understand why you react the way you do. And — I didn't see it myself — for criticizing you in front of the children.

Marek was silent. Anna sat down beside him.

– I listened to a podcast today – she said. – They talked about how authority is built on apologizing. How children need to see that their parents make mistakes too, and admit to them. And that if we want our children to feel their own worth, we have to be a safe example for them.

Marek looked at her.

– So what? – he asked. – Does that mean I should stop expecting anything of them? Let them do whatever they want?

– No. It means we should expect things differently. With respect. We should say "Sweetheart, I can't agree with what you're doing, because it's hurting you" — not "You're hopeless." We should praise them descriptively, not judge them. And we should apologize when we let them down.

Marek rested his head in his hands.

– I don't know how – he said quietly. – My father never apologized to me. He never said "I raised you well." He never said "I love you." And I don't want to be like him. But I don't know how to be any different.

Anna put her hand on his shoulder.

– We can learn. Together.

---
The next morning, at breakfast, Marysia spilled her milk. Her eyes filled with tears before anyone had a chance to say a word.

– I'm sorry – she whispered. – I'm sorry, Mummy.

Anna took a deep breath. In the past she would have said "Again? How many times have I told you to be careful?" Instead, she went over to Marysia, hugged her and said:

– I can see you're upset. It's not nice when something spills, is it? But nothing happened. We'll get a cloth, wipe it up, and everything will be fine. Would you like to help me?

Marysia looked at her, surprised. She nodded. Together they wiped up the milk. Then Anna added:

– I can see you tried so hard to pour your own milk. It wasn't easy, but you tried. I'm proud of you.

Marysia smiled. For the first time in many days.

Marek watched it all from the corner. He said nothing. But Anna saw something in his eyes that she hadn't seen in a long time — reflection.

---
A few days later, Marek came home from work early. Marysia was showing him a drawing she had made at school.

– Look, Daddy! This is our family.

Marek looked at the drawing. Everyone was smiling. Even the dog.

– It's beautiful – he said. And then, to Anna's surprise, he added: – I can see you worked on this for a long time. There are details here I didn't notice before — like those little flowers in the background. You can draw things other people would never notice.

Marysia beamed.

Anna went over to her husband and whispered:

– I can see you're learning to praise.

– So are you – he answered. – And I can see it works.

---
That evening, while the children slept, Anna and Marek sat on the sofa. Marek took her hand.

– I'm sorry – he said. – For criticizing you. For not seeing that you give everything you have. And for sometimes being too hard on Marysia. It's not her fault that I don't know any other way. But I want to learn.

Anna leaned into him.

– I'm sorry too – she said. – For not being able to listen to you. For not seeing that you need support as well. And for sometimes speaking badly about you in front of the children. That was a mistake.

– We'll learn – said Marek.

– Together.

---
A month later, Anna sat down at her laptop to write a message to the author of the podcast. She wanted to say thank you. For finding the courage to change her life. For understanding that raising children is not training, but a relationship. And that the greatest gift she could give her children was a sense of their own worth — which is built in a parent's loving eyes.

She thought about what Mrs Maria had said: "Love is the time we give." And she understood that it wasn't about being a perfect parent. It was about being present. And about trying again, every single day.

– Mummy! – Marysia called from her room. – Come and read me a bedtime story?

Anna closed her laptop.

– I'm coming, sweetheart.

She got up, walked to the door and looked at her children. Marysia lay in bed with a book on her lap. Ala was cuddling her teddy bear.

Anna sat down between them.

– What story today?

– The dragon who didn't know how to say sorry – said Marysia.

Anna smiled. She opened the book and began to read.

And in her head the words that had changed everything kept echoing: "Authority isn't built on rules and prohibitions. It's far sooner built on 'I'm sorry.'"

And she knew it was true. Because in those three phrases — in "I love you," in "you matter," and in "I'm sorry" — lay everything children need to be happy.

And Anna, who for years had thought she had to be perfect, finally understood: it was enough to be real.

**THE END**
