**THE VICTIMS**

## I. THE BEGINNING

Marek Kowalski stood in the middle of the empty room of his apartment in Warsaw and looked at the box he had found in the basement. It was old, dusty, tied with a string that fell apart in his hands. Inside were letters – hundreds of letters, written by hand, in Polish and in Ukrainian.

His grandfather, who had died a year earlier, had never talked about the war. When Marek asked, he would only answer: "Those were difficult times. It's not worth going back."

Now Marek was holding in his hands proof that his grandfather had lied. Or at least – that he had not told the whole truth.

The first letter was dated 1943. It was addressed to "Dear Janku" – signed by someone whose surname Marek recognized as Ukrainian. The letter began with the words:

_"Janku, I don't know if this letter will reach you. Please, forgive me. What is happening is terrible. They are forcing us to do things we don't want to do. They say that if we don't go, they will kill our families. Please, don't think badly of me."_

Marek read the letter three times. Then he put it down and looked at the rest of the box. There were dozens of similar letters – testimonies of fear, compassion, but also horror. Descriptions of the massacres that took place in Volhynia, in Eastern Lesser Poland, in towns whose names he knew only from history textbooks.

He closed his eyes. In his mind a memory of his grandfather appeared – an elderly man with calm eyes, who never raised his voice, never talked about the war. And now Marek understood that his grandfather had carried a burden he could not bear.

---

## II. THE DEVELOPMENT

A month later Marek sat on a train heading east. In his backpack he had copies of the letters, a notebook, and one question that would not let him rest: _Who was the man who wrote that first letter? Did he survive? Did he regret it?_

The train passed Lublin, then Zamość, then the border. The landscape outside the window changed – from Polish fields and forests to Ukrainian steppes and hills. Marek watched it all and thought about the people who, years before, had watched the same landscape, but with terror, not curiosity.

In Lutsk, at the station, an older man with grey hair and tired eyes was waiting for him. His name was Ołeksandr and he was a historian from the local university.

– Mr Marek? – he asked, walking up. – I got your e-mail. Please, let's go to the café. I'll tell you what I know.

---

## III. THE TURNING POINT

The café in Lutsk was small, cosy, smelling of coffee and cakes. Ołeksandr ordered tea and for a moment looked at Marek in silence.

– You're looking for the truth about Volhynia? – he asked at last.

– I'm looking for an answer to the question of who the man who wrote this letter was. – Marek placed a copy on the table. – Do you know who he is?

Ołeksandr took the letter and read it carefully. Then he put it back on the table and looked at Marek with a mixture of sadness and understanding.

– I know that surname. He was my grandfather.

Marek felt a chill run down his spine.

– Your grandfather?

– Yes. His name was Iwan. He was a peasant from a village near Lutsk. When the war broke out, he was conscripted into the UPA. He didn't want to, but he had no choice. They threatened that if he didn't go, they would kill his family. – Ołeksandr paused, sipping his tea. – My grandfather never talked about the war. Only at the end of his life, when he was already old and ill, did he tell me what had happened. He cried when he spoke. He said he had seen things he would never forget for the rest of his life. And that he would never forgive himself for not doing anything to prevent it.

Marek was silent. In his mind a memory of his grandfather appeared, who also never talked about the war. And suddenly he understood that both grandfathers had carried the same burden – the burden of memories they could not cast out of themselves.

– What happened to your grandfather? – he asked at last.

– He survived the war. But he didn't survive what he saw. – Ołeksandr looked at him with pain. – After the war he never returned to a normal life. He kept thinking about those who had died. About Poles, about Ukrainians, about everyone who lost their lives in that madness. And he died with a sense of guilt that he had not done anything to save them.

---

## IV. THE CONCLUSION

A year later Marek returned to Poland. He brought with him notes, memories, and a story that had changed his view of the world. He already knew that there were no simple answers to the question of Volhynia. That both Poles and Ukrainians had their grievances, their traumas, their stories that were hard to reconcile.

But he also knew something else – that memory does not have to be a tool of division. It can be a bridge.

He sat down at his desk and opened the notebook. On the first page he wrote:

_"There is nothing worse than victims who try to choose their own executioners."_

For a moment he looked at those words. Then he began to write.

He told the story of two grandfathers – a Polish one and a Ukrainian one, who had never met, but carried the same pain. He wrote about the letter that connected two families. He wrote about the fact that the truth about Volhynia is not black and white – that it contains victims and perpetrators, but also people who were forced to do things they did not want to do.

At the end he wrote:

_"The memory of Volhynia should unite us, not divide us. Not in order to forget. But in order to understand."_

He sent the text to the editorial office. He didn't know whether it would be published. But he knew he had to write it.

Because in the end he understood that the truth about the past is not just history. It is also a responsibility.

---

## V. THE EPILOGUE

A few months later Marek received a letter from Ukraine. The envelope was addressed by hand, without a sender. He opened it and read:

_"Mr Marek, I read your article. Thank you for writing it. My grandfather also never talked about the war. I thought it was because he didn't want to remember. But now I know – he remembered only too well. Thank you for showing that even in the worst of times one can preserve one's humanity."_

Marek put the letter down and looked out of the window. Outside the sun was shining, and he felt that at last he had done something that mattered.

He did not change the past. But he helped others understand that the past does not have to be a burden that crushes us. It can be a lesson that teaches us.

He stood up, took his coat and left the house. Ahead of him there were many questions to which he did not know the answers. But he knew he would look for them.

Because the truth always deserves to be known.
