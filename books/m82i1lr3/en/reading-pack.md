## Text

**THE BLACK BOX**


## I. Control

When Robert opened his eyes at 5:47, the first thing he did was check his phone. Not to see if anyone had written to him. To check whether something had changed. Whether the world was still the way it should be. Whether his plans for today were still current. Whether, by any chance, something had happened that he hadn't foreseen.

For the past twelve years, Robert's life had been one long controlled sequence. He got up at the same time, drank coffee at the same time, read reports at the same time, left for work at the same time. His calendar was written out to the nearest five minutes. His goals were measurable, achievable, realistic and time-bound. Everything else was chaos. And chaos was the enemy.

— You need to relax — his wife, Magda, told him every time she saw him clench his jaw at breakfast. — It's just work.

— It's not just work — he replied. — It's life. And life has to be controlled.

Magda didn't understand. Magda was an artist. She painted pictures that were never finished, that changed while being made, that lived their own lives. For her, control was a prison. For him, it was the only way not to go mad.

He didn't yet know that in a moment he would lose everything he controlled. And that it would be the best thing that could have happened to him.

---

## II. The Phone Call

The job interview took place over Zoom at 10:15. Robert was wearing a shirt he hadn't taken off since yesterday, and a face that hadn't seen a razor in three days. If someone had told him a year ago that he would look like this at a job interview, he would have thought it was a joke. But a year ago he had a company. A year ago he had control.

Now he had nothing.

— Mr Robert — said the recruiter, looking at him from the other side of the screen. — You have excellent references. But I have to ask: why did you leave your previous job?

Robert smiled. That smile he had learned to show when he didn't want to show the truth.

— It was a strategic decision.

— I understand. And what, in your opinion, was your biggest mistake in that company?

He hesitated. The biggest mistake? He'd been thinking about it for the past six months. He thought about it every night when he couldn't sleep, every morning when he woke up with anxiety, every afternoon when he looked at his empty account.

— I think — he said at last — that I tried to control things that can't be controlled. And that killed my company.

The recruiter nodded, wrote something down and moved on to the next question.

Robert didn't tell her the whole truth. He didn't tell her that his company hadn't fallen because of a bad market. It had fallen because of him. Because of his need to control everything — employees, clients, suppliers, even the weather. Because of his conviction that if he was careful enough, disciplined enough, tough enough, everything would work out the way it should.

But the world didn't work the way it should. The world was chaotic, unpredictable, indifferent to Robert's plans. And in the end he understood that.

Too late.

---

## III. The Journey

He was going home late in the evening. He no longer had a car — he had sold it three months ago to pay off part of his debts. Now he took a train that was always late and a tram that was always crowded. Once that would have infuriated him. Now it almost didn't bother him.

He sat in the compartment and looked through the window at the housing estates passing by, at the lights flashing in the darkness, at the people getting on and off as if they had nowhere to go. And suddenly he realised something he hadn't understood his whole life.

He also had nowhere to go. For twelve years he'd thought he had a goal, that he had a plan, that he knew where he was heading. But really he had only been running. Running from chaos, from uncertainty, from what he couldn't control. And that flight had led him to where he was now — without a company, without money, without certainty about tomorrow.

He got off the train and walked towards home. He passed shops that were already closed, streetlights casting yellow light on the pavements, and dogs barking from behind fences. It was all the same as yesterday. And the same as a year ago. Only he had changed.

He entered the flat. Magda was sitting in the living room, in front of her a cup of tea that had long gone cold. She looked at him and smiled.

— So? — she asked.

— I don't know. Maybe.

— That's good.

— You always say it's good, even when it's bad.

— Because it's always good. Even when it seems bad.

Robert sat down beside her and for a moment looked at her hands. They were stained with paint, as always. Magda never stopped painting. Even when she had no money for canvases, she painted on old sheets of paper, on pieces of wood, on anything she found.

— How do you do it? — he asked.

— Do what?

— Stay calm all the time. Never get upset.

Magda looked at him.

— I do get upset. I just don't show it.

— And how do you cope?

— I feel it. And I let it go.

Robert shook his head.

— I can't let go. I hold on all the time.

— I know. And that's why you're so tired.

---

## IV. The Book

A few days later Robert received a parcel. It was from Agnieszka, his sister, who had lived in the mountains for years and did something Robert had never fully understood. She called it "working with emotions".

In the parcel there was a book. The title read: "Let Go and See What Remains".

Robert put it aside. He had no time for reading. He had job interviews, he had debts, he had a life that demanded action. But in the evening, when he couldn't sleep, he reached for it.

_"Most people think manifestation is positive thinking"_ — he read. _"But that's not true. Manifestation is working with the body, with emotions and with releasing the fear that blocks us from receiving what we desire."_

He turned the page.

_"If you hold dark scenarios in your mind, they will strive to manifest. If you keep fear inside you, you attract situations that confirm that fear."_

Robert put the book down and closed his eyes. He remembered all the situations in which he had worried about something and it had happened. Not because he had foreseen the future. Because he had created it himself. With his fear, his control, his conviction that if he wasn't careful, something bad would happen.

And suddenly he understood that what he had called control was only an escape. An escape from fear, from uncertainty, from life. And that escape had led him to the place he was in now.

He started reading on.

_"To manifest something, you must first release the fear. You have to sit with it, feel it, let it flow through you. And only then can you begin to create."_

---

## V. The Practice

The next morning Robert tried. He sat down on the living room floor, closed his eyes and began to breathe. For the first few minutes he thought about everything — the job interviews, the debts, the fact that he had no work, no money, no future. The thoughts circled round and round, like something gone mad.

And then, at some point, he did what he had learned from the book. Instead of fighting the thoughts, he let them be. Instead of trying to control them, he observed them. And then he felt something strange — the fear he had carried inside for years began to dissolve.

It didn't disappear right away. It didn't disappear in one session. But with each day, with each practice, it grew smaller. And in the place where the fear had been, space began to appear. And in that space, for the first time in years, Robert felt peace.

It wasn't the peace that comes when everything is under control. It was a different peace. The peace that comes when you accept that you have no control. And that that is all right.

---

## VI. The Conversation

Two weeks later Robert got a job. Not the one he had been looking for — less paid, less prestigious, less demanding. But one that gave him time. Time for practice, for family, for being.

Magda looked at him and smiled.

— You're different — she said.

— Different how?

— Calmer. As if you'd stopped being afraid.

Robert thought for a moment.

— Because I did stop — he replied. — I stopped being afraid that something would go wrong. And you know what? Nothing has changed. The world keeps turning. Only I changed.

Magda took his hand.

— That's called letting go — she said.

— I know. And it's the best thing I've ever done.

**THE END**
