## Text

**BOLIEWICZ**


When Antoni Dudek first saw Lech Wałęsa live, he was twelve years old. It was 1983; Wałęsa had just received the Nobel Peace Prize, and martial law was still in force in Poland. Dudek, like millions of other Polish children, watched on television the pictures of the leader of Solidarity – that modest electrician from Gdańsk who had thrown down a challenge to the communist empire. For him, as for a whole generation, Wałęsa was a hero. He was a symbol of hope, of resistance, of the dream of freedom.

Forty years later Dudek sits in the studio of the podcast "Historia jakiej nie znacie" (A History You Don't Know) and talks about Wałęsa in a way that would have been unthinkable a dozen or so years earlier. He talks about the Bolek file, about informing on colleagues, about a presidency that was a failure. But he also says that Wałęsa is a complex figure – seen neither through rose-tinted nor through black glasses.

– I would only appeal for people not to look at Wałęsa exclusively through rose-tinted or exclusively through black glasses – he says. – And the same goes for all other historical figures.


**The hero**

In 1980 Wałęsa was someone chosen by history, though he himself wasn't fully prepared for it. A shipyard worker, a man of little education but incredible charisma. When in August 1980 he took the lead of the strike at the Gdańsk Shipyard, he had no plan for Poland. He had only one thing: not to be broken.

– He starts to stand up for himself fairly quickly, already in 1972 – says Dudek. – He says: "But listen, you're not doing this right. Pass it on to Comrade Gierek that this or that." He begins to think that those SB officers are representatives of the highest party leadership from Warsaw, and that he will conduct some negotiations with them here.

That was his greatest strength and his greatest weakness. Wałęsa believed that you could reach an understanding with the communists. That you could convince them, that you could negotiate with them. And throughout the 1980s he tried to do just that – balancing between radicalism and conciliation, between struggle and dialogue.

For the authorities he was a convenient leader. Moderate, predictable, ready to talk. For many Solidarity activists – a traitor who too readily compromised. For the West – a symbol of resistance that drew the world's attention.

– If the Western correspondents had gone to Gdańsk rather than to Szczecin, the star would have been Marian Jurczyk – says Dudek. – As it was, Wałęsa signed with Jagielski, and Jurczyk with Barcikowski. And nobody outside Szczecin remembers that.


**Bolek**

Wałęsa had his dark side. In 1970, after the December protests, he was recruited by the Security Service (SB) as a secret collaborator under the pseudonym "Bolek". For several years he informed on colleagues, reporting planned demonstrations, who wanted to protest and who was backing out.

– In the first half of the 1970s Wałęsa collaborated with the security service – says Dudek. – We have hard evidence that could not be undermined in Wałęsa's case. Handwriting analyses were made which confirm that Wałęsa collaborated as the secret collaborator Bolek.

– Does that erase Wałęsa's entire historical role? – asks the host.

– In my opinion, no – answers Dudek. – Of course it weighs on him, but it weighs on him mainly in the eyes of those who demand heroes of bronze. I, on the other hand, think that we should assess the balance of a life. And the balance of Wałęsa's life is positive because of the 1980s.

It is not a simple assessment, however. Wałęsa informed about his colleagues, which led to dismissals and harassment. But there is no evidence that his reports led to arrests. The Gierek team, as Dudek says, "generally wanted to know rather than to strike".

– If the Gierek team had used Bierut's method – arrest, hand down sentences, prisons – then Wałęsa's story would have been much blacker. But they didn't do that.


**The president**

When Lech Wałęsa won the presidential election in 1990, Poland believed that her greatest living hero would lead her into a new era. He was the first president elected in a popular vote, the first who did not come from the communist nomenklatura. Behind him were ten years of fighting the system, a Nobel Prize, the support of millions. It seemed that nothing could stop him.

And yet he failed.

– Wałęsa's presidency was unsuccessful – says Dudek bluntly. – He completely failed to understand the role of the president in that system. He thought the president was supposed to govern on a day-to-day basis. And you could say that only when he became president did he learn that the government is really run by the prime minister.

Wałęsa was not prepared to wield power. He had no political education, he didn't understand the constitution, he didn't know how parliamentary democracy worked. His method was acting on instinct rather than knowledge. And although that worked in the 1980s, in the 1990s it proved tragic in its consequences.

– He had this idea that he should also be prime minister – says Dudek. – After the 1991 elections Wałęsa voiced the view that the best thing would be for him to become prime minister as well. And in this way he started a great constitutional education for the Poles, making them aware that we have this strange system in which we have both a president and a prime minister.

Wałęsa not only failed to understand the system, but actively destabilised it. His conflicts with successive prime ministers, his attempts to take over the government's powers, his lack of respect for procedures – all of this created chaos that affected the whole country. Poland in the 1990s was politically unstable, and Wałęsa was one of the main causes of it.

– Were it not for Wałęsa's determination to be a president elected by the nation, rather than like Jaruzelski, elected by the National Assembly, we might not have popular presidential elections at all – says Dudek. – But the fact that we have them doesn't mean it's a good solution.


**The fall**

Wałęsa lost the presidential election in 1995 to Aleksander Kwaśniewski. For him it was a blow from which he never recovered. But for Poland it was the beginning of the end of his political career.

– He ends his career with 1% support in the next presidential election – says Dudek. – A man who was the leader of a legendary social movement, who was a president elected in a popular vote, ends his career with 1% support. And now it's worth asking how it happened that he fell from such a high horse.

Wałęsa was unable to admit to anything. He didn't admit to Bolek, he didn't admit to the mistakes of his presidency, he didn't admit to anything. Instead, the more he was criticised, the more he dug in. His statements became more and more absurd, more and more megalomaniacal. And people saw it.

– I actually think that the Bolek file didn't hurt Wałęsa the most, nor did all those attacks – says Dudek. – Wałęsa hurt himself the most by his behaviour and statements after he left the office of president. That megalomania he exhibited constantly, that inability to admit to anything, in fact hurt him very much.


**Bolek versus Bolek**

The most fascinating thing about Wałęsa's story is how he destroyed himself. If in the 1990s he had stood in front of the camera and said: "Yes, I was Bolek. I was weak, I made a mistake, but then I fought for free Poland for years" – he would probably have been forgiven. Poles are a forgiving nation, especially towards those who can admit their guilt.

But Wałęsa didn't do that. Instead he dug in his heels. He hired pseudo-graphologists to prove that the signatures on the files were forged. He claimed it was all a conspiracy, that it was Kiszczak, that it was the Kaczyńskis, that it was everyone except him. And in this way he made the people who might have defended him turn away from him.

– That's a question for psychologists – says Dudek. – Whether he repressed it, or believed it would never come out. But we know that from 1992, from the fall of the Olszewski government, the matter was made public and dragged on for years.

The fall of the Olszewski government is one of the most controversial episodes in the history of the Third Republic. Olszewski wanted to reveal the list of SB agents, including – as it later turned out – Wałęsa's name. Wałęsa, together with part of the deputies, brought about the overthrow of that government. And in this way he made enemies for life.

– Wałęsa got entangled in politics – says Dudek. – And then that earliest past caught up with him. The Bolek affair, which he was unable to face.


**The legacy**

Today Wałęsa is over eighty years old. His statements are more and more chaotic, more and more detached from reality. Recently he stated that it was he who had inspired Donald Trump to run for president. That sentence drew a wave of laughter, but also of sadness. Here is a man who was once the pride of the nation, today he is a hero of memes.

– He already has meme potential – says Dudek. – I often have the opportunity to comment on some next statement by Wałęsa, and I decline, because I simply don't want to mock him anymore. What he does now arouses pity.

And yet – despite everything – Wałęsa remains a figure who cannot be dismissed. Without him there would have been no Solidarity. Without Solidarity there would have been no Round Table. Without the Round Table there would have been no free Poland. And although his presidency was unsuccessful, although his past is dark, his services to Poland are undeniable.

– The balance of Wałęsa's life is positive because of the 1980s – says Dudek. – I think his presidency, frankly speaking, is much more of a problem for Poland than his informing on his shipyard colleagues in the first half of the 1970s. But we dwell less on Wałęsa's presidency and more on the Bolek affair.

Wałęsa is a figure who forces us to ask a difficult question: how should we assess heroes? Should we look at them only through their great deeds, ignoring their small sins? Or should we condemn them for every mistake, forgetting what good they did?

– Don't look at Wałęsa exclusively through rose-tinted or exclusively through black glasses – Dudek appeals. – And the same goes for all other historical figures.


**Conclusion**

Wałęsa's story is the story of the Poles. Of their dreams of freedom, their disappointments, their need for heroes. It is also the story of how hard it is to be human in times when you are expected to be a saint.

Wałęsa was a hero. He was also an agent. He was a president. He was also a failure. He was a leader. He was also a man who could not admit to a mistake. All of that is true. And all of it is part of the same story.

– I would only appeal for people not to look at Wałęsa exclusively through rose-tinted or exclusively through black glasses – Dudek repeats. – And the same goes for all other historical figures.

Because in history, as in life, there are no flawless people. There are only people who at some point face a choice. And sometimes they choose well. And sometimes badly. And for that we must judge them – but with reason, not with emotion.

Wałęsa, Bolek, president, man. It is all one. And it all together forms a picture that – despite everything – deserves attention. Because this is our history. And it is our responsibility to understand it well.


**THE END**
