## Text

**Tales from a Past Life**

Anna always said that her son had been born old. Not physically – he was a small, fair-haired boy with honey-coloured eyes – but in the way he looked at the world. When other three-year-olds ran around the playground, screaming and tripping over their own feet, Franek sat on the bench and watched. He looked at the clouds, at the leaves, at the passing cars. Sometimes he raised his hand, as if he wanted to catch something nobody else could see.

The first time it happened was on a Sunday. They were sitting in the living room – she with coffee, her husband Piotr with a newspaper, Franek on the carpet with his blocks. The television was showing a programme about the Second World War. On the screen appeared an old bomber, black and white, with a female figure painted on its fuselage.

Franek raised his head.

– My plane was burning – he said calmly. – It was a Corsair. I didn't make it back to the carrier.

Anna froze with the cup halfway to her lips. Piotr lowered his newspaper.

– What did you say? – she asked, trying to make her voice sound normal.

– My plane. My name was Daniel. I served on the Natoma Bay. It was a big ship. I had a friend, his name was Jack. Jack liked to play cards. He always lost.

He put down the blocks and went back to playing, as if he had just told them what he had eaten for breakfast.

That night Anna didn't sleep. She sat at the computer until three in the morning, typing phrases she had never heard of into Google. _Natoma Bay. Battle of Iwo Jima. Corsair shot down 1945._ When she found the list of pilots who had fallen in that battle, the name Daniel Rakowiecki jumped out of the screen at her like a punch. Born in Detroit, of Polish descent. Decorated posthumously. Died on 21 February 1945.

Then other things came. Night screams. Drawings of planes, always the same ones, always with flames at the right wing. And the questions – the most unsettling ones. "Mum, why am I small now? Why don't you remember when I was big?"

Anna tried to rationalise. Maybe he had seen a video on YouTube. Maybe someone at nursery school had been telling stories. Maybe he just had an imagination more vivid than other children's. But where did he learn the word _carrier_? How did he know that the Corsair had a tendency to overheat its engine at high altitudes? How the hell did he know the name Jack Larsen, the second pilot, whose name Anna later found in the military archives, buried on the third page of the list of the fallen?

That was the beginning of her private investigation.

---
Six months later she was sitting in the office of Doctor Michał Kosakowski at Adam Mickiewicz University in Poznań. She had been referred to him by a psychologist friend who had said: "He deals with things like this. Scientifically. Without the woo."

The office was small, cluttered with books. On the desk stood a mug with the words _Extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence_. Kosakowski was younger than she had expected – maybe forty, maybe less. He had the calm, inquiring look of someone who had already heard hundreds of such stories and was in no hurry to judge.

She told him everything. About the plane. About the nightmares. About the list of pilots. About how Franek had one day looked at a photo of a carrier in a book and said: "I was stationed here, but that was before the modernisation." About how a child couldn't have known that, because the photo came from 1944 and the modernisation wasn't mentioned in any popular source.

Kosakowski listened, without interrupting. Then he leaned back in his chair.

– Mrs Anna, I'd like to ask you one question. Before Franek started telling these stories, had he ever been with you to an aviation museum? Or watched a programme about Second World War battles? Maybe there were books or albums at home?

Anna hesitated.

– My father was a pilot. A civilian one, not military. But he had old albums with photos of planes. He kept them in the attic. Franek may have seen them once or twice, when we visited my parents.

Kosakowski nodded.

– What you're describing sounds extraordinary. But I'd like to propose an alternative explanation. Not to undermine your experience, but to show how powerful our minds can be.

And he began to explain. He talked about cryptomnesia – a phenomenon in which the brain forgets the source of information but keeps the information itself. About how young children have a particularly plastic memory and weaker ability to tell apart what they have heard from what they have experienced.

– Imagine – he said, drawing a diagram on a piece of paper – that Franek saw his grandfather's albums. Maybe he watched a programme on television. Maybe he overheard an adult conversation. All those scraps of information – the name of a pilot of Polish descent, the name of the ship, the technical details of the plane – fell into his mind. And then, in the process of memory reconstruction, they were assembled into one coherent story. A story that feels true because it is so detailed, so vivid.

– But where did he get Jack's name? – asked Anna. – The second pilot? I myself only found that name after hours of digging through the archives.

Kosakowski put down the pen.

– And how do you know he knew the name earlier? Can you be sure you first heard it from him? Or did you first find it in the archives, and then – in the course of a conversation with Franek – a question came up: "And what was your friend's name?" And then the boy answered "Jack". Because Jack is a common name. Because he had heard it somewhere. Because you, even unconsciously, may have prompted him.

Anna felt something tighten into a knot inside her. She wanted to deny it. But she couldn't. Because it was true – first came the archive. Then the conversation. And then everything blurred into one narrative.

– That's called confirmation bias – Kosakowski continued. – When something seems true to us, we begin selectively collecting evidence that it is. Every detail that fits the story – for example, the fact that Franek likes drawing planes – becomes confirmation. Every detail that doesn't fit – for example, the fact that Daniel Rakowiecki died somewhere other than where Franek described – is ignored. Or rationalised: "Maybe the boy remembers only part, the rest has faded."

Silence fell. Anna looked at her hands. At her wedding ring. At the scar from an iron burn, a souvenir from the time when she herself was a child.

– So what am I supposed to do? – she asked. – Pretend nothing happened?

– No. Listen to the child. Don't ignore his stories. If something worries him – nightmares, fears – talk to him about it. But also remember that a child's imagination is extremely powerful. That what seems like a memory may be a story the mind told itself. And that there is nothing wrong with that – it's part of development. Imaginary friends also disappear eventually. These stories will disappear too.

Anna returned home late in the evening. Franek was already asleep in his bed, with a stuffed plane under his arm – a gift from his grandfather, who couldn't resist. Piotr was waiting for her in the kitchen.

– So? – he asked.

She sat down at the table. She told him about cryptomnesia, about memory reconstruction, about studies on children in India and Lebanon, where the cultural expectation of reincarnation made such stories appear more often. About how hypnosis – which some recommended as a way to recover memories – can in fact create false memories, instead of revealing the truth.

– So it was all... made up? – asked Piotr.

– Not made up. – Anna shook her head. – Real for him. His brain assembled that story from the pieces it found around him – from his grandfather's albums, from television, from our conversations. He really believes it. Just as we believe our childhood memories, though half of them never happened.

That night, when Piotr had fallen asleep, Anna got up and went to Franek's room. The boy was sleeping peacefully, without nightmares, without screams. He was breathing evenly, steadily, as if no memories – true or false – had any power over him.

She sat on the edge of the bed and looked at him in the half-darkness. His hands, small and plump, rested on the duvet. Hands that drew planes. That pointed on a map to the place of a battle he should have known nothing about. That had once – perhaps – held the controls of a fighter over the Pacific.

And perhaps not. Perhaps they had simply held crayons and watched too many films with grandpa.

She bent down and kissed his forehead.

– Whoever you were – she whispered – now you are mine.

Franek stirred in his sleep. His lips curled into a smile, as if he were dreaming of flying. Or perhaps of something entirely different – of blocks, of the neighbour's dog, of tomorrow's breakfast. Of things that need no explanation.

Anna stayed with him a little longer. Then she got up and returned to the bedroom. Outside the window the moon was shining – the same moon that had seen everything: wars and births, disasters and smiles, true stories and the stories a brain tells itself to make sense of the world.

And somewhere on the border between the one and the other, between memory and imagination, between what was and what might have been – a lone Corsair was flying, leaving behind a contrail that slowly dissolved into the darkness.

**THE END**
