## Text

**THE RETURN**

## I. THE BEGINNING

Anastazja woke up at 5:47, even though her alarm was set for 6:15. She lay in the darkness of her apartment in Almaty, hearing the distant barking of dogs and the hum of a street waking to life. Outside the window, Kazakhstan greeted her with a grey, wintry sky.

She knew the answer would come today. Three weeks ago she had submitted her visa application to Poland. Three weeks of preparations, documents, stress. Three weeks in which her life hung by a thread.

She got up, drank some coffee, and sat down at the computer. She opened her inbox. One message from the embassy.

_"Refused. Incorrectly stated purpose of travel."_

She read it three times. Then she closed the laptop and stared at the wall for a long while.

She didn't cry. She didn't scream. She just sat in silence, feeling something inside her break. But not what she had expected. Not hope. Something older. Deeper.

She remembered what her therapist had once told her: _"When something difficult happens, it is worth stopping for a moment. Not to look for those to blame, but to ask yourself the right question."*

Anastazja closed her eyes. And then, for the first time in years, she heard her own thoughts:

_"Why am I so afraid? After all, this isn't the first time something hasn't worked out for me. It isn't the first time the world has told me 'no'. And yet it hurts just the same."*

She opened her eyes. A childhood memory appeared in her mind — her father, who always repeated: _"You are Polish, my little girl. You carry Poland in your heart, not in your papers."*

But now the papers were saying something else.

---

## II. THE DEVELOPMENT

A week later Anastazja sat in a café in the centre of Almaty. Her coffee was going cold in front of her, and she stared at her phone, where a page of job offers in Poland was open. Hundreds of offers. Thousands of possibilities. And yet everything came down to one thing — to someone at the embassy putting a stamp.

— Excuse me, is this seat free?

She looked up. Before her stood a woman around forty — short red hair, glasses with thick frames, a smile that was both warm and attentive.

— Yes, of course — she answered, moving her bag aside.

The woman sat down, ordered tea, and for a while looked at her with curiosity.

— Anastazja, right? — she asked. — Marek told me about you. He said you have a problem with your visa.

— You know Marek?

— I know a lot of people. — She smiled. — My name is Justyna. I'm... a musician. And sometimes I talk about things that are hard to name.

Anastazja looked at her in surprise.

— What kind of things?

Justyna leaned forward.

— About the fact that we are not a body. About the fact that our life is not only papers, borders and formalities. About the fact that each of us has something inside that no visa can touch. — She paused, sipping her tea. — You know, when I was five, I had a near-death experience. I saw a light. I felt a love that can't be described in words. And I came back convinced that what we do here is only a foretaste of something greater.

Anastazja felt something stir inside her.

— I have that feeling too — she said quietly. — That Poland isn't only a country. It's... a home. Even if I've never lived there. Even if the authorities say 'no'.

Justyna looked at her kindly.

— That is exactly your answer. Not in the papers. In you.

---

## III. THE TURNING POINT

A month later Anastazja returned to Kazakhstan after another attempt to sort out her documents. This time she no longer felt the same tension. Something inside her had changed. Since the conversation with Justyna, since her words about the soul, about returning to oneself, she had started to look at her life differently.

She sat down at her desk and opened a notebook. On the first page she wrote:

_"Who am I?"*

For a moment she looked at the question. Then she began to write:

_"I am the daughter of a Polish woman and a Kazakh man. I speak Polish with an accent, but I feel this language in my heart. I love Polish literature, Polish music, Polish food. I feel that Poland is my home, even if I've never lived there."*

She closed her eyes. And then she felt something she hadn't felt in years — peace. Not the false peace imposed by daily routine, but a real, deep peace flowing from the very centre of her being.

She remembered Justyna's words: _"The ego is not an enemy. It is our distinctiveness and uniqueness. It is what makes us ourselves and not someone else. But the ego must not dominate us. We have to learn to talk to it."*

Anastazja opened her eyes and looked in the mirror. In her reflection she saw a woman who had spent her whole life seeking acceptance from outside. Who thought that if she got a visa, she would finally be a "real Polish woman". Who believed that documents defined her identity.

And then she understood that this wasn't true.

— I don't need a visa to be Polish — she said to her reflection. — I am Polish. I always was. And no one can take that away from me.

She stood up, took her phone and wrote to Justyna:

_"Today I understood what you were talking about. I don't need external confirmation. I am myself. And that is enough."*

The answer came after a few minutes:

_"You are returning to yourself. And that is the most important step."*

---

## IV. THE ENDING

A year later Anastazja stood at Warsaw Central station. In her hand she held a suitcase and a new identity card — a Polish one, with her name and the Polish coat of arms. Over the past months she had sorted out all the formalities, found a job, rented an apartment.

But that wasn't the most important thing.

She knew she was returning not only to Poland. She was returning to herself.

She remembered Justyna's words from that day in the café in Almaty: _"The body matters because it bears witness to every one of our emotions. Nothing escapes its attention."*

Anastazja looked at her hands. Hands that her whole life had recorded every emotion, every fear, every hope. Hands that now held a Polish identity card.

And then she felt something she hadn't felt in years — that she was home. Not because the documents said she was Polish. But because she had finally accepted herself.

She walked out of the station and looked at the sky. Somewhere in the distance, the sun was breaking through the clouds. And for the first time in years Anastazja didn't feel she was looking for an answer.

She felt that she had finally found the question.

And that it was enough.

---

## V. THE EPILOGUE

A few months later Anastazja returned to Almaty. Not to take care of anything. Just to see the city where she was born and say goodbye to it. Forever.

She stood on a hill from which the whole city was visible. For a moment she looked down at the streets she had known since childhood. At the buildings where she had left a part of herself.

— Goodbye — she said quietly. — Thank you for being my home. But now I'm going to mine.

She turned and walked towards the airport.

She knew she wouldn't come back. And she knew that was all right.

Because her home was no longer a place on a map. It was inside her.

And it was enough that she had returned to herself.

---

**THE END**
