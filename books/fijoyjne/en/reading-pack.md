## Text

**Like BOR out of the Blue**

THERE ARE THINGS IN THE UNIT I WILL NEVER BE ABLE TO TALK ABOUT


When Piotr Malinowski first saw "Żuk" – Marcin Kowalczyk – he thought it was some kind of joke. The guy smiled like a child who had just got the toy of its dreams, and behind him were more than two decades in a unit that legends are told about. BOR – four letters that opened doors around the world, and in Poland – were still shrouded in mystery.

They sat facing each other in the studio of the "Bez Sekretów" podcast. Piotr looked at his guest and asked:

– Marcin, adrenaline enthusiast, car lover, former operator of a BOR unit. Good morning.

– Good morning, everyone – answered Żuk, smiling even wider.

Piotr knew this conversation would be different. He knew that before him sat a man who had gone through hell and come out of it with a smile on his face. A man who could be a ruthless operator in one moment, and a warm family father in the next.

– Is there a difference between an operator and a soldier? – Piotr asked to begin.

– Actually, it's the same thing – answered Żuk. – It's just that, because we have different kinds of specialisations, well, people say you're an operator of the BOR special unit.

– Okay. And if you had to say, is what's in your head more important, or what's in your muscles?

– The head – he answered without hesitation. – Because really the head plays the most important role, just to get through the selection at all.


**Selection – a test of life**

Żuk talked about the selection – a process that lasted months, and for some, years.

– First you submit your paperwork – he said. – And you wait. In my case it took a year. Then psychological tests. Then the mountains. They check whether you're fit for it at all. And only then the basic course – 13 months. Six weeks of green tactics – you don't leave the forest, you sleep three hours. That's a test of you.

– What happens if you don't pass? – asked Piotr.

– You go back. I went through several selections. In "Zachód" I didn't pass. In BOR I also went once and didn't pass. Only the second time.

– And what pushed you to try again?

– The head. You tell yourself: "There's no way I'm not going to pass." At the second selection in "Zachód" it was so bad that my legs were so chafed I couldn't walk. I was taking painkillers and running. Not the psyche.

Żuk said this with a smile, as if he were talking about an ordinary run, not about an exhausting test that ended in a breakdown for many. But he knew that it was precisely this trait – stubbornness, determination, the belief that it can be done – that separated operators from the rest.

– I noticed that when I was physically well prepared, my psyche was unbeatable, impossible to destroy – he added. – But what does that come from? We work on ourselves. All our traits are at such a high level that they simply calm us down for the jobs we do.

– Not everyone can be suited for work in BOR – Piotr summed up.

– Just like I could be suited for work as an accountant – laughed Żuk.


**A day in the life of an operator**

Żuk talked about the everyday life on missions – about what life was like in Afghanistan and Iraq.

– It all depends on whether we have tasks – he said. – Sometimes we waited three days for the job. And sometimes we got the signal and in five minutes we were in the air.

– Five minutes? – Piotr was surprised.

– Yes. Nobody's going to wait around – if BOR didn't fly out, well, the hostage would get to live a little longer. In the operation to free an American soldier – three units flew. Delta, JTFU and us. Poland got in because we stand high in the hierarchy.

Żuk talked about the preparations – checking the equipment, loading the magazines, planning. About how everyone knew their role and nobody had to ask what to do.

– My commander said: "Marcin, you're the comms guy. You're responsible for communications." And he didn't ask whether I'd done it. He said: "Marcin, radios." And he knew they were ready. The driver prepared the car, the gunner took the rifle, the medic took the medical kit. Everyone knew what to do.

Żuk also talked about the terrible side of war – about the IEDs, about fear, about adrenaline.

– There was one operation – he recalled. – We went in at night. Everything silent. A colleague almost stepped on a child, because they sleep on the floors, all next to each other. We came out of the building, and the man we'd detained says that right next door there's another terrorist. We check – indeed there is. Only there was a five-metre wall between us and him. We put a ladder on a tractor, climbed on top. Inside we found suicide vests – the kind you put on and detonate when you see the military. We were lucky we did it silently, otherwise we'd have died.

Piotr listened with bated breath. Żuk said all this calmly, as if he were talking about an ordinary day at the office. But in his eyes you could see something more – the shadow of those events, which had remained in him forever.

– And that's the worst part – said Żuk. – After an operation like that you come back to base, and you still have the paperwork, the photos, the sketches. You go to sleep at four in the morning, and you wake up the next day at seven in the evening. I slept for 27.5 hours without going to the toilet.

– 27.5 hours? – repeated Piotr in disbelief.

– Yes. The organism had to catch up on all those sleepless nights.


**Brotherhood and trauma**

Żuk stressed that the most important thing in BOR was the bond between operators.

– One rule: find yourself work – he said. – When you enter a building, you're not taught that you hold the front and someone else holds the side. You have to think. I see a window, nobody's covering it – I cover it, because someone might shoot at me. Same inside – you've done your part, you cover your colleagues. If someone shouts "malfunction" – you have to go over, help. It requires an open mind.

– So-called brotherhood – added Piotr.

– Yes. One goes into the fire for another. It's stronger than with family.

But that brotherhood had its price. Żuk talked about the death of a colleague from the unit – of blessed memory, Robert Nowak.

– We called each other, we were talking about converting to the Glock – he said quietly. – He told me he was dying. I went to him on Monday. I found him dead. He was already badly decomposed. His wife fainted. And I had such trauma that for two weeks I slept with a candle lit. I kept seeing his face. It was the greatest experience for me.

Piotr was silent for a moment.

– And you didn't have that in the war?

– No. Not in the war. There I saw various things – a colleague whose eye popped out, someone shot. And I didn't have trauma. And here, in civilian life, a buddy I knew... that was different.


**General Dębski – a figure who still stirs emotions**

Żuk couldn't help but mention General Tadeusz Dębski – the founder of BOR, whose death is still shrouded in mystery to this day.

– I don't believe it was suicide – he said firmly. – Too many cases like that had happened. Three days earlier we had talked. He said: "It's never been as good as it is now." And suddenly... he died.

– Why didn't he write a letter? Why didn't he do it at home? Why didn't he get dressed? – he asked rhetorically. – He wasn't that kind of person. We are people of honour. If I were going to do something like that, I certainly wouldn't go to the forest, wouldn't do it in a car. I would write a letter, apologise to my family.

Żuk remembered the general as a person who remembered every soldier.

– I lost a tooth on an operation in 2007. I have an implant. In 2011 the general said: "Where is my soldier who lost a tooth?" He remembered. Later I got shot in the leg – he remembered that too. He remembered everything well. I don't believe in his suicide.


**PTSD – or who's got whom**

Piotr asked about PTSD – post-traumatic stress disorder, which often affects soldiers after returning from missions.

– I read a comment about you – he said. – "It's not Marcin who has PTSD, it's PTSD who has Marcin." And I really liked that.

Żuk smiled.

– Yes, I saw that. A lot of people wrote to me. One lady even wrote that I was mentally ill. Without ever talking to me. I've been to psychologists, psychiatrists. The ones who got to know me know that's just how I am. I simply have that kind of facial expression.

He talked about a conversation with a psychologist who told him that his brain had repressed part of the traumatic memories.

– She asked whether I remembered the whole event, when I was wounded. I say: "Yes, I remember what I was doing, where I was standing, where I was shooting." And she says: "And what did the one who was shooting look like?" And I don't remember. She said: "Your brain cut it out. You remember everything in detail, but you don't remember the face."

– And now, when you hear certain sounds, do you go back in fear?

– No. I try to think soberly. My wife even said I calmed down after I left the army. I joke that I could become a sniper now.


**Farewell**

The conversation was coming to an end. Piotr thanked Żuk for his stories.

– Thank you very much for today – he said. – Stay with us for as long as possible. And I hope this isn't our last conversation.

– Absolutely – answered Żuk. – I'd also like to visit schools. I have an idea to bring 1,000 students to the Warsaw Uprising Museum. I already have a sponsor for the buses. I'd like young people to know what it means to serve.

Piotr nodded. He knew this wasn't just a story about war. It was a story about people who gave everything – health, time, family – so that others could live in peace. A story about honour, about brotherhood, and about what remains in a man once he has left the battlefield.

Żuk stood up and smiled.

– And that's it, ladies and gentlemen. Thank you for your attention.

He left with the same smile he had come with. And Piotr stayed in the studio, looking at the door that closed behind him. He knew he had talked to someone exceptional. Someone who had seen things he would never be able to fully tell. And who, despite that – or perhaps because of it – still smiled.


**THE END**
