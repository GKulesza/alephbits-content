**THE PROVING GROUND**

When Maciek was seven years old, his father took him to the workshop. Inside it smelled of grease and metal, and tools lay on the floor that looked like weapons. Father picked up a hammer and pressed it against a piece of steel.

– See, son – he said. – Metal has to be hardened in fire. Otherwise it stays soft. Good for nothing. Same with a man.

Maciek nodded, though he didn't understand. A few hours later he got his first slap. Not for anything specific – he simply hadn't managed to take out the rubbish when his father came home from work.

– You won't turn me into a softie – his father said. – In life you have to be tough.

Maciek learned two things then. First – that pain is a parenting tool. Second – that it's better to keep quiet.

---
In Maciek's home it was always loud. Not from laughter. From shouting. His father came back from work tired, furious, ready to explode. His mother walked on tiptoes so as not to provoke him. Maciek did too. But even that was never enough.

– In my day children weren't so cheeky – his father would say when Maciek dared to protest an unfair punishment. – When I was little, you ate what your mother gave you and you didn't whine. And you? You think you're owed everything.

Maciek didn't think he was owed everything. He only thought he'd like someone, just once, to ask him what he wanted. But questions were for grown-ups. Children were supposed to listen.

– Children and fish have no voice – his mother would say when Maciek tried to speak up. – Don't talk back.

And Maciek would stop. He would shut himself up inside. And he learned that his words didn't matter. His needs didn't matter. He himself didn't matter.

---
At school Maciek did all right, but not perfectly. Sometimes he got a B instead of an A. Then his father would rage:

– And what's this supposed to be? After all I taught you, all the money I spent, and you come home to me with B's?

– But Dad, I tried...

– I don't care if you tried. What counts is the result. It's not my problem that you don't study.

Maciek would hide in his room and cry. Not because of the B. Because he felt alone. Because his problems were only his. Because no one would help him.

Once, in sixth grade, a teacher said something in class that stuck in his memory. She said that parents should be the first people children turn to with their problems. That parents should be a safe haven.

Maciek thought then: "I don't have a haven like that. I have a proving ground."

---
When Maciek was twelve, his sister Kasia got her first period. She was scared, didn't know what was happening. She went to their mother. The mother, tired, stressed, waved her hand:

– Oh, come on, it's normal. Don't make such a fuss.

Kasia left the room in tears. Maciek saw it and thought: "She has no one to go to. No one will help her."

And then he understood that he wasn't the only one alone. That the whole family was drowning in a silence that was louder than his father's shouting.

---
Maciek's father wasn't a bad man. He was a man who had been raised that way himself. His father had beaten him with a belt, a cable, whatever came to hand. He would say: "You'll grow into a decent man." And Maciek's father did grow. Into a decent man who didn't know how to show love. Who thought pain was the only language children understood.

Sometimes, when Maciek lay in bed after another row, he could hear his father talking to his mother in the kitchen.

– I don't know what to do with him – his father would say. – I'm trying my best. I want him to be tough. To manage in life.

– Maybe you don't have to be so harsh – his mother would answer quietly.

– And if I'm not harsh, he'll grow into a softie. The kind that goes to psychologists and cries over himself.

Maciek heard those words and felt even worse. Because he knew his father really believed what he was doing was good. That he, Maciek, was a project to be improved for his father. Not a person. A project.

---
When Maciek turned eighteen, he moved out of the house. There was no big farewell. No tears. Only silence and relief.

At university he met people who had different families. Who talked to their parents about their problems. Who weren't afraid to hug. Who heard "I love you" not only at holidays.

Maciek watched them with envy. And with anger. Not at them – at himself. At being so broken. At not knowing how to talk about emotions. At shrinking automatically whenever anyone raised their voice, expecting a blow.

He went to therapy. The psychologist said something that surprised him:

– Your parents weren't bad. They were simply absent. Incapable of being present. And that's not your fault.

Maciek cried. For the first time in years.

---
When he became a father himself, he promised himself he'd be different. That he'd listen. That he'd be present. That he wouldn't shout, hit, or scare. That he wouldn't say "children and fish have no voice".

But it was hard. Sometimes, when his daughter cried for no reason, his father's voice would speak up in his head: "Don't get soft, she'll be a pushover." When his son got a C at school, he wanted to shout: "That's not my problem!"

And then Maciek would remember how he himself had felt as a child. How badly he'd needed someone to hug him. Someone to say: "I'm with you." Someone to listen, not judge.

And he did it. He hugged. He listened. He said "I love you". Even when it was hard for him. Even when he heard his father's voice inside.

Because he knew he could break that chain. That he didn't have to pass on what he himself had received.

---
One day Maciek drove to see his father. He was forty, his father seventy. They sat in the kitchen, drinking tea. It was quiet.

– Dad – Maciek said. – I'd like to tell you something.

His father looked at him, uncertain.

– When I was little, I was afraid of you. Not because you were bad. Because you were unpredictable. I never knew when you'd explode. And for a long time I thought there was something wrong with me. That I was weak. That I couldn't be tough.

His father was silent. In his eyes appeared something Maciek had never seen before – shame.

– I... I didn't know – he said at last. – I thought that's how it had to be. That if I was strict, you'd become someone. And you...

– And I did become someone – Maciek interrupted. – I became someone who couldn't ask for help. Who couldn't talk about feelings. Who was afraid of people because they reminded him of pain. But I changed that. I don't want to be like that. And I don't want my children to be like that.

His father lowered his eyes.

– I'm sorry – he said quietly. – I... I didn't want that either. I just didn't know any other way.

Maciek nodded.

– I know. And that's why I forgive you.

There was no great reconciliation. No tears. Only silence and understanding.

---
On the way home Maciek thought about what the psychologist had said: "Your parents weren't bad. They were simply absent." And he understood that it wasn't enough. That being present is something you have to learn. And that he, Maciek, learned it every day.

He came home. His daughter ran up to him with a drawing.

– Dad, look what I drew!

Maciek bent down and looked at the drawing.

– It's us – his daughter said. – Me, you and Mom. And we're all smiling.

Maciek smiled.

– Beautiful. Will you tell me about this drawing?

And he sat down beside her. And he listened. And he was present.

Because he knew that was the most important thing. That you don't have to be a perfect parent. You have to be real. You have to listen. You have to apologise. You have to love.

And you have to remember that a child isn't a piece of metal to be forged in fire. It's a person. Who needs love. And who has wings.

**THE END**
