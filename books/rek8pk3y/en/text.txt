**DIRTY MONEY, CLEAN SCIENCE**

## I. Warsaw, Institute of Experimental Physics

Dr Anna Kowalska was thirty-four years old, held a post-doctoral degree and had a desk covered in documents she should have read three weeks ago. Outside the window it was November, grey and damp, typical for Warsaw, but she didn't see it. She was staring at the computer screen, on which an empty document glowed. She was supposed to write an article on the effect of sweetening substances on cellular metabolism. She was supposed to write it two months ago.

She sighed. The work of a scientist in Poland today looked completely different from when she had started. Back then she thought she would discover the laws of nature, that her name would appear in textbooks. Now she knew her work came down to one thing: finding money.

– Ania, have you got a moment? – the door opened without a knock. In it stood Professor Nowak, her boss, a man in his sixties with a glint in his eye that always heralded trouble.

– Depends what it's about – she answered, turning away from the screen.

– Someone from a company came to see me. A big company. They want to fund some research.

– What research?

– On a new generation of... – he hesitated – ...sweeteners.

Ania raised an eyebrow.

– Sweeteners? We don't do sweeteners. We're a nuclear physics institute.

– I know. But they want us to study how these substances affect the body. At the cellular level. They already have preliminary results, but they need independent confirmation.

– Independent? – Ania smiled bitterly. – For money from the manufacturer?

– For money from a company that wants to know whether its product is safe. What's wrong with that?

– And what if it isn't?

Professor Nowak lowered his eyes.

– Then we'll adjust the results so they match their expectations.

Ania stood up. She walked to the window and looked at the grey sky. For a moment she thought about her father, who had been a scientist in the nineteen-eighties. He had worked for a pittance, but he had a clear conscience.

– I refuse – she said.

– Ania, I understand your scruples, but the institute needs money. Your research needs money too. We can do it so that no one finds out.

– And you? Would you agree?

Professor Nowak was silent.

– Professor, I know it's not easy. I know that each of us has bills to pay. But if we start manipulating results, we stop being scientists. We become... contractors.

– Sometimes you have to choose between ideals and survival.

– No – she answered firmly. – There is no such choice. If ideals aren't worth surviving for, then they aren't ideals.

Professor Nowak shook his head and left.

Ania sat back down at her desk. She picked up a book she had bought a week earlier – "Power, Money, Science" by Tomasz Rożek. She had already read the first chapter about how tobacco corporations hid the harmfulness of their products for decades. And about how the scientists who told the truth were destroyed.

She wondered whether the world had really changed. Whether today, in the age of universal access to information, such things were still possible.

Then she remembered that she knew the answer.

---

## II. Brussels, three months earlier

The conference on ethics in science had gathered several hundred participants from all over Europe. Ania gave a talk on the influence of corporations on scientific research. She spoke about what she had read in books, but also about what she had experienced first-hand.

After the lecture, a woman in an elegant suit approached her. She was about fifty, with grey hair and piercing eyes.

– Dr Kowalska? – she asked. – My name is Maria Nowak. I work for a foundation that deals with the transparency of scientific research. I heard your talk.

– Pleased to meet you.

– I could propose a collaboration. We're looking for scientists who are ready to tell the truth, even when it's inconvenient.

– That sounds... like a mission.

– That's exactly how we think about it.

Ania felt something stir inside her.

– And who funds this foundation?

– The money comes from private sources. Anonymous ones.

– Anonymous? – Ania looked at her with suspicion. – Meaning we don't know who stands behind this money?

– We know, we do, but we don't reveal it publicly. It protects our donors from attacks.

Ania thought about what she had said in her lecture moments earlier. About how important transparency is. About how hiding the sources of funding is the first step toward manipulation.

– Thank you for the offer, but I need to think it over – she answered.

– Of course. Please take my number.

The woman handed her a business card and left, leaving Ania with a strange feeling. It was a mixture of excitement and unease.

---

## III. Warsaw, a week later

She called Maria the next day.

– I've been thinking about your proposal – she said. – And I have a question.

– Go ahead.

– Could I learn the sources of the foundation's funding?

– No. That's impossible.

– Why?

– Because then our work would be impossible. If people knew who stands behind us, they would start questioning our credibility.

– And what if I want to know before I get involved?

– Unfortunately, that's not possible. You have to trust us.

Ania was silent for a long moment.

– I know what you're thinking – said Maria. – That this is exactly the same thing the corporations do. But the difference is that we have no interest in hiding the truth. We want it to come to light.

– And how do I know that's true?

– You don't. You have to trust us.

Ania felt she was standing at a crossroads. On one hand, she had a chance to do something important. On the other – she had to accept something she had always criticised: a lack of transparency.

– I need time – she said.

– Take as much as you need.

She hung up.

---

## IV. Three weeks later

She was sitting in her office reading a report she had received from Maria. It concerned the influence of one of the food corporations on obesity research. The findings were damning: the company had spent years funding studies meant to prove that it wasn't food products causing obesity, but a lack of exercise.

Ania knew this happened. But reading it with her own eyes, she felt something inside her crack.

She called Maria.

– I agree – she said. – I'm in.

– Excellent. Let's meet next week.

When she hung up, she felt relief. Finally she was doing something. Not just talking about the problem, but acting.

And then she looked at the business card Maria had given her. It was simple, elegant, without unnecessary information. Only the name of the foundation, a phone number and an email address.

And then she saw something she hadn't noticed before. At the bottom, in fine print, was an inscription:

_"Funded by the Foundation..."._

The name of the foundation was unknown. Ania typed it into a search engine. The results were ambiguous – a website, a few mentions in the press, no concrete information about who stood behind the money.

She searched deeper. She found an article from two years earlier in which someone suggested the foundation might be linked to one of the pharmaceutical corporations.

She closed her laptop.

– Damn – she whispered.

---

## V. Wrocław, a café in the market square

They met in a small café, away from prying eyes. Tomasz Rożek looked different than on television – more tired, but his eyes were still bright.

– Thank you for agreeing to meet – he said. – Your experience is very valuable to me.

– Your book has explained a lot to me too – answered Ania. – Reading about how corporations manipulated tobacco research, I felt that the same thing was happening in my field.

– And what did you do about it?

– I agreed to work with a foundation that was supposed to change that. And then I discovered it might be linked to a pharmaceutical corporation.

Tomasz nodded.

– That's a classic mechanism. You create a foundation that ostensibly fights dishonest practices, but it actually serves to discredit the competition. Or to create the appearance of independence.

– And what should I do now?

– That depends on you. You can pull out and pretend you saw nothing. You can continue the collaboration, hoping that you're actually doing something good. Or you can reveal what you've discovered.

– And risk being attacked?

– Yes. That is the price paid by those who tell the truth.

Ania thought about her father. About how hard he had worked, how much he had believed in science. And that he had never had to choose between truth and safety.

– I'll do it – she said. – I'll reveal it.

Tomasz smiled.

– I knew you'd say that.

---

## VI. A year later

Ania's article was published in one of the leading scientific journals. In it she revealed the link between the foundation and the pharmaceutical corporation that had funded research on obesity drugs. The text caused a storm.

Some called her a heroine. Others – a traitor. She lost her job at the institute, but found a new one, at a private research centre that wasn't afraid of controversy.

She met Tomasz again, this time in Warsaw.

– How do you feel? – he asked.

– Strange. Part of me regrets doing it. But the larger part is proud.

– That's normal. Change is never easy.

– And you? Do you have such doubts too?

Tomasz looked at her carefully.

– Every day. But I know that what I'm doing makes sense. And that if we don't tell the truth, no one else will.

Ania nodded.

– You know what's the worst? That even when you tell the truth, you're not sure people will believe it.

– That's true. But it doesn't mean it's not worth trying.

---

## VII. Epilogue

A year later Ania stood on a stage in Brussels, receiving an award for her work for transparency in science. In the front row sat Maria, who after the article was published had lost her job, but had found a new one at another foundation.

– Thank you – said Ania, looking at the audience. – But this award doesn't belong only to me. It belongs to everyone who believes that science should serve the truth, not money.

She went home late in the evening. She sat down at her desk and opened Tomasz Rożek's book. On the first page she found a dedication:

_"For those who are not afraid to tell the truth."_

And then she understood that it wasn't about changing the whole world. It was about changing your own little corner. And about others, looking at her, believing it was possible.

She closed the book and looked through the window. Outside, the stars were shining.

– I did it – she said quietly to herself. – And even if it had to happen again, I would do the same.

Because science without truth is only a tool in the hands of those who want to manipulate. And she didn't want to be a tool.

She wanted to be a scientist.

**THE END**
