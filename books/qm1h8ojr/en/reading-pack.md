## Text

A short story in three parts

### PART ONE: THE PROMISED LAND

When Adam Kowalski first heard about the market in Nowy Targ, he thought it was a joke.

– The biggest one in Poland – his brother-in-law Tomek kept repeating, spreading his arms as wide as if he wanted to embrace the whole Podhale. – I'm telling you, man, they have everything there. Jackets, shoes, food, ski gear, even mini bars made from barrels. And Hungarians. There are so many Hungarians you can have a conversation in Hungarian without knowing a single word.

Adam was not convinced. As a resident of Elbląg, raised at the market by Płk. Dąbka Street, he knew the taste of real, old-style trade. There, in his own city, the market was modest. Concrete slabs cracked by winter, rusty canopies, Mrs Jadzia at the vegetable stand who knew the names of all her customers' grandchildren, and Mr Marek with his cold cuts who always threw in an extra slice of ham "to try". That was his world – small, cramped, but real.

Now, however, after the divorce, after losing his job at the Elbląg machine shop, and after his former boss told him he "was not cut out for the modern market", Adam decided to do something crazy. He packed a suitcase, got into his battered Golf with the LPG tank that hissed at every incline, and headed south.

– I'm going to see that market of yours – he told Tomek over the phone. – And if it's a scam, I'm coming back and I'll never listen to your advice again.

– You'll see – Tomek replied. – And you'll stay.

Adam did not believe him. But he drove.

Nowy Targ greeted him with cold. It was April, but in the mountains winter refused to let go. The car thermometer showed minus seven degrees, and when Adam got out at the parking lot on Targowa Street, he felt the frost biting into his lungs.

The parking lot was enormous. And free.

– Not bad – he muttered, pulling his jacket tight. – Even in Elbląg you pay for parking.

He looked around. Cars – Polish, Slovak, Czech, and above all Hungarian. Every second or third car had white plates with a blue stripe. Adam remembered what Tomek had said about the Hungarians. They come here for cashmere, for leather, for wooden seagulls and highlander hearts to hang on the wall. They come because here it is cheaper and better. They come because the market in Nowy Targ is their promised land.

Adam walked into the first aisle and was struck speechless.

The stalls stretched into the distance endlessly. Wooden huts, tents, tables covered with blankets, racks of jackets, shelves of shoes. The smell was intense – leather, wood, fried meat from the food stands, smoked plums, and underneath it all, the frost gripping your nostrils.

The sellers shouted to each other in several languages. Polish "zobacz pan" mixed with Hungarian "nézd meg" and Slovak "pozri sa". Someone was haggling over a leather jacket, someone else over a wooden parzenica. Adam walked straight ahead, not knowing what to look at first.

– Sweaters! – he heard beside him. – Cashmere! Real cashmere! Only 270 zlotys!

He stopped. In front of him stood a tall man in a cap and a leather apron. Sweaters hung at the stall – soft, fluffy, in muted colours. Adam touched one. It was like a cloud.

– Beautiful – he said.

– Beautiful – the seller agreed. – But not for you.

– Why?

– Because you have a leather jacket. You're from the city. You came to buy something, but you don't know yet what. When you know, you'll come back.

Adam laughed. He was right. He didn't know what he was looking for. He had come out of curiosity, out of rebellion, out of a need for change. But not with a shopping list.

– Maybe later – he replied.

– I'll be here – the seller said and turned to the next customer.

Adam walked on. At one point he came across a stall with wooden goods. Revolving snack boards, trays, garden windmills, and at the centre – something that took his breath away.

A mini bar made from a barrel.

Large, oak, with built-in shelves and little lamps gently lighting the bottles. It looked like a work of art. The price – 1,500 zlotys – seemed both high and surprisingly reasonable, given the quality of workmanship.

– Do you like it? – asked the seller, an older man in a woollen waistcoat.

– Very much – Adam admitted. – But I have nowhere to put it.

– Then you'll find a place – the seller smiled. – When a man wants something, there's always a place.

Adam thought about his empty flat in Elbląg, which had not yet been sold. About his things packed into cardboard boxes, about a life that had fallen apart. Maybe he really did need something new. Something he could put in a new place.

He walked away from the stall, but the image of the bar stayed in his head.

He sat down on a bench by one of the aisles to rest. He noticed that some of the stalls were closed. Empty tables, rolled-up tarpaulins, sellers hiding their hands in their pockets.

– The frost – said his neighbour on the bench, an older man with a cigarette in his mouth. – Minus nine. Some people don't come. Too bad, because their goods are good, but when their hands get cold, they prefer to stay home.

– Do you come here often? – Adam asked.

– For twenty years. I have a tool stall. Screwdrivers, hammers, saws. Everything second-hand, but well cared for. When I sell something, it has to be decent. Because if a customer brings it back, it's shameful.

– And I don't like shame – Adam finished with a smile.

– I can see you understand – the man replied. – Where are you from?

– From Elbląg.

– Elbląg – the man took a drag of his cigarette. – There's a market there too. Right?

– There is. On Dąbka. Small, old, but it's there.

– And what are you doing here, if you have your own market?

Adam hesitated.

– I'm looking for something – he said at last. – I don't know what. Maybe a new beginning.

The man nodded.

– That's good. This place is good for beginnings. You see all this? – he gestured at the aisles. – Every one of these traders started once. With one table, one jacket, one screwdriver. And now? Now some of them have three stalls. And Hungarians, Slovaks, Czechs come here. Even Arabs were here during the season. This is not ordinary trade, mister. This is life.

Adam looked down the aisle. Indeed – the market was alive. Even in the frost, even with empty stalls, something was happening. Someone was trading, someone was laughing, someone was trying a smoked plum for 28 zlotys a kilo. Someone was buying shoes for 20 euros – about 89 zlotys – and rejoicing as if he had found a treasure.

– Thank you – Adam said to the older man. – Thank you for the talk.

– Good luck, Elbląg man – the man replied.

### PART TWO: BETWEEN MOUNTAINS AND SEA

Adam came back to the market the next day. This time earlier, at seven in the morning, when the traders were just laying out their goods. He wanted to see how this place woke up to life. He wanted to understand what made the Hungarians come here with such devotion, why the Arabs chose this place over shopping malls, why the locals still came back even though the city had supermarkets.

He watched. First the traders – the old hands. They laid out sweaters in rows, paired up shoes, hung jackets on racks. Every movement was precise, like in a dance. Then the first customers came – locals who knew their stalls by heart. Then the tourists – with cameras, with children, with shopping bags.

Adam walked up to the food stand. A food truck with skewers and fries. Prices in two currencies – zlotys and euros. 36 zlotys for a set, 9 euros.

– Excuse me, may I ask you something? – he addressed the seller, a young man in a white apron.

– Sure.

– Why do you list prices in euros? We're in Poland, after all.

The young man smiled.

– Because Hungarians don't want to convert. Because Slovaks don't either. And they come here in droves. When they see euros, they know how much they'll pay. And they don't have to think. And when they don't have to think, they buy more.

Adam looked at the stalls next door. Indeed – everywhere there were double prices. Zlotys and euros.

– And how do they pay? Cash? Card?

– It varies. There are ATMs on the market grounds. But it's better to have cash. Not everyone takes cards. And if you don't have any, you can withdraw from an ATM. There are three, maybe four. You don't have to look far.

Adam nodded. This place was polished down to the details. A sanitary hub, ATMs, free parking, food in two currencies. This was not an ordinary market. It was a money-making machine – but with a human face.

At one point Adam heard a familiar voice. He turned – it was the sweater seller, the one with the cashmere.

– You came back! – the man called out. – I told you you'd come back!

– You were right – Adam admitted.

– You see, I know people. I know who comes for a day and who stays. You stay. I can feel it.

Adam smiled, though inside he felt uneasy. Maybe that seller really did know something. Maybe Adam really was looking for something more than just shopping.

– Do you have something for me? – he asked.

The seller looked at him carefully.

– Do you have a job? – he asked unexpectedly.

– No.

– Do you have a home?

– Not really. I have a flat in Elbląg, but I'm selling it.

– Do you have a family?

– I was married. Not anymore.

The seller nodded, as if he had heard exactly what he expected.

– Good. That means you can start over. Here. At this market. I need someone to help. Someone who knows Polish, English and at least a bit of Hungarian. Do you know Hungarian?

– No.

– Then you'll learn. It's an easy language. Listen for a month and you'll pick it up.

Adam wanted to protest, but the words stuck in his throat. Was this a chance? Was it a coincidence? Or did the market really have some magic power that drew in people like him – lost, searching, ready for a change?

– I don't know – he said at last. – I need to think.

– Think here – the seller replied. – I'm here every day. From Monday to Sunday. When you decide, you'll come.

Adam walked away, but he didn't head for the exit. Instead he turned into a side aisle he had missed before. He came across an electronics stall. Old VCRs, VHS players, cassettes in banana boxes. The seller – a bearded man in a thick jacket – sat on a stool reading a newspaper.

– Good morning – Adam said.

The seller looked up.

– Are you looking for something specific?

– No. Just browsing.

– Then browse. But touch carefully. Some of these things are thirty years old.

Adam looked at the VCRs. They were cracked, scratched, dirty. Tangled cables, peeling casings. One of the VHS cassettes – from the Scena company, with a sticker printed on the head – lay on top. Adam picked it up.

On the sticker was the title: "Komando". Below, in red print, the description: "about a commando who shoots". The whole thing was made with attention to detail. Two-coloured, neat, as if someone had spent hours on it.

– Nice – Adam said.

– Nice – the seller agreed. – But the box has come unglued. And the tape is not what it used to be. If you want, I can let it go for five zlotys.

Adam put the cassette back.

– No, thank you. I feel sorry for it. I'd rather see what it looked like in its good days.

The seller looked at him with surprise.

– You're sentimental.

– Maybe.

– That's good. The market needs sentimental people. The rest is just numbers.

Adam nodded and walked on. Those words – the market needs sentimental people – stuck in his memory.

### PART THREE: A NEW BEGINNING

A week passed. Adam returned to Elbląg, sold the flat, packed the rest of his things and set off back south. He didn't know why he was doing it. Maybe because the market in Nowy Targ was the first place in years that made him feel alive. Maybe because the sweater seller in the cap had told him something he wanted to hear: you can start over.

He came back to Nowy Targ on Saturday morning. The market was crowded – more Hungarians, more tourists, more noise. The sweater seller, whose name was Jacek, greeted him with a wide smile.

– I thought you wouldn't come back – he said.

– I thought I wouldn't either – Adam admitted.

– Well, here you are. And what are you going to do now?

Adam looked around the market grounds. Aisles full of colours, smells, sounds. Wooden seagulls, leather jackets, cashmere, smoked plums, mini bars from barrels, revolving boards for 70 zlotys, thermal gloves for 30 zlotys, Polish winter garlic and hard toffees that were impossible to bite in the frost. All of it was already a part of him.

– I'd like to help – he said at last. – I'd like to learn the trade. And Hungarian.

Jacek held out his hand.

– Then we begin. From tomorrow. Today walk around, look, listen. Remember faces. Remember prices. And remember that the market is not just goods. The market is people.

Adam nodded. He had known that for a long time. Ever since, as a little boy, he went with his grandmother to the market in Elbląg, to Dąbrowskiego Street, where traders from the Soviet Union sold gold off their bodies and Germans brought electronics in boxes. There were people there too. And there too, life went on.

A dozen or so years later Adam stood at his own stall, between two aisles at the market in Nowy Targ. He sold leather jackets and shoes. He spoke fluent Hungarian and Slovak, and some Czech. His customers knew him by name, and he knew their needs.

Next to him was a stall with wooden goods – mini bars from barrels, revolving boards and parzenica ornaments. The owner, a young woman from Kraków whom Adam had met two years earlier, was his partner in business and in life.

Jacek, the old sweater seller, no longer traded. He had retired, but he still came to the market to sit on a bench, watch the bustle and talk with Adam.

– Do you remember how you came the first time? – he asked one day.

– I remember – Adam replied. – It was minus seven, and you were selling cashmere for 270 zlotys.

– And you didn't know what you wanted.

– Sometimes I still don't.

Jacek laughed.

– That's good. It means you still have a reason to be here.

Adam looked at his stall, at the customers, at his partner, who was just haggling over a revolving board with a German. He looked at the parking lot where hundreds of cars stood – Polish, Hungarian, Slovak, Czech. He looked at the sky, which that day was blue, cloudless, perfect for trade.

And he thought about what the old tool seller in Elbląg had once told him: the market is life.

And he was right. The market was life. Cold, hot, noisy, colourful, full of people looking for something for themselves. Sometimes it was cashmere, sometimes a wooden ornament for 120 zlotys, sometimes a pair of shoes for 20 euros. And sometimes – something that cannot be bought. A new beginning.

Adam went back to work. A customer from Hungary was just trying on a leather jacket. He smiled at her and said in her language:

– It fits you perfectly. Will you take it?

The woman looked at him with surprise.

– Where did you learn Hungarian?

Adam laughed.

– I learned it. At the market.

### Epilogue

Today the market in Nowy Targ is still the largest market in Poland. Hungarians, Slovaks and Czechs still come, and in summer – tourists from all over the world. You can still buy cashmere there for 270 zlotys, a revolving board for 70 zlotys, a smoked plum for 28 zlotys and a mini bar from a barrel for 1,500 zlotys. There are still ATMs, free parking lots and toilets that someone takes care of.

But what really matters – is the people. Those who come, and those who stay. Those who search, and those who find.

Adam stayed. And he did not regret a single day.

Because the market, like life, is not about what you buy. It's about who you meet along the way.

And about the fact that you can always start over. Even at minus nine degrees.
