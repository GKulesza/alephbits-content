## Text

**THE COOKIE BOX**

**A short story**

When he crossed the threshold of his mother's flat for the first time since her death, Krzysztof knew it would be bad. What he did not expect was that it would be this bad.

The flat smelled of age. Not that pleasant kind, of books and dried flowers, but that sickly one that gathers in places where life has stopped in its tracks. Dust covered everything in a thick, grey layer, as if someone had scattered ash across the whole house.

Krzysztof stood in the hallway and looked around. Before him stretched a corridor that he once remembered as bright and spacious. Now it was a narrow passage between two storeys of boxes, bags and piles of unknown origin. Fourteen coats hung on the coat rack. Fourteen. For one person.

— Mum, what have you done to this flat? — he whispered.

Behind him stood his daughter, Lena. She was twenty-three, with red hair tied up in a careless bun, and on her face an expression of astonishment she was unsuccessfully trying to hide.

— Grandpa... is this where Grandma lived? — she asked quietly.

— All her life. Fifty years.

He ran his hand along the door frame. His fingers came across something sticky. He did not want to check what.

Over the next three hours they walked through the flat as if through a museum of curiosities. In the living room — for Krzysztof guessed that this had once been a living room — the furniture stood against the walls, but on it and beneath it objects were piled up. Newspapers from the nineties, neatly stacked. Jars, hundreds of jars, empty and full, with dates on the lids that spoke of preserves from the last decade. Clothes in carrier bags, some still with tags. Books that had never been read stood in rows, and in front of them another row of books, and in front of that yet another.

— How did she live here? — asked Lena, sitting down on the only free chair. She stood up quickly, because something crunched beneath her.

Krzysztof did not answer. He stood before the sideboard in which, as a boy, he used to find sweets. Now, inside, he discovered eighteen towels. All new, in plastic wrap.

— Just in case — he said quietly, quoting the words he had heard from his mother's lips hundreds of times. — Just in case, Krzysiu. It might come in handy.

At night he could not sleep. He lay on a mattress thrown into the corner of the only room where they had managed to make a little space, and stared at the ceiling. His thoughts circled around a single question: how had it come to this?

He remembered the PRL. The queues, the empty shop shelves, the all-present lack. He remembered how his mother used to get him shoes with ration coupons, how she repaired worn-out clothes because there were no new ones. He remembered her hands, always busy — either sewing, or darning, or cooking ahead. "It will come in handy," she would say, putting another portion of compote into a jar. "The winter will be long."

And then he understood. It was not hoarding. It was fear. Fear of the return of that world which had once almost killed them. A fear that had not gone away even when the shops filled to the brim. A fear that had turned the flat into a fortress built of things. Because things were safe. Things did not disappear. Things could be controlled.

— What are we going to do with all this now? — he asked in his thoughts the mother who could no longer answer him.

The next day Lena came with an idea.

— I found someone — she said, taking out her phone. — Her name is Ola. She does something like... decluttering. She helps people sort out their flats. Psychologically, you know?

Krzysztof looked at her distrustfully.

— Psychologically how? I don't need a psychologist, I need someone to carry out this rubbish.

— It's not rubbish, Dad — she said gently. — At least not for Grandma. And not for you. Otherwise you would have done it long ago yourself.

She had hit a sore spot.

— Fine — he muttered. — Let her come.

Ola came three days later. She was around forty, with short blond hair, a calm voice and eyes that seemed to see more than she said. She had not brought any boxes or bags. She brought only two cups of coffee, sat down on the free chair and began the conversation with a question Krzysztof found absurd.

— What do you feel when you come in here?

— Devastated — he answered without hesitation. — And furious. And sad.

Ola nodded.

— And what do you feel when you think about throwing something out from here?

Krzysztof hesitated.

— Fear. That... that I'll throw out something important. Something that she... something that had meaning.

— Had meaning for your mother? Or for you?

— For her.

— And for you?

He fell silent.

They started with the kitchen. Ola had a method: first take everything out, then categorise, then make decisions. Not to throw away at once — first to see how much of something there is.

When they took fifty-three jars out of the cupboards, Krzysztof almost laughed. When they found seven tin openers, although there was not a single tin in the flat that would need an opener, he felt sad.

— What did she need them for? — he asked quietly.

Ola looked at him carefully.

— Maybe because at some point in her life there were no tins. Maybe because a tin opener was a symbol of plenty. That one has something to open. It was not a need, Krzysztof. It was protection against the poverty that had once hurt her.

For a long moment he looked at the pile of jars on the floor.

— I used to be like that too. In my youth. I hoarded things. But then... then I got married, I had Lena, somehow I let it go. And she could not.

— Because her wound was deeper — said Ola. — My grandmother was like that too. After the war. All her life she collected buttons. Hundreds, thousands of buttons in boxes. She said she would need them one day. But really she needed a sense of control. Things gave her the illusion that if she had enough, nothing bad would happen to her.

Krzysztof closed his eyes. In his head he heard his mother's voice: "Krzysiu, put that away, it will come in handy. The winter will be long."

But the winter had long passed. And he was still standing still.

In the bedroom they found a cookie box. He remembered it from childhood, although then it had been full of biscuits. Now inside there were letters. Letters from his mother, written to him when he was a little boy.

He sat down on the floor and began to read. His mother's hand, her characteristic writing, slanted, a little careless, because she always said she had no time for calligraphy.

_"My darling, I'm coming back late. Don't worry. You are the cleverest boy in the world. Remember that we are with you, even when you can't see us."_

The letters were short. Ordinary little notes left on the table when his mother went to work. But in each of them there was love, so simple and everyday, that he had not appreciated then.

Ola came up quietly and sat down beside him.

— These are important things — she said.

— Yes — he answered, not taking his eyes off the paper. — These are important.

— Are all the things in this box important?

He turned over a few pages. He found a receipt from 1988, a bus ticket from thirty years ago, a bookmark with a milk advertisement.

— No. These are just... rubbish.

— Then why do you keep them?

He could not answer. Because in each of those little things there was a piece of his mother. Her hand had touched that receipt. Her fingers had held that ticket. Throwing them away meant throwing her away.

— Listen — said Ola. — What you feel is normal. This is not only your story. That is how memory works. We become attached to objects because they are tangible proof that something happened. That someone existed. That we were loved.

Krzysztof nodded, but he could not speak.

— Only that love — she went on — is not in those objects. It is in you. In who you are. In how you talk about your mother. She lives in your memory, not in that cookie box. Things are only symbols. And you can let them go without losing what truly matters.

An hour later he was holding the empty box in his hands. The letters were in a separate folder that Ola had brought in her bag. The rest went into a sack.

— Do you feel different? — she asked.

— A little lighter — he admitted. — But also... strangely. As if I had lost something.

— Because you have lost something. You have lost the weight you have been carrying for years. It is normal to feel an emptiness. But it will fill with something else. Give yourself time.

Weeks passed. The flat slowly regained its shape. The walls that had been hidden behind piles of boxes for decades revealed their pale, unpainted surface. They found the floor. They found the windows, through which light finally came in.

Krzysztof came every day. Sometimes with Lena, sometimes alone. He was no longer furious. There was no more of that anger at his mother for what she had left behind. Instead, something else appeared — understanding. That what she had been doing was not weakness. It was an act of survival. And he, her son, could finally see it through. He could let go of what she had not known how to let go of.

One day, while emptying the wardrobe in the hallway, he found something he had not expected. A small black handbag, hidden behind the old coats. Inside were photographs. His mother, young, before the war, with a family he had never known. And a letter.

A letter written in her hand, but not to him.

_"Dear Mum, I'm sorry I couldn't come to the funeral. I had no money. I had no strength. I hope you can forgive me. I think about you every day. Everything I do, I do for you. I keep your things. They are with me. You are always with me."_

Krzysztof read it three times. Then he put the letter down and looked out of the window for a long time.

Until then he had not known that his mother, too, had lost someone. That her hoarding was not only a fear of winter. It was an attempt to keep close those who were no longer there. Her things were a bridge to a world that had vanished.

And he understood that he could let it go. Not because he had stopped loving his mother. Because now he understood who she really was.

When the last bags were gone and the flat stood empty and clean, Krzysztof sat down in the middle of the living room. Around him were four walls, a window and a wooden floor. No furniture, no boxes. Only space. An empty space that was waiting for something new.

— And now what? — asked Lena, standing in the doorway.

Krzysztof smiled.

— Now we can start anew. But this time... consciously. Only what we truly need.

Lena came over and sat down beside him.

— Do you think Grandma would be pleased?

— I think... that in the end she understood. That it is not objects that matter. That it is us. And that none of us has to carry the burden of the past if we do not want to.

He put his hand on his daughter's shoulder and looked through the window at the city. Somewhere far away, at the edge of the horizon, the sun was rising.

And then, for the first time in many months, he felt that he was truly breathing.

**THE END**
