**FIRST FLIGHT TO MARS**

Marek was eight years old when he first heard about Mars. It was 1976, when the Viking probe sent the first pictures from the red planet back to Earth. His father, a physics teacher at a Warsaw high school, spread a newspaper with the printed photograph on the floor and said: "Look, son. That's Mars. And one day, someone will fly there."

Marek looked at the picture. He saw a desert, stones, strange rock formations – and something that looked like a face.

– It's a face – he said, pointing his finger.

His father smiled.

– It's just a play of light and shadow. But you know what? People used to think there were canals on Mars. That Martians had built a whole irrigation system to save their planet from drying up.

– And were there?

– No. It was a mistake. But that mistake made people start dreaming. And that dream is more important than the truth about the canals.

Marek didn't understand those words then. But he remembered them for the rest of his life.

---

Forty years later, Marek was already a scientist. Not an astrophysicist, not an astronomer – but someone who had spent two decades analyzing data from Martian rovers. He worked in a laboratory that studied rock samples from the Red Planet – those that had arrived on Earth in meteorites, and those that were still waiting to be brought back.

In December 1984, a meteorite was found in Antarctica. Nobody knew where it came from at the time. For twelve years it lay in a warehouse, forgotten, covered in dust. Until 1996, when a group of NASA scientists announced something that shook the world.

Marek remembered that day. He was sitting in his first apartment, drinking coffee and watching the television, where the President of the United States, Bill Clinton, was talking about possible traces of life on Mars.

– That was the moment when everything changed – Marek told his assistant when he recalled those times. – Suddenly we stopped asking "whether" and started asking "where" and "when".

– And what did you do then?

– We started looking. And we're still looking today.

---

Over the years, Marek followed reports of Martian life. There were many of them – and they always ended in disappointment.

The ALH84001 meteorite, which some researchers believed contained fossilized bacteria, was eventually recognized as the product of geochemical processes. The "Face" of Cydonia turned out to be an ordinary hill when the Mars Global Surveyor probe took pictures at higher resolution. The canals that Schiaparelli had seen were merely an optical illusion.

– It's as if we're chasing a shadow – Marek once said at a conference. – But we can't stop, because that shadow might be real.

In 2008, the Phoenix lander discovered perchlorates on Mars. That explained the mystery of the Viking experiments from 1976, which had suggested the presence of life. It turned out that it wasn't microorganisms releasing radioactive carbon dioxide, but chemical reactions in the soil. Another hope was dashed.

But Marek didn't give up. He believed the answer existed. That somewhere, in some rock, in some sediment, it was waiting to be discovered.

---

In 2021, the Perseverance rover landed in Jezero crater. Marek was in his laboratory then, following the live broadcast. When the first pictures reached Earth, he felt something he hadn't felt in years – excitement.

– That's an ancient river delta – he told his team. – If there was ever life there, this is where it left traces.

For four years, Perseverance crossed the crater, drilled into rocks, collected samples. And last year it found something extraordinary – a rock named Cheyava Falls. It was covered in strange, round spots that looked like leopard spots.

Marek spent another year analyzing the data. Every morning he came to the laboratory, turned on the computer and checked the latest results. He watched his team work tirelessly, searching for an explanation.

And finally, a few weeks ago, the phone rang.

– Marek? – he heard the voice of the mission chief. – We have the results. Come.

---

Everyone gathered in the conference room. On the screen, data from Perseverance's instruments was displayed – PIXL, SHERLOC, a whole range of spectrometers. Marek sat in his place and watched as the mission chief stood up and began to speak.

– Listen – he said. – We've analyzed everything. The chemical composition, the mineral structure, the distribution of those spots. And we've come to the conclusion that there is no known geochemical process that could explain this.

Silence fell.

– What does that mean? – someone asked.

– It means this could be a trace of life. I'm not saying it definitely is. But we can't find any other explanation.

Marek felt his heart begin to beat faster. For a moment he couldn't speak. Then he stood up and asked:

– What about the carbon isotopes?

– That's exactly what we're doing. We'll bring the samples back to Earth – added the mission chief. – Perseverance has already packed them. They're waiting to be picked up.

– When?

– In a few years. Probably in the thirties. If everything goes according to plan.

Marek nodded. He knew this could be the last mission he took part in. He was already sixty. But that didn't matter. What mattered was that for the first time in the history of humanity, we stood on the threshold of an answer to the oldest question: are we alone in the universe?

---

That evening, Marek left the laboratory and looked at the sky. It was clear, starry. Somewhere out there, in the distance, a red dot was shining – Mars.

He remembered his father's words from forty years earlier: "People used to think there were canals on Mars. It was a mistake. But that mistake made people start dreaming."

And then he understood that this dream had never died. It had survived mistakes, disappointments, false alarms. It had even survived the face on Mars, which turned out to be only a play of light and shadow. It had survived, because people need to believe that they are not alone.

Marek took out his phone and wrote a message to his daughter, who was studying astrobiology at the University of Warsaw:

_"We have it. Or almost have it. In a few years you'll know the answer. And if it's true – then you and your generation will be the first to grow up in a world where we know that life exists beyond Earth."_

His daughter replied after a moment:

_"Dad, I always knew it was there. Now we're just waiting for science to confirm it."_

Marek smiled. He put the phone in his pocket and looked at the sky once more. The red dot was still shining.

"Not yet" – he thought. "But soon."
