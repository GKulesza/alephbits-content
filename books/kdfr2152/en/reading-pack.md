## Text

**THE WORD THAT ISN'T THERE**


## I. Four years and two hours

When Ala was four years old, she disappeared. Not literally – physically she was in her room, on the carpet, surrounded by blocks that were supposed to help her learn to read. Her face had disappeared. Or rather – she had ceased to exist in the field of attention of her parents.

– Ala, look, this is the syllable "ma" – said her mother, Kasia, holding up a card with big letters.

Ala was looking somewhere else. At the curtain, at her own fingers, at the fly on the ceiling. Everything was more interesting than those black marks on a white background.

– Ala, look here – Kasia tried again, touching her chin with her finger to guide her gaze.

Ala pushed her hand away and started stacking the blocks into a tower.

– I don't want to.

– But we're playing, sweetheart.

– This isn't play – said Ala with the seriousness of a four-year-old. – This is work.

Kasia felt something break inside her. She had no strength. She went back to the kitchen, where her husband, Tomek, was just finishing a phone call.

– How did it go? – he asked.

– Terribly. I don't feel like it.

– Maybe it's too early? She's only four.

– Professor Cieszyńska says it's already late. That the brain is ready from the age of two.

– And what about her childhood? She should be playing, not learning.

Kasia sat down at the table and buried her face in her hands.

– That's exactly the point, Tomek. She doesn't know how to play. She can't come up with a game by herself. You give her a toy, she looks and waits for someone to do it for her. If I show her how to do it, she repeats. But she won't come up with it herself. That's the problem.

Tomek looked at her carefully.

– You mean she has no imagination?

– No. She has imagination. It's just that it's an imagination plugged into the screen. Into what she's seen in cartoons. Into ready-made patterns. She doesn't need to imagine anything, because everything is already made. And we give it to her.

Tomek walked to the window. In the yard, children were running after a ball. His daughter wasn't running. She was sitting in her room, waiting for someone to tell her what to do.

– What were you saying about that method? – he asked.

Kasia looked up.

– Early reading instruction. It's supposed to engage the whole brain. The right hemisphere, the left hemisphere, the cerebellum. It's supposed to teach a child that what she does makes sense. And it's supposed to bring joy. It's not about her sitting at a desk and repeating. It's about laughing, about feeling that she can do it.

– And you think it will work?

– I don't know. But I have to try.

---

## II. One day a week

Kasia started with one day a week. On Saturday morning, instead of turning on a cartoon, they sat on the floor with a set of vowel cards. The first session was a disaster. Ala squirmed, screamed, tried to run away. Kasia didn't give in.

– Ala, look, this is "o" – she said, showing her a card with a big O. – When someone falls, they say "o". When your lollipop drops, you say "o". Watch. – She tossed a little ball that fell to the floor. – O! – she said loudly.

Ala looked at her. For the first time in a long while, something interested her.

– Now you.

Ala took the ball and tossed it clumsily. The ball fell.

– O – she said quietly.

Kasia smiled broadly.

– Yes! Ala said "o"! And I've got it! – She took out a crayon and drew a ball on the card. – And now? – she asked. – What will happen if you take the teddy bear?

Ala looked at the plush toy lying nearby. She tossed it. The bear fell.

– O? – she tried.

– No, look – Kasia tossed the bear. – The bear says "a", because he bumped himself. But he doesn't cry, because he's brave. – She stood the bear on its feet. – And when it jumps, it says "e". Watch. – The bear jumped.

Ala started to laugh.

– Again!

And then Kasia saw what she had been looking for for months. A spark. Interest. Joy.

---

## III. Three months later

Ala was four years and three months old when she read her first sentence. It wasn't a sentence from a book. It was a sentence she had put together herself, from little cards: "MOM-MY L-O-V-E-S A-L-A" ("Mummy loves Ala").

Kasia didn't know what to do. She stood in the kitchen, staring at the card Ala was holding in her little hand. Her heart was pounding wildly.

– Ala, did you put that together yourself?

– Yes – answered Ala with pride. – You love Ala.

Kasia sat down on the floor and hugged her.

– I love Ala very much. And I always will.

Ala pulled away.

– So can I have an ice cream now?

Kasia laughed.

– First dinner. You eat your dinner, you get the ice cream. But remember, it's not a reward. It's dessert.

Ala looked at her.

– Why isn't it a reward?

– Because a reward is something you get for doing something you didn't want to do. Dessert is part of the meal. A different part, but a part.

Ala thought for a moment.

– So can I have dessert without dinner?

Kasia smiled.

– No.

– But why?

– Because.

Ala sighed with exaggerated seriousness.

– Then I'll eat my dinner. And then the ice cream.

Kasia looked at her. This was a different child than three months ago. A child who was beginning to understand that the world has rules. And that those rules are not against her, but for her.

---

## IV. The screen

Tomek was sitting in the living room, scrolling through his phone. Ala came to him with a little book.

– Daddy, will you read with me?

Tomek looked up.

– In a minute, I'm finishing something.

Ala sat down next to him and waited. Tomek kept reading. Ala waited. After five minutes she got up and went to Kasia.

– Mummy, daddy doesn't want to read with me.

Kasia looked at Tomek.

– Tomek, she waited for you for five minutes.

– Oh, sorry, I didn't notice.

– But that's exactly what Professor Cieszyńska was talking about – said Kasia. – You're showing her that the screen is more important than she is. And then she, too, will look for an escape in the screen.

Tomek put his phone down.

– You're right. I'm sorry, Ala. Come on, let's read.

Ala came back and sat on his lap.

– What are we reading?

– You choose.

Ala picked a little book about the adventures of Jagoda and Janek. Tomek started reading. Ala pointed at the syllables with her finger.

– There's "ja" – she said.

– Yes, there's "ja".

– And here "ko" – she added.

– And "ty" – Tomek finished.

Ala smiled.

– I like reading.

– And I like reading with you.

Kasia watched them from the kitchen. She knew this was only the beginning. That there would be worse days, that there would be moments when she would want to give up. But at this moment, looking at them, she felt that it all made sense. That she was building something that can't be bought. Not with money. Not with gadgets. Only with time.

---

## V. A year later

Ala was five years old when she started reading books by herself. Not all of them. Not ones with long sentences or difficult words. But ones that had a story. And in those stories she found something no screen had ever given her: her own imagination.

One day she brought a drawing to school. The drawing showed a house, a tree and the sun. But not like from a template. The sun had a face and was smiling. The tree had arms with which it hugged the house. And the house had windows that were looking.

– What is this? – asked the teacher.

– It's my world – answered Ala. – I made it up.

The teacher looked at Kasia.

– Is she your daughter? – she asked.

– Yes.

– When did she start reading?

– At the age of four.

– And how did that affect her imagination?

Kasia smiled.

– I think it wasn't reading that affected her imagination. It was her imagination that made her want to read. To get to know the world. To create it anew.

The teacher shook her head in disbelief.

– Do you know that this is very rare? That children her age can't read, let alone make things up?

– I know. But I didn't want her to be like other children. I wanted her to be like herself. And she is creative. And aware.

The teacher took the drawing in her hands.

– This is beautiful. Truly beautiful.

Ala walked up to Kasia and whispered in her ear:

– Mummy, she thinks I'm clever.

– And you are clever – answered Kasia.

– But not because I can read. Because I can think.

Kasia hugged her. And then she understood that what she had done was not only teaching reading. It was teaching someone to be human.

---

## VI. Epilogue

Ala was ten years old when she wrote her first story. It was about a girl who learned to read in a world where no one believed it was possible. About a girl who discovered that words can create worlds.

Kasia read the story and burst into tears. Not out of sadness. Out of joy.

– Mummy, why are you crying? – asked Ala.

– Because I'm proud. And happy. And grateful.

– For what?

– That back then, when you were four, I didn't give up. That I tried. And that you tried.

Ala looked at her.

– I'm grateful too.

– For what?

– That you didn't give me a phone. That you gave me books. And that you taught me that I can.

Kasia smiled through her tears.

– Now go. Write another story.

Ala left the room, but after a moment she came back.

– Mummy?

– Yes?

– What do you think would have happened if you had given me that phone back then?

Kasia thought for a moment.

– I think there would be no story. And there would be no this conversation. And there would be no you, the way you are.

Ala nodded.

– Good thing you didn't give it.

And she left.

Kasia was left alone in the room. She looked out the window at the city where her daughter was growing up, and thought about how many things could have gone differently. If back then, four years ago, she had chosen the easier path. If instead of books she had given her a tablet. If instead of time she had given her a cartoon.

But she hadn't done that.

And that was the most important thing.

---

**THE END**
