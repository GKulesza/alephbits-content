**Four in a Boat**

Tomek woke up at five in the morning, as always. After years of working in a sports newsroom he had got used to early rising – matches, line-ups, injuries, everything happened before breakfast. But today it wasn't the sound of a phone or an alarm that woke him. It was the silence. That specific silence of a Monday morning, when you know that something has changed, you just don't know what yet.

He reached for his phone. On the screen, a notification from Google Alert – a name he had typed in years ago and never deleted. "Kanał Sportowy publishes results for 2025." He opened the link and for a moment simply stared at the numbers.

Nine and nine tenths of a million in revenue. Three hundred thousand in losses. A drop by one third year on year.

He put the phone down on the duvet and looked at the ceiling. He remembered that day exactly, almost six years earlier, when the four of them sat in a cottage in Masuria – him, Krzysiek, Mateusz and Michał – and sketched out the shareholding structure on a napkin. All equal. Fair. Because after all, what could go wrong? Each had twenty-five percent, each had a vote, each had the same vision – to build the biggest sports channel on Polish YouTube.

"Fair" – he thought now, looking at the ceiling. – "That word should be crossed out in every business dictionary."

He got up, made coffee, sat down at his desk. On the laptop he still had the page with the results open. Next to it popped up a Twitter notification – Krzysiek Stanowski had just bragged that Kanał Zero had passed two billion lifetime views and that from AdSense alone he had generated twenty-two and a half million zlotys. More than the entire Kanał Sportowy had earned in a year.

Tomek laughed bitterly. He opened the desk drawer and pulled out an old, already yellowed napkin. The same one on which they had drawn the company structure back then. "KS – shares: 4 x 25%". Beneath it, Krzysiek's note: "All or nothing."

---

Six years earlier, a cottage in Masuria, a May weekend.

They sat on the terrace, drinking beer and talking about the future. Krzysiek – the visionary, a man who saw further than anyone else. Mateusz – a journalist with the biggest ego Tomek had ever seen, but also with an incredible nose for a story. Michał – steady, deliberate, the one who always said "let's wait, let's think it over". And he himself – the fourth, the least known, but with a head for numbers and organisation.

– Listen – Krzysiek was saying, drawing on a napkin – we're founding a company. Everyone gets an equal share. None of us is more important than the other. We all pull the cart together and we all share the profit.

Tomek remembered that even then something felt off to him. Not that he didn't trust the guys – he did. But he saw how different their approaches to work were, how different their financial needs were, how different their plans for life were. Krzysiek was already wealthy then after selling Weszło. Mateusz was earning extra on the side. Michał had savings. And he, Tomek, had a mortgage and two kids.

– And what if someone cares more than the others? – he asked then.

Krzysiek waved his hand.

– That's impossible. We all believe in this.

Tomek took a sip of beer and said nothing. But in his mind he thought that "belief" is not a good basis for building a company.

---

Four years later, Warsaw, the Kanał Sportowy office.

They sat in the conference room – the same one where they had recorded their first programmes, when everything still smelled of freshness and success. On the table lay a printout with the results for the last quarter. Things were going well. Very well. Revenue was growing, subscriptions were growing, everything was growing.

But the atmosphere was as thick as before a storm.

– I want to keep developing the channel – Krzysiek was saying, tapping his pen on the tabletop. – More formats, more people, more of everything. We have momentum. We have to use it.

– And I think we should focus on what we have – Michał was answering. – We can't spread ourselves too thin. If we take on too many things at once, it will all fall apart.

Mateusz was silent. He always fell silent when he couldn't get his way. And Tomek was counting in his head – how much the new equipment would cost, how many people would need to be hired, what impact it would have on their personal payouts. Because he, unlike Krzysiek, didn't have a financial cushion from previous businesses.

– So let's vote – someone suggested.

And they voted. Two to two. A tie.

Krzysiek looked at Tomek with something that looked like disappointment.

– I thought you'd understand – he said.

And Tomek wanted to answer: "I do understand. But I can't afford the risk that you can afford." He didn't say it. Instead he left the room and called his wife.

– How's it going? – she asked.

– I think I'm starting to understand why partners split up – he answered.

---

Two years later, the registry court, an extract from the National Court Register.

Tomek stood before the clerk and filed an application to be struck off the company. He was the second to leave. The first had been Michał – tired of the constant rows, he said that he "didn't found a sports channel to fight with colleagues every day". The second was him, Tomek – because he understood that a company in which no one has a majority and everyone has the same ego is not a company, but a minefield.

Only Krzysiek and Mateusz remained. The two biggest egos in one room. Like two black holes that cannot exist side by side, because in the end one will swallow the other.

And it did. Krzysiek left, founded Kanał Zero, and Mateusz stayed with a channel that slowly, inexorably began to lose viewers, revenue, relevance.

Tomek now looked at the results for 2025 and thought about what he had said back then, years earlier, in Masuria: "And what if someone cares more than the others?". No one had listened. No one had wanted to listen. Because the vision was too beautiful, too attractive, too fair.

And yet business is not about fairness. Business is about results.

---

He sat at his desk, scrolling on. Meczyki – a channel that had started as a small project of Sebastian Felkner – had just overtaken Kanał Sportowy in terms of viewership. Tomek knew Sebastian. Once, at some industry conference, they had drunk coffee together and Sebastian had told him something that had sounded like heresy at the time:

– I don't take partners. I take employees. And I give them conditions such that they work like partners, but they have no voting rights when a decision has to be made.

Tomek had thought then that it was cold, calculating, corporate. And now he looked at Meczyki's results – growing, stable, without internal wars and public breakups – and thought that maybe Sebastian had been right after all.

---

He remembered one more story. A few weeks earlier he had read about the company mPay. It too had once been on a roll, it too had big plans – a stablecoin, a payments revolution, "better than Blik". The shares were flying up, the pumpers were rubbing their hands. And then the KNF revoked their licence. The shares fell by half in one day. Another company, another collapse, another proof that foundations – not visions – decide whether you survive.

"Interesting" – Tomek thought – "how many mPay investors listened to all those visions and lost their life savings. And how many of them thought that since so many people speak positively about it, it must be safe."

Just as they had all thought about Kanał Sportowy. That since there are four of them, since everyone believes, since everything is divided fairly, it must work out.

---

He closed the laptop. Outside the window the day was dawning – grey, foggy, Monday. He looked at the napkin in his hand and for a moment felt like tearing it up. But he didn't. He put it back in the drawer, under a stack of documents, together with the other keepsakes from that time.

Then he reached for his phone and wrote to Krzysiek. They hadn't talked in months – not because they had quarrelled, but because life had taken different paths. But today, after reading all those results, he felt the need to reach out.

"I saw the results. I'm sorry it turned out this way. But looking back – it couldn't have worked. Four guys with the same competences, the same ego and different wallets. It was a ticking bomb."

The answer came after a few minutes.

"I know. I've been thinking about it too. You know what's worst? That I saw it all from the beginning. I just didn't want to believe it. Because the vision was too beautiful."

Tomek smiled sadly. That was exactly what he had thought back then, in Masuria, when he watched Krzysiek drawing four equal slices of the cake on a napkin.

"Next time" – he replied – "maybe just let one draw, and the rest ask questions."

"Next time" – Krzysiek replied – "there will be no partners."

Tomek put the phone down and looked out the window. The fog was slowly lifting, revealing the roofs of Warsaw's apartment blocks. Somewhere over there, in an office building on the other side of the city, Mateusz was probably already sitting over a strategy for the next quarter. Somewhere else Sebastian Felkner was opening the morning statistics of Meczyki – growing, as always. And somewhere even further away Krzysiek was planning his next big thing, alone, without partners, without votes, without two-two ties.

And he, Tomek, sat at home, drinking his now cold coffee and thinking that sometimes the greatest value you take away from a collapse is not money, or contacts, or experience. But the awareness of what went wrong. And that next time – if there is a next time at all – you won't repeat the same mistake.

Although you probably will. Because business, just like life, gives no guarantees. It only gives lessons.

And lesson number one was: never split a company into four equal parts if you are not ready for the fact that one day two of them will vote against you.

He pushed the napkin deeper into the drawer and went to make himself a fresh coffee.
