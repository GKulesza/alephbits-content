## Text

**GOLD AND ORDINARY HAPPINESS**

In Warsaw's Old Town, in one of the narrow alleyways, stood a shoemaker's workshop whose fame reached far beyond the city walls. They said that Master Straszewski was a descendant of Jan Kiliński himself, the hero of the insurrection, whose monument still stands proudly in the Market Square. Customers came from all over Poland to order shoes from him – they were so durable and comfortable. The master was rich. Unfortunately, he was also stingier than almost anyone.

Penny after penny, thaler after thaler, ducat after ducat – he hid everything in secret caches under the floor. The workers could barely make ends meet, and the apprentice Lutek was the poorest of them all. He ate potatoes, wore patched clothes, and the garments on his back held together only by God's grace and the good will of thread. Even so, he did not complain – at least not aloud. He had learned that in life one must be patient. And that some day, perhaps, fate would smile on him.

---
One evening, after a hard day's work, Lutek went to see a friend who had recently earned his journeyman's title. Over a mug of beer, the old masters began telling Warsaw legends. Finally, one of them, a grey-haired shoemaker, looked at Lutek:

– They say that on Tamka Street, in the cellars of the Ostrogski Castle, a princess enchanted into a golden duck rests. Whoever finds her and breaks the spell, to him she will reveal the secret of gaining countless treasures.

Lutek jumped to his feet.

– So where are these cellars? I'm going there!

The master shook his head.

– Many have tried, my boy. Few came back. And even those who came back returned empty-handed. The legend says it can only be done on St. John's Eve. There is still some time until then.

Lutek sat down, but a fire blazed in his eyes. He decided he would wait. And he prepared. In his head he made plans: how to get through the cellars, how to avoid the traps and how, at last, to gain the wealth that would change his life.

---
The long-awaited day came. St. John's Eve was warm and starry. Lutek, armed with a candle and chalk to mark his way, slipped out of the house and set off for Tamka Street. The Ostrogski Castle towered majestically before him, and its dark windows seemed to look down on him with disdain.

He got inside through a small basement window. The corridors were narrow and winding, and the smell of mustiness and damp almost choked him. He walked straight ahead, leaving chalk marks on the walls every now and then so he could find his way back. At last, after a long trek, he reached a great underground chamber. In the middle of it lay a dark-blue lake that seemed to glow with its own light. And on it – a golden duck wearing a crown.

– I'm here! – cried Lutek with joy. – I've found you, princess!

The duck looked at him, then wrapped itself in a pink mist. When the mist lifted, Lutek saw before him the most beautiful girl he had ever seen. Golden hair, eyes like stars, lips like raspberries.

– What do you want, young man? – she asked in a voice as clear as silver coins.

Lutek, though dazzled, quickly pulled himself together and bowed with a gallantry nobody had expected of him.

– I long for treasures, my lady. I want to be rich and happy. For myself... and maybe for others – he added after a moment's thought.

The princess smiled strangely.

– Very well. Here is a purse with a hundred ducats. You have one day to spend them. But only on yourself. For yourself alone. Not a single penny for anyone else. If you manage this, the countless treasures will open before you.

Lutek snatched the purse and hurried out of the cellars as fast as he could. When he came to the surface, his heart was pounding wildly. A hundred ducats! For one day! That was a fortune!

---
In the morning, when he woke in his shabby little room above the workshop, he already had a plan. He dressed in the most expensive garments he could find in the shop on Krakowskie Przedmieście, bought a gold watch studded with diamonds, a cigar holder he would never use, and a great ruby signet ring. He also ordered the finest carriage to be found in all of Warsaw, with four matched stallions and a coachman in livery.

Everyone looked at him in admiration. "The shoemaker millionaire!" – people whispered in the streets. Lutek felt like a lord, a prince, a king. But when he looked into the purse in the afternoon, it turned out there was still plenty of money left.

– How is one to spend a hundred ducats in a single day? – he wondered, eating a lavish dinner at the best restaurant in the city. He ordered the most expensive dishes, drinks, cigars – and the purse was still almost full.

He started playing cards, then roulette, losing and winning in turn, but the purse only seemed to shrink. Evening was approaching quickly, and Lutek felt panic rising within him. What if he failed to spend all this money? What if he lost his chance at wealth?

And then, as he walked along an empty street of the Old Town, he saw a stooped old man. A war invalid, missing an arm, in a tattered uniform, and on his chest gleamed the Virtuti Militari cross.

– Sir... – the old man groaned. – I haven't eaten for two days. Save me, young man.

Lutek wanted to pass by indifferently. After all, he was supposed to spend that money only on himself. That was the condition. But he looked into the old man's eyes and saw in them the same hopelessness he himself had felt for so many years. In the very moment he understood this, he reached into the purse, poured all the remaining ducats into the old man's hand, and flung the empty purse into the gutter.

– Let come what may – he muttered.

And then lightning lit up the sky, and a dull, angry voice reached him from the earth:

– You broke your promise! You spent the money not for yourself!

Lutek froze. But he felt no fear. He rather felt relief that it was finally over. Because the wealth the princess had promised him was not worth forgetting who he was. And he, a poor shoemaker's apprentice, was someone who could not pass indifferently by human suffering.

– God reward you, sir – said the old man, vanishing into the darkness of the alley.

Lutek smiled. He returned home late at night. He was tired, but strangely at peace. And in the morning he got up, put on his old clothes and went to the workshop, as if nothing had happened. He regretted nothing.

---
And then something extraordinary began to happen. People started coming to him with orders. Not only ordinary townsfolk, but officers, aristocrats, even the voivode himself. His shoes won fame across all of Warsaw. Soon Lutek became a master, opened his own workshop, married the most beautiful girl in the city, started a family and lived long and happily.

He did not gain the treasures the legend spoke of. But he gained something more precious – the respect of people, peace of mind and true happiness. And he knew that it was not gold that was the key to wealth, but the kindness he had shown that night on the empty street.

And the Warsaw legend of the golden duck still lives on. And you can still meet bold souls who try to find her. But those who truly understand her message know that the real treasure waits where an ordinary human heart meets the courage to do something good.

And perhaps that is why the fountain in the courtyard of the Ostrogski Castle still stands, and in its basin a golden duck swims peacefully – a reminder that the greatest wealth is the ability to share what you have, even if it is only the last penny in your pocket.

**THE END**
