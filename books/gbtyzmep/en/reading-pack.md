## Text

**THE STICK AND THE CARROT**

When Magda was seven, her father put her on a stool in the corner of the room and told her to sit there for an hour. She had three questions for him, but she could not ask them, because "you don't talk in the corner". Instead she looked at the white wall and thought about the fact that she was angry. Not about the fact that she had done something wrong. Just angry. At her mother, who had not stood up for her. At her father, who was unfair. And at herself, because she could not be better behaved, no matter how much she wanted to be.

Twenty years passed. Magda had children of her own — Gabryś and Tymon. And when one day Tymon threw himself on the floor in the shop, screaming that he wanted the red toy and not the blue one, the same stool in the corner appeared in Magda's head. The same reflex she knew from childhood: "If you don't calm down, you'll go to the corner."

But Magda did not say it. Tymon was three years old, and his head was buzzing with emotions he could not name. She knew that sending him to the corner would teach him only one thing: that when things are hard for him, he is alone. And that his emotions are a problem that has to be isolated.

Instead, she bent over him, touched his shoulder and said:

– Tymon, I need a hug.

The boy looked at her in surprise. How can you ask for a hug from someone who is screaming and thrashing on the floor? And yet Magda repeated:

– I need a hug.

And then, for the first time, something changed. Tymon stopped screaming. He looked at her as if he were seeing her for the first time. And he threw himself into her arms. A moment later the crying subsided. And Magda, holding him, thought: "It works. It really works."

But it was not always so easy. There were days when Magda felt she was about to explode. When her patience ran out and the same voice she had heard in her childhood switched on in her head: "You have to be strict, or your children won't respect you." When she wanted to shout, forbid, punish.

And then she remembered the four long-term effects of punishment she had read about in books on positive discipline. About the resentment that grows in a child who feels treated unfairly. About revenge, which makes him think: "I'll show you." About rebellion, which makes a child do things out of spite, to prove that no one has power over him. And about retreat, which can turn into low self-esteem — "I'm a bad person, I'm good for nothing."

And she knew she did not want that for her children. She did not want Gabryś and Tymon to one day, as adults, go back in their minds to the stool in the corner and feel only regret.

---

In Magda's home there was no system of punishments and rewards. No reward charts where a child collected stars for good behaviour. No "if you tidy your room, you'll get ice cream". No "if you don't tidy it, there'll be no TV".

– That sounds like black magic – a friend once said when she told him about her approach. – How on earth do you teach a child right from wrong without punishments and rewards?

Magda smiled.

– Let me tell you something – she replied. – Imagine you go to work. Your boss promises you a bonus for every project done well. And for every mistake — he deducts from your pay. How would you feel?

– Like he was treating me like a dog.

– Exactly. And what would you do?

– I'd try not to make mistakes. But not because I want to be better. Only because I'm afraid of punishment.

– And that is the heart of the problem. When a child gets a reward, he learns that something is worth doing only if he gets something in return. He loses his natural motivation. And when he is punished, he learns to avoid punishment — not to understand why something is wrong.

The friend looked at her as if she had just reinvented the wheel.

– So you're saying I shouldn't tell my child: "If you tidy your room, you'll get chocolate"?

– Exactly. Instead, ask: "What can we do to make your room pleasant?" or "Do you need help tidying up?". When a child feels he has an influence on the situation, he wants to act. Not for a reward, but for the satisfaction of having done something important.

---

The hardest thing for Magda was not punishing when something went wrong.

She remembered how Tymon, at three, spilled juice on the freshly washed carpet. Her first reflex was to shout: "What have you done?!" And then, a moment later, she saw the terrified eyes of her child. She saw him shrink into himself, expecting punishment.

Instead of shouting, Magda took a deep breath.

– Tymon – she said calmly. – You spilled the juice. Now the carpet is wet and dirty. What can we do?

The boy looked at her in silence.

– We can wipe it – she added. – I need a cloth. Will you get it for me?

Tymon ran to the kitchen and brought back a cloth. Together they wiped the juice off the carpet. And then Magda said:

– See? When something gets spilled, it has to be cleaned up. Next time, remember to put the cup away from the edge of the table. Then it won't tip over.

That was a consequence — not a punishment. Magda did not shout, did not take away his toy, did not send him to the corner. She showed him what to do when you make a mistake. And she gave him the chance to fix it himself.

– But it's hard – she said later to her husband. – Because inside me there's still that voice: "He deserved punishment, he did something wrong, he has to face the consequences."

– But he did – her husband replied. – He wiped the carpet. That was the consequence.

– Yes, but I wanted to tell him it was the last time, that if he spilled again, then... exactly. That would have been a punishment.

– I know. And what would it have changed?

– Nothing. He would have learned only that mummy is angry. And that when he does something wrong, it's better not to say anything.

Her husband smiled.

– Then I think you already know what to do.

---

One evening Magda was reading a book to Gabryś. It was the moment she loved most — silence, peace, closeness. Suddenly Gabryś asked:

– Mummy, why are you never angry?

Magda froze.

– I am angry sometimes – she replied.

– But you never shout. You never send me to my room.

– Because it doesn't help. When you're angry, you need someone to help you calm down. Not someone to leave you alone.

Gabryś thought for a moment.

– And how do you calm down?

– Sometimes I take a deep breath. Sometimes I count to ten. Sometimes I say: "I need a moment". And then you help me by giving me that moment.

– And can I do that too?

– Of course. When you feel you're about to explode, you can say: "I need a moment". And then we'll leave you alone, or hug you. Whatever you prefer.

Gabryś smiled and snuggled into her.

Magda thought to herself that this was the most important thing: not teaching children how to be obedient, but how to cope with their emotions. That punishment teaches only the avoidance of punishment. And that conversation teaches understanding. And that sometimes, instead of telling a child to go to the corner, it is enough to say: "I need a hug."

And then that child — the same one who a moment ago was screaming and stamping his feet — throws himself into his parent's arms, because deep down that is all he needs.

---

Magda was not perfect. There were still days when she lost patience. When the voice in her head whispered: "Punish him, that will teach him." But every day she tried to remember what she had learned over the years: that punishment and reward are two sides of the same coin. That both destroy inner motivation. And that the only way to raise a child who will know how to make decisions, face consequences and feel valued, is to give up both.

One day, when Gabryś came back from kindergarten, he said:

– Mummy, at kindergarten there's a thinking corner. When someone is naughty, he has to sit there.

– And what do you think about that corner? – asked Magda.

Gabryś shrugged.

– I don't know. When I sit there, I think that the teacher is unfair. And that I want to go back to playing.

Magda smiled.

– And if you had your own corner at home, what would be in it?

– My teddy bears. And a blanket. And books.

– And would you call it a thinking corner, or something else?

– A calm corner – answered Gabryś. – Because when I'm calm, I think better.

Magda hugged him tighter.

She knew that many more times she would be tempted to reach for the old methods. That she would want to shout, forbid, punish. But she also knew that every such action was a step backwards. And that if she wanted to raise children who would know how to deal with their emotions, who would be independent and responsible — she had to walk this path, however hard it might be.

Because it is not about children being obedient. It is about them being ready for life.

**END**
