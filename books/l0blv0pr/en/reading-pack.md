## Text

**THE GIRL WHO VANISHED AT DAWN**


When Joanna Nowak first saw the CCTV footage, she knew this was not an ordinary disappearance. She had been a police officer for fifteen years, but this case – the disappearance of eighteen-year-old Agnieszka Kowalska from Gdynia – had something about it that kept her awake at night.

It was supposed to be simple. The girl was coming back from a party, walking barefoot because her high heels were pinching, her phone had run out of battery, and then... nothing. As if the ground had swallowed her.

But Joanna knew the ground doesn't swallow people without a trace. And she also knew that the first forty-eight hours are the most important time in any investigation. Yet in Agnieszka's case, that time had been wasted.


**Point zero**

4:12 in the morning. A CCTV camera at the entrance to the beach records the last image of Agnieszka. She is walking barefoot, holding her high heels in her hand. Her step is steady, but clearly stressed. Someone who doesn't know behavioural analysis would say the girl is simply tired. Joanna saw more.

– Look – she said to her partner, Marek. – She's not walking like someone who wants to continue the party. She's walking home. And she's so focused on that goal that she stops paying attention to her surroundings.

– False sense of security threshold syndrome – muttered Marek.

– Exactly. She thinks she's already close to home, so her alertness drops to zero. And that's when, in that blind spot of the cameras, someone stopped her.

Joanna stared at the screen. The recording ended where Agnieszka was supposed to step into the shadow of the trees. There were no more shots. The cameras in the housing estate had not been secured in time, and the records were overwritten by new data. Another piece of evidence that could have been crucial was lost forever.

– Why didn't anyone secure those recordings? – she asked, though she knew the answer. The first report had been treated as a teenager running away. Standard procedure in holiday season. Except that this time it wasn't about any runaway.


**A mobile capsule**

Joanna closed her eyes and tried to enter that night. Four in the morning, the middle of summer holidays, a seaside district of Gdańsk. At this hour there is no silence here. Anglers, runners, people going to early shifts, partygoers returning home.

– If someone took her from here, they must have done it within seconds – she said to Marek. – They had to act where there are no cameras. Or in a car.

– A car – repeated Marek. – A mobile capsule.

– Yes. Someone she knew stopped and offered her a ride. Or a stranger who took advantage of her helplessness. In either case she got in voluntarily. No struggle, no screams. If there had been an attack on the street, someone would have heard it.

Joanna opened her eyes. In front of her on the desk lay photographs of Agnieszka's shoes – the model her mother had bought after her daughter disappeared, to understand how she could have felt that night. Joanna knew that for the mother this wasn't a whim. It was an attempt to step into her child's skin, an attempt to understand the pain that led towards home.

– Her mother bought the same shoes – she said quietly. – She wanted to feel what Agnieszka felt, walking barefoot on the asphalt.

– And what did she feel?

– Pain. And tiredness. And relief that she was almost home. And then someone took that relief away from her.

Joanna got up and walked to the window. Beyond the glass stretched the grey Gdańsk sky. Somewhere out there, in Regan Park, in the Jelitkowo housing estate, in the forests of the Tricity – anywhere – an answer was waiting.

– Interviews – she said, turning to Marek. – We have to go back to the interviews.


**A conspiracy of silence**

Marek spread the interview records of Agnieszka's friends on the table. There were over forty people. Each of them told the same story. "We argued about nothing. Agnieszka got upset and left. We don't know why. We don't know what happened."

– That's impossible – said Marek. – Forty people can't remember the same night in the same way. Human memory is chaotic. Everyone remembers something different.

– Exactly – replied Joanna. – They agreed on this version together. Before the police interviewed them, they had time to talk it out. And it's not that they're all murderers. It's that each of them has something to hide. Alcohol, drugs, lies to their parents. And they were afraid that if they told the truth, they would become the suspects.

Joanna picked up the polygraph results. Over forty people from Agnieszka's circle had been tested. Almost every one of them reacted with stress to the key questions. In the public opinion this was taken as proof that they were all involved in the murder.

Joanna knew better.

– It's the shared secret syndrome – she explained to Marek. – They're not hiding a killing. They're hiding their own secrets. And that clouds the picture. It's like trying to find a needle in a haystack when the whole haystack is made of needles.

– So what do we do?

– We look for that one needle that has a different colour.


**Systemic inertia**

Years passed, and the case of Agnieszka Kowalska remained unsolved. The police formally closed the investigation in 2012. The girl's mother, who had never accepted the prosecutor's decision, fought on. She wrote letters, filed appeals, met with journalists. Her determination meant the case never fully died.

Joanna watched from the sidelines. She saw how Agnieszka's mother became an icon of the fight for justice. She also saw how the media began to exploit her tragedy for profit. Books, podcasts, articles – everyone wanted to tell Agnieszka's story, but no one wanted to help her mother get to the truth.

– This is a vampire market – Joanna once said to Marek. – They feed on her pain. They publish one theory after another, but none of them brings her closer to an answer.

– And she cooperates with them anyway – replied Marek.

– Because she has to. It's the only way for the case to stay alive. If she closed the door on the media, everyone would forget about it. And then the killer could sleep peacefully.

Joanna picked up the latest book about Agnieszka's case. The author, an investigative journalist who had been the mother's ally for years, had just published the second part. In the first he promised a breakthrough. In the second he was selling the same theories the police had had ten years earlier.

– They're not looking for the truth – she said quietly. – They're looking for sensation. And as long as this case remains unsolved, they'll keep making money on it.


**The X-Files archive**

In 2019, the case of Agnieszka Kowalska was transferred to the Kraków department for unsolved crimes, known as the X-Files. That's where Joanna and Marek – transferred from Gdańsk – were to start over.

At the very beginning they cut the mother off from information. Not out of malice, but out of necessity. For years the woman had been a source of leaks to the media, unwittingly making it easier for the perpetrator to follow the progress of the investigation.

– It has to be silence – Joanna said at the first team meeting. – No press conferences, no leaks, no "anonymous sources". Until we have something concrete, we work in absolute secrecy.

They started from scratch. They analysed all the camera footage that had been secured – although most of it no longer existed. They used new technology to recreate Agnieszka's route in 3D, simulating the angles of light and the density of the trees at 4:12 on the morning of July 17. They analysed every vehicle that had passed through Sopot and Gdańsk that night, looking for anomalies in traffic.

And finally, in December 2022, they made a move that shook public opinion. On the prosecutor's orders, the area around the Zatoka Sztuki venue in Sopot was searched, and in Kraków, Paweł P., one of Agnieszka's closest friends, was detained.

He was charged with obstructing the investigation, removing traces and tampering with evidence. He couldn't afford to commit murder – but he knew more than he said.

Joanna watched as Paweł P. walked out of the courtroom. He was pale, but not broken. He knew the charges were serious, but not serious enough to sentence him to life in prison. He hoped the truth would never come out.

– He knows where the body is – said Marek as the doors closed.

– I know – replied Joanna. – And we're going to find out.

– How?

– With patience. And technology. Let them think they've won. Let them think they've worn us out. We're waiting for their mistake.


**The crack**

A year later Joanna received a phone call from Agnieszka's mother. The woman was ill – her immune system had failed after years of permanent stress. She spoke quietly, as if every word cost her enormous effort.

– Will you find him? – she asked.

– We will – answered Joanna.

– You promise?

– I promise.

The conversation ended. Joanna put down the receiver and looked at the photo of Agnieszka that still hung on the board in her office. The girl was smiling, as if the whole world was ahead of her.

And then Joanna returned to the analysis. To the traces no one wanted to see. To the digital shadows that the X-Files archive had just begun to illuminate with new algorithms. To the time that still kept working in the killer's favour.

– We'll wait – she said to herself, closing her eyes. – We'll wait until it cracks. And then we'll catch him.

Dusk was falling outside the window. Somewhere out there, in the darkness, lurked a man who knew what had happened to Agnieszka. And Joanna knew that sooner or later – thanks to digital mathematics, cold investigative psychology and a patience bordering on obsession – the perpetrator would make a mistake.

And then the truth would finally come out.


**THE END**
