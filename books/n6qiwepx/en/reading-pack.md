## Text

**THE GOOD GIRL**

She was five years old when she first heard those words. She didn't remember the circumstances, only that her mother smiled at her over breakfast and said: "Zosia, you're such a good girl."

And that was like a screw. Like something that twists into the middle of a person and never comes out again. From then on, Zosia knew who she was supposed to be. Well-behaved. Good. Perfect. Someone who causes no trouble, who studies for straight A's, who wears her hair in a ponytail and doesn't shout, even when she wants to.

---
In her family home there were always newspapers. They lay on the table, on the floor, on the shelves. Her father, a man with big hands and an even bigger heart, read them at breakfast, at dinner, in every free moment. Zosia grew up among words – printed, spoken, whispered. She knew that words have power. That they can build or destroy. That you have to weigh them like gold.

She had two sisters, twenty years older. For Zosia they were like aunts, like second mothers. She watched their lives from a distance, saw their mistakes, their successes, their disappointments. And she learned from their mistakes. She wouldn't repeat the same errors. She would be better. Perfect.

Because that's what the world expected of her. And she, the good girl, always gave the world what it wanted.

---
An all-girls school. Six years among girls. For others it was a prison, for Zosia a refuge. Where there were no boys, there was no rivalry. There were friendships that survived to this day. There was a solidarity she never found again.

But that didn't mean it was easy. Every day, when she left home in the same navy-blue uniform, she felt the weight of expectations on her. She had to be the best. She had to have straight A's. She had to be a role model. Because if she wasn't, she would disappoint her parents. And disappointing her parents was the worst thing that could happen to her.

So she was. Every day. Hour after hour. She shut herself in her room with her books, with Harry Potter, with the diary she received at the end of every school year. She wrote everything. About parents who didn't understand her. About sisters who had it easier. About boys who never looked at her. About being alone, even in a crowd.

And then she closed the diary and hid it in a drawer. Because a good girl doesn't complain. A good girl smiles.

---
Tennis, dance, singing, languages. Activities she hated but had to do. Because her parents wanted her to be prepared for life. To know how to do everything. To never have to ask for help.

– Zosia, you'll see – her dad used to say. – You'll be grateful we taught you. One day you'll thank us for everything.

They were right. They did it out of love. But Zosia couldn't forgive them. Not for pushing her. For never asking what she wanted.

And then, one day, something inside her broke. It was an ordinary morning, before leaving for choir rehearsal. She was maybe fifteen. She packed her bag, got dressed, left her room. And stopped in the bathroom doorway. And couldn't take another step.

– I'm not going – she said quietly.

– What? – her mother looked at her, surprised.

– I'm not going. I don't want to go there. I never wanted to.

She locked herself in the bathroom and sat there until she heard her parents cancel the trip. That day, for the first time in her life, she said "no". And although she was afraid the world would collapse, it didn't. Her parents still loved her. And she felt as if she were breathing for the first time in years.

---
University. Journalism. A disappointment shaped like lectures about newspaper print runs and the founding dates of television stations. Zosia wanted to write, and they taught her history. She wanted to create, and they told her about statistics.

So she changed course. She went into European studies, because she was tempted by Erasmus, travel, discovering the world. And there she met her husband. There, where she least expected it.

He was the first boy who looked at her. And the first one she looked at. For years she had been convinced she was invisible. That there was nothing in her that could interest anyone. And he came and said: "I see you."

And then, for the first time, she understood that she didn't have to be perfect. That she could simply be herself.

---
Working in the media. A newsroom where grown men cried after meetings. They waged wars over positions, over titles, over recognition. And she, the good girl, watched it all from the side and thought: "This isn't for me."

She'd had enough. Enough of the lies the media tried to sell to viewers. Enough of the emotion industry where the click mattered, not the truth. Enough of people who said one thing and did another. Enough of herself, pretending everything was fine.

In 2017 she left. She resigned from her job at the prime minister's office, from her work at WP, from that whole world that was devouring her. She went back home. To her husband. To the children who didn't exist yet, but were already waiting.

And she began to breathe.

---
The pandemic. A pregnancy that wasn't easy. Seven months in bed, with a phone in her hand, with her husband in the next room, with the sound of her own thoughts in her head. It was a time when, for the first time, she felt that she really needed help. That she couldn't manage to be perfect. That some things were bigger than her.

She read every guide she could find. And every time she felt worse. Because the guides said: "You have to be calm", "You have to be understanding", "You have to be perfect". And she wasn't. And she felt she was failing.

Until one day, lying in bed, she understood that it was all a lie. That there is no single right path. That every parent is different. That you don't have to be perfect to be good.

And then she began to write.

---
Then came her little son. And Zosia, who all her life had done everything to be good to others, understood that now she had a chance to be good to him. Not the way her parents had raised her – through demands, through expectations, through disapproval. But differently. Through listening. Through being there. Through showing that you can be imperfect and still be loved.

She started recording conversations. At first only for herself, for the family. Then on Instagram, TikTok, YouTube. And it turned out that people needed this. That hundreds of thousands of mothers, fathers, grandparents – everyone was looking for the same thing: someone who would tell them that it's enough.

And Zosia, who all her life had hidden her sensitivity behind a mask of perfection, began to show it. In her videos, in her stories, in conversations with her son, who was three years old and spoke better than some adults.

Because she talked to him as a person. Not as a child. Not as someone who doesn't understand. She took him seriously. And he answered her.

---
– Why do people love you so much? – the journalist asked her once in a podcast.

Zosia thought for a moment.

– Maybe because – she said – I'm simply normal. Because I show that you can be a mother and be yourself. That you don't have to be perfect to be a good parent.

– And what would you say to that little girl who wrote diaries in her room?

Zosia smiled.

– I'd tell her: "It will be all right. You don't have to be so perfect. Being yourself is enough."

---
Then Zosia talked about work, about her children, about her husband. About how she learned to say "no" – after thirty years of life, after decades of being a good girl. About how she still has a problem accepting compliments, because in her head she still hears a voice saying: "You don't deserve it."

But also about the fact that she works on herself every day. That she goes to therapy, even though she's afraid. That she talks to her husband about her fears. That she's learning to be assertive – not only with the world, but also with herself.

At the end of the conversation, when the journalist asked whether she believed in fate, Zosia answered:

– Yes. I believe someone is watching over me. And I believe that if we listen to our intuition, if we are authentic, life leads us where we should be.

And then, for the first time in this conversation, I understood that Zosia isn't perfect. That she never was. That all her life she had been learning to be herself, throwing off the mask of perfection she had put on as a little girl.

And that that is her greatest success.

---
Zosia took a sip of water. The studio was warm, the bulbs lit her face, which – despite everything, despite years of struggle, despite the hard moments – still radiated.

– Thank you – she said. – For letting me be myself.

– You were – answered the journalist. – And that's exactly what makes you so special.

Zosia laughed quietly.

And somewhere in her head, in the drawer with the diaries, a little girl holding a notebook said: "See? I told you it would be all right."

**THE END**
