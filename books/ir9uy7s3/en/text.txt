**THE DANCE**

## I. THE BEGINNING

Mai stood in the middle of an empty hall in Stockholm and looked at her reflection in a large mirror. She was wearing the traditional Hmong outfit – hand-embroidered, with colourful fringes that rustled with every movement. Snow was falling outside the window, and she felt something inside her breaking.

For the past three years she had worked at a corporation. Digital transfers, apps, cards, accounts. Everything was convenient, fast, modern. Until last week, when the payment system crashed all over Sweden. She stood in line at the grocery store, holding a basket of food, and the cash registers did not work. For a moment she felt helpless – no cash, no way to pay, no control.

Then she remembered the money she had hidden in a drawer a year ago. Banknotes she kept for a rainy day. She took them, went to the little shop on the corner, where an elderly lady accepted them without hesitation. And she bought bread, milk, eggs.

– This is magic – she said to herself then.

But now, looking in the mirror, she understood that the real magic was somewhere else.

---
## II. THE DEVELOPMENT

Mai was born in a village in the mountains of northern Vietnam. Her mother was a Hmong dancer, and so was her grandmother. Dance was in her blood – every movement had meaning, every gesture told a story. When she was ten, her family emigrated to Sweden. A new country, a new language, new rules. She quickly learned that to survive, you had to be like the Swedes – digital, efficient, cashless.

For years she forgot about the dance. About her roots. About the fact that once, as a little girl, she had danced on the green hills, and people from the whole village had watched her with pride.

Until one day, by chance, she came across a video on the internet. Girls in traditional outfits, dancing against a backdrop of mountains. Dancing the way she once had. Someone had written under the video: _"Beautiful dance, wonderful girls. You can feel the joy and the lightness."_

Mai felt something trembling inside her. She didn't know whether it was emotion or longing.

---
## III. THE TURNING POINT

A week later, Mai signed up for Hmong dance workshops in Stockholm. The hall was small, but full of people – Vietnamese, Swedes, and even a few Poles who had come out of curiosity. The instructor, an elderly woman with grey hair and calm eyes, looked at her and smiled.

– You've come back – she said.

– What?

– You've come back to yourself. Your body remembers. Just let it dance.

Mai began to dance. At first her movements were stiff, uncertain. But with every minute she felt something unlocking inside her. As if someone had lifted a weight she had been carrying for years.

After the workshop, a young woman came up to her.

– I'm Ania – she said in Polish. – I'm here on a student exchange. I was looking for something that would remind me of home. And I found it here.

– Home is not a place – answered Mai. – Home is a feeling. And you can find it anywhere, if you only let yourself dance.

Ania smiled.

– This is magic – she said.

– No – replied Mai. – It's just dance.

---
## IV. THE ENDING

A year later, Mai ran her own Hmong dance workshops. Not in Stockholm, but in Warsaw, where she had moved with her partner. In the hall where she taught hung photographs of mountains, greenery, people in traditional outfits. And always, at every class, she said something she had remembered from her grandmother:

– Dance is not just movement. It is a conversation with your ancestors. It is a way of remembering who we are.

The students came from all over. Some were looking for a connection with culture, others – an escape from everyday life. But they all found the same thing: peace, joy, lightness.

And when someone asked her whether she believed in magic, she answered:

– I believe in dance.

---
## V. THE EPILOGUE

One evening, after the workshop, Mai sat down on the floor and looked at the ceiling. In her hands lay a banknote – a hundred crowns she had brought from Sweden. The money that had once saved her from hunger on the day the system crashed.

She closed her eyes and thought about all the things people call magic. About prayer, about synchronicity, about superstitions, about the cash that gives you freedom. About the dance that makes us feel alive.

– Maybe magic is simply what helps us survive – she whispered to herself. – Maybe it's the dance. Maybe it's the banknote in the drawer. Maybe it's the smile of a stranger in a shop.

She stood up, walked to the window and looked at the night sky. Somewhere out there, in the distance, stars were shining. And she knew that what really matters does not require electricity, or the internet, or anyone's permission.

All you have to do is dance.

**THE END**
