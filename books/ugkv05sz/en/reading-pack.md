## Text

**The New Patient**

Kasia clutched a cardboard folder with her test results in her sweaty hands. Professor Czupryniak's office smelled of disinfectant and something that reminded her of a library – old paper, dust, years of knowledge shut inside the hefty volumes on the shelf behind him. She had waited three months for this appointment. Three months during which she had managed to read everything about insulin resistance. Forums, blogs, diet guides. She had even bought a recipe book – low-glycemic-index breakfasts, gluten-free lunches, dinners made almost out of thin air.

The professor sat down opposite her, not even looking at her documents yet. He was an older man with tired but sharp eyes. When Kasia walked in, he greeted her with courteous resignation, as if welcoming an old acquaintance with whom he had a difficult matter to settle.

– I have insulin resistance – she blurted out before he could say anything. – For ten years. I've seen an endocrinologist, a gynaecologist, two dietitians...

The professor sighed. It wasn't a theatrical gesture, more a reflex of someone who had heard the same words hundreds of times and still couldn't come to terms with them. She handed him the folder. Inside was a stack of laboratory printouts – fasting glucose levels, insulin after a load, the HOMA index with red arrows pointing dramatically upward.

– HOMA 7.2 – he read aloud. – Insulin after two hours: 380. Ms Kasia, do you know what the HOMA index really is?

She hesitated. Of course she knew – insulin resistance, the higher the worse. That's how it had been explained to her. That's what every forum said.

– It's the product of glucose and insulin divided by 22.5. It was invented by Professor Matthews in the eighties for scientific research. – The professor put the results down on the desk and looked at her with something resembling sympathy. – And you know what? There is no norm for this index. None. Laboratories provide it on their own, unasked, and then patients come to me terrified that they have nine, ten, twelve. And I'm supposed to tell them what it means? I don't know. No one knows. Because no one has ever studied it on enough people to establish a norm.

Kasia felt something break inside her. No, it wasn't a break – it was a tremor, as if someone had undermined the foundation on which she had stood for a decade.

– Then why do all these tests? – she asked quietly.

– Good question. – The professor reached for her results and spread them on the desk. – Look. Fasting glucose: 88. Two hours after drinking glucose: 110. Within the norm. Perfect. And insulin? Insulin after two hours: 380. Is that a lot? And how am I supposed to know, since there is no norm for insulin after a load?

He picked up a pen and drew a circle on the margin of some piece of paper.

– Imagine you're sitting on a bench. Your heart rate is 60. Suddenly you jump up and chase a bus. After a minute your heart rate is 180. Is that bad? No. Because the heart beats that fast so you can catch that bus. It's exactly the same with insulin. It's supposed to be secreted as much as needed to keep your sugar within the norm. Your sugar is within the norm. So your pancreas produced exactly as much insulin as was needed. 380 units. If it had produced 500, that would also have been fine. If 200 – also. There is no upper limit, because the body itself knows how much it needs.

Kasia stared at the piece of paper, where the professor was drawing more diagrams – a cell, receptors like locks, insulin like a key.

– So... I'm not ill? – she asked uncertainly.

The professor put down his pen.

– You don't have insulin resistance as a disease. You have obesity. And that is a disease. A real, serious, chronic one. The ICD code: E66. And this – he patted the test results – is just a symptom. Like a rapid heartbeat is a symptom of anaemia. You don't treat tachycardia in someone who has severe anaemia. You treat the cause.

Kasia was silent. Ten years. Ten years of the ketogenic diet, of intermittent fasting, alternating, restrictive. Ten years of metformin, after which she felt weak and nauseous for half the day. Ten years of hearing from successive specialists that she had insulin resistance and that she had to eat less and move more. And now a professor of medicine is sitting in front of her saying that it was all... what? A mistake? A lie?

– Don't think it's your fault – said the professor, as if reading her mind. – You didn't invent your insulin resistance. The system talked you into it. Listen.

And he told her a story that, as he admitted, made him sick every time he recalled it. About how, a dozen or so years earlier, a certain German-Italian metformin manufacturer had influenced the Ministry of Health to introduce reimbursement of the drug for insulin resistance states. States that – mind you – are not a disease. They are not in the ICD classification. There are no diagnostic criteria. There are no norms for the tests that supposedly confirm them.

– It was a money grab – said the professor bitterly. – Metformin reimbursement in Poland cost a hundred and twenty million zloty a year. And the manufacturers rubbed their hands. And suddenly everyone started having insulin resistance. Because when a laboratory gives you a HOMA index with a red arrow, the patient gets scared. And a scared patient will go to the doctor, the doctor will prescribe metformin, metformin is reimbursed... And the circle closes.

Kasia looked at her results. Two red arrows next to HOMA. 7.2. She remembered the first time she had seen that result. She was sitting in her car in front of the laboratory and crying. Because she thought it was a sentence. That she would be ill forever. That that was why she kept gaining weight, even though she barely ate. That it was why she couldn't get pregnant. That it was why her husband...

She cut off the thought.

– And I... – she began, but the professor interrupted her.

– You, like everyone, have obesity. And you know what the best part is? That insulin resistance is not your enemy. It's your friend.

She looked at him, bewildered.

– It's a defence mechanism. – The professor leaned forward slightly. – Cells that are overloaded with fat stop responding to insulin. They hide their receptors because they're saying: "Enough, we don't want more energy, we don't want to keep growing." And if it weren't for this mechanism, you would grow without end. Like a balloon. Like a man who weighed almost seven hundred kilograms.

– Seven hundred?

– Yes. A young man, an American. In his mid-thirties. When they took him to hospital, the fire department had to knock down the wall of his house. He lay on the floor, spilled out like wax. And you know why he weighed seven hundred kilos? Because he didn't have insulin resistance. His cells couldn't say "stop". They accepted everything he supplied. And he grew without limits. And he died.

Kasia felt a cold shiver down her back. For years she had thought of her insulin resistance as a curse. As a betrayal by her own body. And now the professor was telling her that this body – in its clumsy, imperfect wisdom – had protected her from the fate of that man from America.

– But it hurts – she whispered. – What you're saying... hurts. Because if it's not insulin resistance, then what? All my life I've heard that I'm lazy. That I stuff myself. That I have no willpower. I believed it myself. And now you're saying that it was all... that I have a disease called obesity and that it's not my choice?

The professor looked at her with something resembling respect.

– Obesity is a chronic disease. Like diabetes. Like hypertension. And just as with hypertension we don't tell the patient "please stress less and your blood pressure will drop", with obesity we can't say "please eat less and move more". Because it doesn't work. You know it doesn't work, right?

She knew. How many times had she tried? A thousand? Two thousand? Every Monday was a new beginning. Every Friday – a failure. A fridge full of vegetables, and she ordered pizza. A training plan printed and stuck on the fridge, and she lay on the sofa hating herself more and more.

– We have medications – said the professor. – Not metformin. Real medications for obesity.

And he told her about GLP-1 analogues. About once-a-week injections that act on the satiety centre in the brain. About how patients suddenly stop thinking about food – that nagging, constant noise in the head disappears, the food noise that tells you to open the fridge even though you just ate.

– Eighty percent of patients lose weight – said the professor, and in his voice appeared an enthusiasm Kasia hadn't expected. – Not in a month, not with a miracle diet. But within a year they can drop sixteen, eighteen, twenty-two percent of their body weight. And you know what the most beautiful part is? That they can finally do what they'd been told all along: eat less and move more. Because these medications make it possible.

Kasia looked at her hands. Sweaty, with bitten nails. The hands of someone who had been fighting a losing war against her own body for a decade.

– So... that means I can try? – she asked, and her voice was as thin as a child's.

– We can try – the professor corrected her. – But we have to be honest. This isn't a six-month treatment. Obesity is a chronic disease. These may be medications for years, maybe for life. Let's not fool ourselves.

– And what, I'll take these medications and suddenly lose weight?

– Not suddenly. Slowly. Without hunger. Without obsessively counting calories. Without feeling like you're hopeless because you ate a candy bar again. Because these medications make you not want that candy bar. And that is – the professor paused – absolutely revolutionary.

Kasia remembered the patients the professor had mentioned in passing. Women who lost weight and came back to life. Women who, after years of feeling invisible, suddenly discovered they were attractive. Women who left husbands who looked at them with contempt when they weighed a hundred kilos, and who now didn't recognise them in their new bodies.

She thought about Tomek. Her husband. About how for years he hadn't even looked at her. About those remarks at Sunday dinner: "Maybe fewer potatoes?", "Did you exercise today?", "You used to have such a waist...". About how she stayed silent, and then at night ate half a jar of Nutella to drown the shame.

– Divorce – she said suddenly and snorted with bitter laughter. – I read that these medications threaten divorce.

The professor wasn't surprised.

– I've heard that from several of my patients. They said: "Professor, you don't warn us that these medications can end in divorce." Because suddenly, when a woman regains control over her body, she also regains control over her life. And she looks at her husband, who for twenty years looked at her as if she were a piece of furniture. And she thinks: "What did I ever see in him?"

Silence fell. The professor looked at his watch, but didn't rush her. He waited.

– Will you prescribe these medications for me? – she asked at last.

– I will. But first you have to see a dietitian. Our dietitian, who works at the clinic. Not some internet guru of insulin resistance. Someone who understands how these medications work and what you should eat so you don't lose muscle mass. And you have to know that these are not cheap medications. From four hundred to two thousand a month.

– I have savings – said Kasia. – For ten years I saved up for dietitians and supplements. I can spend it on something that really works.

The professor wrote out a prescription. When Kasia took it, she noticed her fingers were trembling.

– I'll give you one more piece of advice – said the professor, when she was already standing up. – Stop thinking of yourself as "ill with insulin resistance". That's not your identity. It's not your fault. It's simply a mechanism that stopped working the way it should. And fortunately, we now have a way to fix it.

Kasia left the office and stood in the hallway for a moment, leaning against the wall. She was holding a prescription for a medication that was going to change her life. And she felt something strange – something she hadn't felt for years.

Relief? No. It was something more.

Hope.

And anger. Anger at ten years wasted on miracle diets, on false experts, on laboratories printing a HOMA index without a norm, on a system that preferred to reimburse metformin than to say aloud: "This is not your fault, this is a disease."

She called Tomek. He answered on the third ring.

– So, did they give you new pills? – he asked, not even bothering with a "hello".

Kasia looked at the prescription. Ozempic. Once a week. The beginning of the road.

– They did – she said. – But first I need to tell you something.

– What?

– That it wasn't my fault. It never was.

She hung up before he could answer. And she walked towards the elevator, her step lighter than in the last ten years.

Behind the wall, Professor Czupryniak was already sitting with a new patient – another person with a folder of results and the word HOMA on top. He sighed inwardly, but outwardly smiled courteously.

– Doctor – the patient began. – I have insulin resistance...

The professor tightened his fingers on his pen, but kept his calm.

– Listen – he said. – I'll tell you a story. About a man who weighed seven hundred kilograms because he didn't have insulin resistance.

And he began to explain. For the sixteenth time that day.

**The body remembers**

A year later Kasia almost didn't recognise her own hands.

She was sitting in the waiting room of the diabetes clinic on Banacha Street, this time not as a patient but as a volunteer in an educational programme for people newly diagnosed with diabetes. Professor Czupryniak had offered her the place six months earlier, when she had gone below ninety kilograms for the first time in ten years. – You now have an insight into something most people don't understand – he had said then. – You can help those who are where you were.

Her hands. Once sweaty, with bitten nails, fingers nervously picking at the skin by the thumb. Now they lay calmly in her lap – even nails, moisturized skin, no trace of nights spent clenching her fists. She had lost twenty-three kilograms. Not in a month, not with a miracle diet. Slowly, week after week, with Ozempic, then with Mounjaro, because the first medication had stopped working after six months. With a dietitian who didn't say "insulin resistance", but "please eat more protein, because if you're losing weight, you can't lose muscle". With a physiotherapist who taught her that movement isn't a punishment for being fat, but something that brings pleasure – first the pool, then the bicycle, and for a month now the gym, which she entered with disbelief, as if crossing the threshold of a temple she had never had the right to enter.

And she had divorced. Not immediately. Not because the medication "threatened divorce", as the professor had joked. Tomek couldn't stand Kasia no longer being his piece of furniture. Once, at Sunday dinner, he made his usual remark: "Maybe fewer potatoes?". And she looked at him – for the first time in fifteen years – and said: "I'll eat as much as I want." It wasn't about potatoes. He understood that. She understood that. Three months later they were signing the papers.

Now she was sitting in the waiting room waiting for her charge – a woman in her fifties with newly diagnosed type 2 diabetes who was supposed to come for her first appointment. Kasia was to accompany her, show her that life is possible, that a diagnosis isn't a sentence, that you can lose weight and not go crazy. The professor called such patients "I send them to Kasia, because I no longer have the strength to explain the same thing for the sixteenth time".

The door of the professor's office opened and a man came out. He was maybe sixty-five, with a belly sagging over his belt, a face grey with fatigue. Kasia noticed he was limping. No, not limping – he was almost dragging his right leg behind him, as if it were made of lead. Behind him came the professor, this time without his usual courteous resignation. He looked tired. Really tired.

– Mr Zbigniew – he said quietly, but firmly. – You have to see this.

The man turned around slowly.

– Professor, I already told you. It'll pass. It always passed.

– It won't pass. – The professor took off his glasses and wiped them with a slow movement. – Sit down. I'll show you something.

Kasia froze. She knew that tone. She had heard it a year earlier, when the professor spread out her test results and said: "There is no such disease."

The man – Zbigniew – sat down heavily on a chair in the waiting room. The professor crouched beside him. Kasia could see how much it cost him – that gesture, lowering himself to the patient's level, literally and figuratively. The professor was not a young man, and yet he crouched on the hospital corridor to look into the eyes of someone who didn't want to listen.

– I'll show you a photo – said the professor, taking out his phone. – This is another patient's foot. He also thought it would pass. He came to me and said: "Doctor, it tingles a bit, it goes numb a bit, but it's nothing." And then he bought himself shoes for his son's wedding. Too tight. He didn't feel they were too tight, because he had neuropathy. Damaged nerves. Do you know what that means? That the body stops telling you something is wrong. And he danced all night in those shoes. And in the morning he took off his socks together with his toenails.

Zbigniew stared at the phone screen. Kasia couldn't see the photo, but she saw the man's face change – first disbelief, then disgust, then something like fear.

– This isn't your fault – the professor continued. – No one explained it to you. No one told you that diabetes doesn't hurt. That sugar can be high for years and nothing will hurt you, until one day you wake up and can't feel your foot. Or worse – you feel something sloshing in your shoe. And it turns out to be pus.

– Professor... – Zbigniew swallowed. – I just... I didn't have time. I was building a house for my daughter. I couldn't stop.

The professor sighed. Kasia saw him clench his jaw – the same grimace she recognised from herself a year earlier. Anger and compassion at once.

– Mr Zbigniew – he said at last – I understand. Really. But listen. That foot... – he pointed at his right leg – ...didn't happen yesterday. You've had it for months. And if you don't start treatment now, in six months you'll come to me with a fever of forty degrees, with phlegmon, with necrosis. And then I'll have no choice. I'll have to amputate that foot.

Silence fell in the waiting room. Kasia held her breath.

– Do you know what amputation means? – The professor stood up, straightened his knees. – It's not like we cut it off and you're healthy. A diabetic foot is a signal that the whole body is ill. That the blood vessels are destroyed. That the heart barely pumps. That the kidneys are failing. Amputation is not the end of the disease. It's only the beginning.

Zbigniew was silent. Kasia saw his gaze wander to her hands – calm, dry, with even nails. She didn't know what he saw there. Maybe a reminder that he had once had such hands. That he could still have them.

– What should I do? – he asked at last.

The professor looked at Kasia. She understood.

– Mr Zbigniew – she said, stepping closer. – I was in the same place. Only not with a foot, but with weight. I also thought it was my fault. That I was lazy. That if I wanted to, I would lose weight. But it was a disease. Obesity is a disease. And diabetes is a disease. Both can be treated. Really.

The man looked at her with something like hope.

– You... You were ill?

– All my life. – She smiled slightly. – But I've been in treatment for a year. And you know what the strangest thing is? That the hardest part was believing it wasn't my fault. That I can take medications and that it isn't cheating. That I don't have to punish myself for every bite.

The professor sat down on a chair next to them.

– You know what the core of the problem is? – he asked, looking at Zbigniew. – Loneliness. You get ill in loneliness. You may have a loving family, friends, but it's you who hurts. Only you know what's wrong with you. And no one can cure it for you. But we can help. You just have to want it.

Zbigniew looked at his hands. Kasia noticed he was wearing a wedding ring – thin, worn, probably as old as she was, maybe older.

– My wife said – he said quietly. – That I should go. That something was wrong. That I drink too much water. That I get up to the bathroom at night. And I said: "Leave it, it'll pass."

– It won't pass – the professor repeated. – But we can stop it.

Zbigniew raised his head. In his eyes Kasia saw something she knew all too well – the fear of a change that seems impossible.

– What do I do? – he asked.

And the professor began to explain. Once again that day. About neuropathy, about how sugar damages nerves like bleach damages fabric, that from glucose in neurons sorbitol is formed – the same sweet alcohol that's in sugar-free gums – and that it swells in nerve cells and they go dull. About how a diabetic foot is not a sentence, but a last warning. That there are medications – GLP-1 analogues – that help you lose weight, and once you lose weight, the sugar drops and the nerves stop being flooded with toxic sugar alcohol. That you have to buy shoes a size bigger and look at your feet every day, and never, ever downplay even the smallest cut.

Kasia listened and remembered her first appointment. She had sat the same way, with clasped hands, unable to believe that it could all be so simple. That you don't have to suffer.

When Zbigniew left the office with a prescription and a referral to a dietitian, the professor came over to Kasia.

– You see? – he said quietly. – This is the worst job in the world. No one comes to me to say "I feel great". Everyone complains. Everyone has some problem. And I, with a smile and kindness, try to improve their condition.

– But sometimes it works – Kasia replied.

The professor looked at her with something resembling gratitude.

– Sometimes it does. And you know what? Those are the moments worth living for. The moments for which I don't throw it all to hell and move to the countryside to keep bees.

She laughed. It was the first time she had seen the professor say something so personal.

– Do you have bees?

– I don't. But sometimes I think about them. Bees don't lie. Bees don't say "I thought it would pass". Bees don't ignore symptoms until it's too late. – He sighed. – But bees also don't come back after a year and say "thank you, you saved my life".

Kasia looked at her hands. Even nails. Dry skin moisturized with the cream she had bought last week – for the first time in years not because she "should", but because she wanted to. She thought about all those years when no one had explained to her that obesity is a disease and insulin resistance is a myth. About the ketogenic diet after which she fainted in the street. About the doctors who said "please eat less" and looked at her with contempt. About Tomek, who never asked how she felt.

And she thought about the professor, who for the sixteenth time that day was explaining the same thing, and yet still found reserves of patience in himself. Who talked about loneliness in illness, and himself listened to other people's complaints and fears every day. Who, when asked whether patients didn't tire him out, had once answered: "They do. But it's not my job to judge whether someone's life is worth living. My job is to help."

– Professor – she said suddenly. – Thank you.

He looked at her over his glasses.

– For what?

– For not believing me. In that insulin resistance. For telling me the truth.

The professor smiled – for the first time that day it wasn't the smile of a tired doctor, but of someone who had really heard.

– Ms Kasia – he said – you told yourself the truth. I only provided a few facts. The rest was done by the medications and your determination.

– And your patience.

– That too. – He put his phone in his coat pocket. – But you know, patience is the only thing I have in abundance. Because in this job, if you don't have it, you can go crazy.

Kasia stood up. In a moment her charge was due to arrive – another frightened woman with a stack of results who thought insulin resistance was a sentence.

– I'm going – she said. – My new patient is waiting.

– Tell her the same thing I told you a year ago.

– That there is no such disease?

– That she is not alone. That you get ill in loneliness, but you don't have to be treated in that loneliness. That she has us. Me, you, the dietitian, the physiotherapist. That we can help her. But she has to want it.

Kasia looked at her hands one last time. They were no longer the hands of a woman who had been fighting a losing war against her own body for a decade. They were the hands of someone who had won – not against the body, but against the myth that had made her believe she was worse, lazier, not good enough.

And she thought about something the professor had said at the end of their first appointment, but which had only reached her now: "Health is like a bank deposit. What you do today, you'll collect in twenty-five years. Or not. It's your decision."

She had made her decision.

She went out into the corridor, where a woman in her fifties was already waiting – tired, frightened, with a folder of results in her sweaty hands. Exactly like Kasia had been a year earlier.

– Good morning – she said, reaching out her hand. – My name is Kasia. I'll be with you. Don't be afraid. It's not your fault.

And she began to tell.

**THE END**
