## Text

**FRACTIONS**

Magda stood in the hallway with a jacket in one hand and a backpack in the other. The clock on the wall showed 7:48. They were supposed to be at kindergarten in twelve minutes. At work in twenty. And her four-year-old daughter Zosia sat on the floor, staring at the wall as if she had no intention of going anywhere.

– Zosia, we're getting dressed! – said Magda, trying to keep her voice calm.

Zosia didn't react.

– Zosia, can you hear me? Jacket, shoes, hat. Quickly.

Zosia looked at her, but didn't move.

– Come on! – Magda felt the irritation starting to bubble inside her. – Get a move on, we're going to be late!

Zosia stood up, walked to the coat rack, took down her jacket and... hung on it, staring into space. Magda sighed heavily and stepped closer.

– Jacket on your shoulders, sleeves, sleeves! – she shouted, pushing her daughter's arm into a sleeve.

– Mum, I do it myself – Zosia protested.

– Then get dressed, I've been telling you for ten minutes!

Zosia burst into tears. Magda closed her eyes. She knew this wasn't the way. She knew her daughter wasn't malicious, wasn't lazy, wasn't doing it to spite her. But she still didn't know how to change it.

---

In the evening, while Zosia slept, Magda opened her laptop and turned on a podcast. It was meant to be her relaxation, a moment just for her. But the first words she heard made her sit up straight.

– Break the task into fractions – said Aga Rogali's voice. – What is obvious to us, for a child is a series of complicated actions that have to be performed in the right order.

Magda held her breath. Aga talked about a boy in class who didn't know he was supposed to walk up to the candle and blow it out until he was given a detailed step-by-step instruction.

– Put on your jacket – said Aga. – For us that's one sentence. But for a child it's: take the jacket off the hook, find the right sleeve, put the right arm into it, deal with the jacket on your back, put the other arm into the other sleeve, and maybe even zip the jacket up. That's many actions, many steps.

Magda remembered the morning. "Get dressed" – she had said. For Zosia that might have meant no more than: "Stand, think about ten things at once, without knowing where to start".

– Children need navigation – Aga continued. – Be there with them, tell them what has to be done in what order. And persevere in making them do it themselves. If there's difficulty, help, but as little as possible.

Magda closed the laptop. For a moment she sat in silence, thinking about the mornings, the evenings, the hundreds of situations in which she had said: "Do this" – and wondered why it didn't work.

---

The next morning Magda got up fifteen minutes earlier. Not because she wanted to. Because she knew she needed that time.

– Zosia – she said, sitting down on the floor next to her daughter. – I'll show you how we get dressed. Together.

Zosia looked at her with surprise.

– First we take the jacket off the hook – said Magda, pointing. – See? The jacket is hanging on the hook. Take it.

Zosia took the jacket.

– Now we find the sleeve. Which sleeve is for the left arm? This one? Good. Put your left arm into it.

Zosia did.

– Now we swing the jacket over our back. We grab the other sleeve and put in the right arm. Great! Now we zip it up.

Magda helped her with the zipper, but only at the last moment, when Zosia got stuck. The rest she did herself.

– You did it! – said Magda, hugging her daughter. – See, you can do it. You're getting better and better.

Zosia smiled. For the first time in many days.

---

But that wasn't the end of the challenges. In the evening, when Magda said: "Get ready for bed", Zosia again froze in place.

– What does that mean, Mum? – she asked.

Magda froze. In her head "getting ready for bed" meant: brushing teeth, putting on pyjamas, going to bed. But for Zosia it could mean anything. Or nothing.

– Come, I'll show you – said Magda. – First: we go to the bathroom. Second: we brush our teeth. Third: we put on our pyjamas. Fourth: we go to bed. We'll do it together.

They went to the bathroom. Magda showed Zosia how to put toothpaste on the brush, how to brush her teeth, how to rinse. Step by step. Without rushing. Without getting upset.

– You did it! – she said when Zosia finished. – Look at those clean teeth. Great job.

Zosia smiled. And suddenly, for the first time, there was no fight.

---

A week passed. Mornings and evenings still weren't easy, but they were easier. Magda had learned to break tasks into small pieces. Into fractions.

Instead of saying: "Tidy up the toys", she said: "Blocks into the box. Then the teddy bears onto the shelf. Then the books onto the bookshelf." And Zosia did it step by step, without getting discouraged.

Instead of saying: "Wash your hands", she said: "Turn on the water, wet your hands, turn off the water, take the soap, lather, turn on the water, rinse, turn off the water, take the towel, dry, hang the towel." Zosia did all of it, looking at her with pride.

– Mum, I know how – she said one day, when Magda started giving her the instructions.

– I know you know – Magda answered. – But sometimes you need a reminder.

Zosia nodded.

– It's like with a song – she said. – When I know all the words, I sing. When I don't remember, you help me.

Magda smiled.

– Exactly.

---

A few weeks later Magda went to work. She was tired, but not like before. Before, she had felt that every day was a fight. Now she felt that every day was a lesson – for her and for Zosia.

One evening, when Zosia was already asleep, Magda opened her laptop and wrote a message to Aga Rogali:

_Dear Ms Agata, thank you. Your podcast changed my life. I learned that "break the task into fractions" is not just a method, but a way of thinking. That when I look at my child, I don't see a malicious person who doesn't want to cooperate. I see someone who needs navigation. And that my patience is the key to their independence._

She didn't send it. Instead she closed the laptop and went to Zosia to straighten her blanket. And she thought that someday, when Zosia was grown up, she might not remember these mornings, these evenings, these small steps. But she would know that she can. That she is independent. That her mother believed in her.

And that was what mattered most.

---

Months passed. Zosia grew. Magda still broke tasks into fractions, but now Zosia did them herself, without instructions, without reminders. Not because Magda had stopped helping her. Because she had helped her learn to help herself.

One day, when Magda came home from work, she found Zosia in the kitchen. She was sitting at the table with a glass of milk. Next to her lay a plate with a sandwich.

– I made it myself – said Zosia with pride. – First I took the bread, then the butter, then the cheese, then I cut it, then I made tea.

Magda smiled.

– I see you remember.

– I remember – answered Zosia. – Because you showed me.

And then Magda understood that what she had been doing wasn't only about getting dressed, tidying up, brushing teeth. It was about something bigger. About showing her daughter that the world is understandable. That it can be grasped. That there's no need to be afraid, no need to rush, that things can be done step by step.

And that in every step there is success.

---

That evening, when Zosia was asleep, Magda sat down on the sofa and turned on another podcast. Not for learning. For pleasure. She listened to Aga Rogali's voice, which talked about something else, something she didn't remember. But in her head the sentence that had changed everything still echoed:

_Break the task into fractions._

And she thought that this sentence could be applied not only to children. To life too. To work, to relationships, to dreams. That sometimes the biggest things start with small steps. And that every step, even the smallest, matters.

She closed her eyes. She felt calm inside. And the thought that tomorrow would also be a good day. Because she had learned that everything can be broken down into parts. And that every part can be done.

You just have to know where to start.

**THE END**
