## Text

**Why Do Radio Waves Pass Through Walls, but Light Doesn't?**

**A story inspired by materials about Richard Feynman**


Warsaw, 1980. A small flat on the fourth floor, outside the window the honking of car horns, neon signs and concrete buildings stretching to the horizon. In the room, on a wooden table, stands an old radio receiver. From its speaker comes jazz – notes flowing from hundreds of kilometres away, from Kraków, or perhaps even from abroad.

The radio signal passes through walls, through concrete, through bricks, through human bodies, to reach the receiver's antenna. Meanwhile, light from the hallway – the same light that lets us see shapes and colours – stops at the wall as if at an invisible dam.

Why?

This question troubled me since childhood. When I grew up, I understood that the answer lies in something that goes beyond our everyday intuition. The physics practised by people like Richard Feynman shows that reality is much stranger than our brain suggests.


**The invisible ocean**

In 1865 James Clerk Maxwell combined electricity and magnetism into a single theory. He proved that light and radio waves are the same phenomenon – electromagnetic waves. They differ only in frequency.

Imagine the electromagnetic field as an invisible ocean stretching across the entire universe. If you move the surface slowly, you create radio waves metres, even kilometres long. If you move it faster – microwaves appear. Even faster – infrared radiation. You speed up still more – visible light appears. And when you shake that field at an extremely high frequency – you get gamma radiation, which can split atoms.

Nature uses one foundation across the whole spectrum. Visible light is nothing special from the point of view of physics. It is only that tiny fragment which the biology of the human eye learned to register.


**The wall that is empty**

When you punch a wall, you feel its hardness. But physics tells a completely different story here.

If you magnified an atom millions of times, you would see that it is essentially empty inside. The atomic nucleus is comically small compared to the whole atom. If the nucleus were the size of a pea in the middle of a national stadium, the electrons would circle around the top tiers – not as hard little balls, but as fuzzy clouds of probability.

What we call a hard object is in reality a field of electromagnetic forces between atoms. When you put your hand on a table, your electrons never actually touch the electrons of the table. What you feel is a powerful repulsive force between the electron clouds. You both carry a negative charge – you repel each other with unimaginable force.

The feeling of physical hardness does not come from matter being a solid block. It comes from the electromagnetic field not allowing electrons to pass through each other.


**The quantum lock**

In 1900 Max Planck made a discovery that shook physics. He discovered that energy does not flow continuously like water in a river, but appears in small, separate portions. He called them quanta.

The equation was surprisingly simple: **E = h × f**

Where E is energy, h is Planck's constant (an extremely small number), and f is the frequency of the radiation.

The higher the frequency, the greater the energy. Radio waves have low frequencies, so their photons are incredibly weak. Visible light is much stronger. Gamma rays carry enough energy to tear atoms apart.

Electrons in atoms can occupy only strictly defined energy levels – like stairs, not a smooth ramp. An electron never stops halfway between steps. It can only be in specific, allowed places.

This means electrons are picky about the kind of energy they absorb. They will absorb a photon only if that photon's energy perfectly matches the difference between the levels of the stairs. If it doesn't match even slightly – the electron simply ignores it.


**The secret of passing through**

And at this point everything becomes clear.

Photons of radio waves carry very little energy. So little that electrons in building materials – brick, concrete, wood – simply ignore them. Radio waves don't have the right amount of energy to force electrons to jump to higher levels.

From the perspective of radio waves, those solid, massive walls simply aren't there. They pass through them almost without resistance.

Visible light plays in a completely different league. Its photons carry energy millions of times greater than radio photons. And most importantly – this energy level perfectly matches what electrons in matter like. Thanks to that match, the particles of matter aggressively absorb, reflect or scatter the visible light falling on them.

The wall that is transparent to radio waves becomes, for light, a wall that cannot be passed.


**The world we don't see**

The human eye perceives only a sliver of the electromagnetic spectrum – from 400 to 700 nanometres. That's all you have ever seen: the blue sky, the faces of friends, twinkling stars, the lights of night-time Warsaw.

Beyond that narrow range there exist radio waves, infrared radiation, ultraviolet, X-rays, gamma rays – they surround you every second. Your brain is completely unaware of them.

In 1800 William Herschel discovered infrared radiation by placing a thermometer where light could no longer be seen. The temperature at that spot rose. Nature had been glowing in infrared all along – people simply hadn't noticed.

A snake perceives infrared radiation as a natural part of its reality. A bee sees ultraviolet patterns on flower petals that are hidden from us. Every species lives in its own version of the universe.

Reality is always much broader than human experience. Right now, billions of radio waves are passing through your body. The infrared radiation from your skin spreads around the room. Cosmic rays from deep space race through the atmosphere. Your brain registers only a tiny sliver of all this and calls it simply "the world".


**The cosmic dance**

The laws of physics that explain why radio waves pass through walls also govern the motion of galaxies.

Our galaxy – the Milky Way – is racing toward Andromeda at about 110 kilometres per second. That's over 400 thousand kilometres per hour. Andromeda contains around a trillion stars – far more than our galaxy.

In about 4–5 billion years these two galaxies will begin to merge. But it won't be the spectacular collision that films show. The average distance between stars is 4–5 light years. If we shrank the Sun to the size of a tennis ball and placed it on a street in Warsaw, the nearest star would be hundreds of kilometres away, in a completely different province.

The probability of two stars directly colliding is negligible. The biggest changes will happen in gravitational orbits. Gravity will draw hundreds of billions of stars into a new dance of spacetime.

The same gravity that made an apple fall on Newton's head controls the motion of galaxies containing hundreds of billions of stars. The universe makes no distinction between what is small and what is enormous.


**The wall and reality**

The deeper I delve into physics, the more convinced I am that science does not make the universe smaller or less mysterious. It does exactly the opposite. It constantly reminds us that what we call reality is much larger and stranger than the biological perception evolution bestowed on humans.

The wall beside you is not nearly as solid as your human brain tells you. What you call hard, transparent or dark – depends only on how a given matter interacts with a wave and how your sense organs interpret that interaction.

Physics absolutely does not claim that the world is an illusion. It merely makes us realise that our perception is dramatically limited. The deeper you look into the interior of an atom, the less it resembles a physical object. It begins to resemble a mad dance of probability and pulsing force fields.

What we carelessly call a wall is the result of trillions of quantum interactions happening in real time with unimaginable speed. Our biological brain gathers all that incomprehensible chaos and sticks a convenient label on it: "hard wall".


**Feynman and the way of looking at reality**

Richard Feynman taught us that physics is not just formulas and equations – it is a way of looking at the world. He explained that matter reacts selectively to different types of electromagnetic energy at the quantum level. Understanding the differences in wave frequencies allows us to look at physical reality in a completely new way, going beyond the limits of human perception.

Once you understand this, you will never look at light and radio waves the same way again. A wall is no longer an obstacle for you – it is a gateway to understanding how nature negotiates with matter at the most fundamental level.

If you were a creature that had evolved the sense of sight using radio waves, the world around you would look radically different. Concrete walls would be a translucent mist for you, not a massive obstacle. Architecture, cities, the experience of space – everything would stand on its head.

Reality has no absolute, fixed form. What you call the world is only an interpretation – one of infinitely many possible ones. And somewhere out there, in that space invisible to you, radio waves still pass through walls, through bodies, through everything – dancing their quantum dance, which has lasted since the beginning of the universe.


_It's not magic. It's not future technology. It's the laws of physics, working around us constantly._


[The End]
