## Text

**SOUTHERN HOSPITAL**

## I. THE BEGINNING

Doctor Marek Kamiński woke up at 5:47, before the alarm had rung. He lay in the darkness, listening to his own heartbeat and the hum of the radiator. Outside the window, Warsaw was only waking to life – the first buses, a dog barking far away, raindrops tapping against the glass.

He knew that today he would again have to do something he hated: go to the Southern Hospital. Not because he didn't like his work. He loved medicine. He loved his patients. But he hated the system that made him feel like a cog in a machine he didn't understand, a machine that was slowly killing him.

He got up, drank some coffee and sat down at the computer. He opened the file with the schedule. For the past three years he had worked at the Southern Hospital as a surgeon. Three years in which he had watched the system that was supposed to heal people become a tool for making money by people who had never touched a scalpel.

– Another day – he said to himself. – Another day in this circus.

He got dressed and left the house. On his way to the hospital he passed the building that housed the Civic Platform headquarters. In front of the entrance stood a small group of journalists waiting for a comment. Marek looked away. He had no strength for politics.

---
## II. THE UNFOLDING

At the hospital everything looked as usual. Crowds of patients in the corridors, doctors in their offices barely having time to talk. Marek entered the ward and immediately felt the familiar discomfort – the feeling that everything happening here was only a pretence.

– Good morning, doctor – greeted him a nurse, an older woman with tired eyes. – A new patient in room four. Waiting for you.

– Thank you.

Marek entered the room. The patient, a man of about sixty, lay on the bed hooked up to drips. He looked tired but conscious.

– Good morning – said Marek, sitting down beside him. – What seems to be the matter?

– Pain in my chest – the patient answered. – For two weeks. The family doctor said it could be the heart, but here... – he paused, looking around the room. – Here nobody has time to examine me properly.

Marek nodded. He knew what the patient meant. He knew that in this hospital doctors' time was accounted for so tightly that there was none left for proper diagnostics. He knew that some doctors worked several jobs at different facilities, logging hours they physically couldn't have worked. He knew the system was broken.

– I'll examine you – he said. – Don't worry.

For the next two hours Marek examined the patient, analysed the results, consulted other specialists. In the end he made the diagnosis: a heart attack, but at an early stage. Had the patient come here a week later, it might have been too late.

Marek left the room and met an older colleague in the corridor, Doctor Nowak.

– Have you heard about the scandal at the Southern Hospital? – asked Nowak.

– Something was in the media – answered Marek. – But I didn't follow it.

– Apparently Trzaskowski was supposed to be kicked off the seat of Warsaw's president. Tusk wanted to get rid of him, but he stood his ground. Not bad, huh?

Marek shrugged.

– And what do I care? I work here. I see what's going on. I don't need politicians to tell me something is wrong.

Nowak looked at him carefully.

– Do you know that what's happening in this hospital is only the tip of the iceberg? – he asked. – The system has been rotting for years. I remember Łódź, twenty years ago. Pavulon was flowing at full speed, doctors were finishing off patients for a pittance. Then only the small fry went to prison, while those who really made the money still have their children in high positions today. It's the same here.

Marek was silent. In his mind appeared a memory from a few years back, when he had worked at another hospital. He had watched then how the system swallowed even the most honest people. He had watched how journalists who tried to expose irregularities were intimidated or bribed. He had watched how politicians who promised change became part of the same system after a few months.

– I know – he said at last. – But what are we supposed to do? Take to the streets? Go on strike? Nothing will change anyway.

Nowak put his hand on his shoulder.

– Maybe nothing will change – he said. – But if we don't talk about it, it certainly won't.

---
## III. THE TURNING POINT

That evening Marek came home late. His wife was waiting with dinner, but he had no appetite. He sat down at the table and looked at the plate for a while, unable to reach for his fork.

– Did something happen? – asked his wife.

– Nothing. Only... – he paused. In his mind appeared a memory from today – Nowak's words about a system that rots. And suddenly he understood something he hadn't understood for years.

– You know what's the worst? – he said. – The worst is that everyone knows what's going on. Journalists know. Politicians know. We, the doctors, know. And yet nothing changes. Because this system is built so that it won't change.

His wife looked at him in surprise.

– And now what?

– I don't know – he answered. – But I think I have to stop pretending I don't see anything.

The next day Marek got up at 5:47. This time he didn't go to the hospital. Instead he sat down at his desk and started writing. Notes, memories, observations. Everything he had seen over the past three years. A system that let doctors earn serious money without working. A system that treated patients like product, not like people. A system that was corrupt from top to bottom.

After a few hours he had a finished text. He read it twice, corrected a few sentences, and then sent it to a journalist he knew at an independent portal.

_"What I have written may be controversial"_ – he wrote in the email. _"But it's the truth. And I think people should know it."_

The answer came after a few hours:

_"Marek, this is amazing. May I publish it? Anonymously, of course, if you want."_

Marek hesitated for a moment. He knew that publishing this text could destroy his career. He knew he could lose his job, his friends, even his family. But he also knew that if he didn't do it, he would regret it for the rest of his life.

_"Publish it"_ – he answered. _"Let people know."_

---
## IV. THE ENDING

The text was published a week later. Within a few hours it had spread across the entire Polish internet. The comments carried voices of outrage, but also voices of support. People wrote that finally someone had said what they had known for years.

Marek received several threatening phone calls. His boss called him onto the carpet and said that if he didn't retract his words, he would have to terminate his contract. Marek refused.

A week later he was fired.

But he didn't feel like a loser.

He left the hospital building for the last time and looked at the sky. Somewhere far off, an aeroplane cut through the clouds. And for the first time in years Marek didn't feel that he had lost.

He felt that he had won.

Because he knew he had done what he had to. That he had told the truth. And that even if the system didn't change, at least people would know how it really worked.

He went home. His wife was waiting for him with a smile.

– I'm proud of you – she said.

– I know – he answered. – And I'm proud of myself too.

He sat down at the table and started writing another text.

This time about how the system swallows even the most honest people. About how politicians who promise change become part of the same system after a few months. About the fact that the only hope for change lies in people who have the courage to speak the truth.

And about the fact that even if nothing changes, it was worth trying.

---
## V. EPILOGUE

A few months later Marek received a message from a woman who worked as a nurse at the Southern Hospital:

_"I read your text. For three years I thought I was alone. That only I saw what was going on. Thank you for writing it. Thank you for showing that we are not alone."_

Marek read the message three times. Then he put the phone in his pocket and went back to writing.

He knew it wasn't the end. That he would keep writing as long as he had the strength. Because he knew that was the only thing he could do.

And that it would be enough.

---
**THE END**
