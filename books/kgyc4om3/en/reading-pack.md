## Text

**THE GIRL FROM YELAN**

Warsaw welcomed her with rain. Anastasia stood on the platform of Central Station, breathed in the damp air and smiled – even though her new pink jacket wasn't prepared for Polish April weather. In Germany, where she had lived for nine years, rain was different. More... German. Here, in Poland, it had something familiar about it, though she didn't know why. Maybe the sound of raindrops hitting the station roof reminded her of the sound of raindrops hitting the tin roof of the house in Yelan.

She had come here to learn Polish. Not for practical reasons – she had no family here, no career plans. She simply, one day, heard the sound of the language and felt that she wanted to have it inside her. As if her soul had recognised something her conscious mind hadn't yet grasped.

She got into a taxi. The driver, an older man with a moustache, said something in Polish. She understood only half of it. She answered in Russian, and he smiled broadly.

– Russian? – he asked.

– Yes – she answered in her best Polish, which was still more German than Slavic. – I'm from Russia, but I live in Germany. I want to learn Polish.

– Why? – the driver was surprised. – Don't you have family here?

– No.

– And a boyfriend?

– No.

The driver looked at her in the rear-view mirror and shrugged. He had seen many foreigners coming to Warsaw for various reasons. But for the language alone? That was new.


**A room in the attic**

She rented a small room in Praga, in an old tenement house that smelled of damp plaster and history. On the walls there were traces of old wallpaper – layers that told stories of residents from decades ago. Anastasia put her suitcase on the floor, opened the window and let the sounds of the city in.

Sounds she didn't know from Bonn. There it was quiet. There everything was orderly, arranged, predictable. Here, in Warsaw, she felt something she couldn't name. Maybe it was chaos. Maybe it was life.

She sat on a chair by the window and took out the book she had brought from Germany – "The Doll" by Bolesław Prus. She had bought it at the airport because she saw the cover and thought it would be a good start. She was already two hundred pages in, in the Polish original, with notes in the margins that she made when she didn't understand words.

– "The girl from Yelan" – she said to herself, lingering on the words she had found in the book. – "A young woman who came from a small town to the capital."

She smiled. That sounded familiar.


**The "Nowy Świat" café**

The next day she went to a café on Nowy Świat. She ordered coffee – black, without sugar, because in Germany she had learned to drink it that way. But when the waiter asked: "Popcorn? Sweet or salty?", she froze.

She remembered how in Germany, in the cinema, every time she went to see a film, it smelled of caramel and butter. Salty popcorn was like a foreign planet to her. And yet she tried it. And... she liked it. There was something about it that fit this café, this city, herself.

She sat at a table by the wall. She took out her phone and opened the Polish lesson she had received from her teacher, Karolina. The teacher was more than just a person who taught her the language. She was a guide to a world Anastasia was discovering anew. Every week she sent her songs, articles, book excerpts. It was she who had recommended "Dumplings" to her – a band whose music sounded like love for a language you don't know.

Karolina wrote to her that day: "How's your Polish adventure going? Remember that every mistake is a step forward. Even if you order something too spicy, like last time, it'll be a good story."

Anastasia smiled, remembering that situation. A few days earlier, in a restaurant, she hadn't understood the waiter's question about the level of spiciness. She answered "yes" and got a dish that burned her mouth so much she drank water for half an hour. But she ate it all. Because once she started something, she had to finish it.


**A meeting in the studio**

A week later she sat opposite Karolina Walczowska, who hosted the podcast "Nieobcy" ("Not a Stranger"). She was nervous, but she was smiling. She knew she would have to tell about her life, about Russia, about Germany, about why Polish.

Karolina started with Yelan – a small town where there was no asphalt, no cinema and no prospects. Anastasia talked about it with a mixture of tenderness and relief – that she no longer lived there.

– I always wanted to leave that place – she said. – That doesn't mean I don't have good memories. I do. But they're small, like that town.

– What was the worst thing for you? – asked Karolina.

– The lack of prospects. There's nothing there. You either have a business, or you drink. And that's all.

– And now, how do you see it? After years in Germany?

– Now I know it wasn't my fault that I wanted to escape. It was natural.

Karolina nodded. Then she asked about Moscow, about journalism studies, about what Russia was like before the war.

– Back then you could still talk about politics, invite opposition figures to lectures – said Anastasia. – Now that's impossible. My programme, political journalism, was closed. Officially – because nobody wants to study it. In reality – because nobody wants the truth.

– What is it like to be a Russian woman in Germany, during the war?

Anastasia fell silent for a moment. That question had come back to her every day since Russia attacked Ukraine. Does she feel guilty? No. But does she feel responsible? Yes, in a way. For having to explain that she doesn't support the war, that she's not like her government.

– People sometimes think I'm Ukrainian – she said. – And that's... dangerous. I've been attacked on the street because someone thought I was from Ukraine. And it's terrible that war causes such hatred.

Karolina listened carefully. Then she asked about something that had interested her from the start:

– And why Polish?

Anastasia smiled. It was a question she had no simple answer to.

– I don't know – she admitted. – I just... like the sound of it. It wasn't practical. I had no reason to learn it. But I simply wanted to. Maybe that's real love.

Karolina laughed.

– Well, that looks like real love.


**An evening in Warsaw**

After the recording they went out for dinner together. Anastasia ordered pierogi – because "it's the most important Polish dish, so you have to try it", as Karolina said. She ate them with sour cream and felt that it was good. Not just the food, but the fact that she was here. In Poland. In Warsaw.

– You know – said Karolina, sipping her tea – you're like a typical Pole. You come to Germany, you work, and then you go back to Poland for your holidays.

Anastasia laughed.

– Maybe. Maybe one day I'll stay here.

– You are very welcome.

They left the restaurant as dusk was falling. The city lights reflected in the puddles on the pavement. Anastasia breathed in the air. Warsaw smelled of rain, petrol and something sweet – maybe popcorn from the cinema next door. She didn't know.

She only knew that she felt good here.


**Return to Bonn**

A few days later she went back to Germany. On the train from Berlin to Bonn she wrote a message to Karolina:

_"Thank you for everything. Poland is beautiful, but the language... the language is wonderful. I will learn it. I promise."_

Karolina replied quickly:

_"You're already learning. And you're doing it wonderfully. See you soon."_

Anastasia closed her phone and looked out the window. Landscapes moved past the glass – fields, forests, small towns. Somewhere there, in the east, was Yelan. And somewhere here, in Germany, was her home. But in her heart, since that week, there was something new. A love for a language she doesn't have to use. And for the country that speaks it.

She smiled. She could hear the sentence she had read in "The Doll" repeating in her head:

_"A man often thinks he knows what he wants, and then discovers that he wanted something completely different."_

Maybe that was exactly how it was with her. All her life she thought she only wanted to escape from Yelan. And now she was discovering that she also wanted to return to something.

Not to Yelan. To Poland. To the language. To what she felt when she spoke words that were not yet hers, but already sounded like hers.

Maybe that was real love. Or maybe just the beginning.


**THE END**
