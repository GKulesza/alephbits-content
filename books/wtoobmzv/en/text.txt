**THE LABEL**

He was twelve when he first heard that he was a layabout. He didn't remember the exact circumstances. He only remembered his father's voice, which said:

– You're as lazy as they come. If you don't pull yourself together, nothing will come of you.

Those words stuck in his memory like a tack. Not because they were particularly cruel. They were everyday. Spoken at breakfast, when he hadn't managed to take out the rubbish. At dinner, when he hadn't cleaned his room. On Sunday after church, when instead of studying for the test he was reading comics.

– You've always been a layabout – his father said. – Even when you were little, we always had to push you.

And Kamil believed it. He began to think of himself as a layabout. When he had something to do, he thought: "Why bother, since I'm lazy anyway?" When he got a bad grade, he thought: "Well, I'm a layabout, I don't know how to study." When he didn't get into the school of his dreams, he thought: "I guess I don't deserve it, I'm not good enough."

---
Twenty years passed. Kamil was now forty, with his own company, a wife and two children – ten-year-old Adam and eight-year-old Zuzia. On the surface he had succeeded. But inside he still felt like that twelve-year-old boy who had heard that he was a layabout.

When Adam got a worse grade, Kamil felt anger rising inside him. Not at Adam – at himself. Because he saw in his son the same thing his parents had once seen in him.

– Adam, aren't you doing your homework again? – he said, and the same note he remembered from his own childhood appeared in his voice. – You've always been a layabout.

Adam lowered his eyes. Kamil saw in them the same thing he had once seen in his own – resignation. And then something inside him broke.

---
That evening, when the children had gone to bed, Kamil sat down at the computer. He had no idea for work. Instead he opened a book he had bought a few weeks earlier but hadn't had time to read. "Dziki ojciec" (The Wild Father) by Szymon Grzelak. He turned the pages, looking for something that would help him understand why he felt the way he felt.

And then he came across the chapter about labels.

"Often, when we call a child a layabout, the child begins to see himself that way. It's a self-fulfilling prophecy. The longer it lasts, the harder it is for him to get out of it."

Kamil read that sentence several times. An image of Adam appeared in his head – Adam, who yesterday hadn't done his homework. And his own words: "You've always been a layabout."

– God – he whispered. – I'm doing the same thing my parents did.

---
For a few days Kamil watched himself. He noticed how often he said things that labelled Adam. "You're careless." "You forgot again." "You never finish anything."

He also noticed how Adam reacted. He was withdrawing more and more. Taking the initiative less and less often. Saying more and more often: "Well, I can't do it anyway."

Kamil knew he had to change something. But he didn't know how. He went back to the book. He found a passage that talked about how to use the labelling mechanism for positive purposes.

"Instead of talking about laziness, let's look for moments when the child took the initiative. Let's describe them, appreciate them. Let's give new labels – persistent, responsible, engaged."

Kamil decided to try.

---
The next day Adam came home from school with a test. This time he had got a C, which for him was a good result. Kamil took a deep breath.

– I can see you prepared – he said. – I remember how you sat over your books yesterday evening. It was hard, but you didn't give up. That's called persistence.

Adam looked at him, surprised.

– But I only got a C – he said.

– So what? – Kamil answered. – What matters is that you tried. And that you didn't give up. That's more important than the grade.

Adam smiled. For the first time in a long while.

---
A few days later Kamil asked Adam to help him take out the rubbish. Adam got up without complaining, took the bag and went. Kamil thought about how usually he would have said: "See how I have to ask you? You'd never think of it yourself." Instead he said:

– I see you got up before I even asked. You took the initiative yourself. That's very mature.

Adam looked at him as if he couldn't believe his ears.

– Really? – he asked.

– Really – Kamil answered. – I appreciate it.

---
A month passed. Kamil kept working on not labelling Adam negatively. He didn't always succeed. Sometimes he fell back into old habits, especially when he was tired or upset. But more and more often he managed to find the positive moments. More and more often he said: "You were responsible today", "You took the initiative", "You were persistent".

And Adam began to change. He began to believe that he was more than just "a layabout". He began to do things he had avoided before. He did his homework by himself, cleaned his room by himself, took the initiative himself.

One day Kamil asked him:

– Adam, how do you feel when you hear that you're persistent?

Adam thought for a moment.

– Good – he answered. – I feel like I can do something. That I don't have to be just what I think I am.

Kamil felt tears welling up in his eyes.

– You know what? – he said. – I'm learning too. And sometimes I fail. But I want to be a better dad.

Adam hugged him.

– You're a good dad – he said.

---
That evening Kamil opened Szymon Grzelak's book at the next chapter. This time about punishments and rewards. He read that punishments should be proportionate and predictable. That natural consequences are better than invented punishments. That praise should be descriptive and specific.

He remembered how he himself had been punished as a child. How he had been spanked for things he didn't understand. How he had heard "You're hopeless" instead of "You did something wrong, but I still love you."

And he understood that he didn't want to repeat those mistakes.

---
The next day Adam came home with the news that he had broken the school project. He was ashamed, afraid of his father's reaction.

– Dad, I broke the project – he said quietly.

Kamil felt anger rising inside him. He wanted to shout, to say that Adam always had to break something, that he was careless, that he never finished anything.

But he took a deep breath.

– What happened? – he asked calmly.

– I didn't read the instructions to the end. I did something wrong and now it doesn't work.

– That's a difficult situation – said Kamil. – What do you think you can do?

Adam thought for a moment.

– I could try to fix it. Or ask the teacher what I can do.

– That's a good idea – Kamil answered. – And what would you do differently next time?

– I'd read the instructions to the end. And maybe I'd ask if there's something I don't understand.

Kamil smiled.

– I can see you're learning. That's more important than a broken project.

Adam looked at him with gratitude.

– Thank you, Dad.

---
Another month passed. Kamil felt that something had changed in his relationship with Adam. His son was more open, more trusting, more willing to take the initiative. And Kamil knew it wasn't only thanks to his new approach. It was the result of the work they had both put into the change.

One day Adam said:

– Dad, do you remember when you said I was a layabout?

Kamil froze.

– I remember – he answered. – And I'm very sorry about that.

– You don't have to be sorry – said Adam. – Because back then I thought it was true. But now I know I'm not a layabout. Sometimes I just don't have the motivation. But I know I can change that.

Kamil hugged his son.

– Yes – he said. – You can. And so can I.

---
Kamil went back to Szymon Grzelak's book. He read the chapter about labels once more. And he understood that what had changed wasn't only Adam. He himself had changed. He had stopped seeing only the negative traits in his son. He saw his potential.

And that was the most important thing.

Once he had thought that upbringing was about punishment and reward. That you had to be strict so that a child would grow into a decent person. Now he knew that wasn't true. That the most important thing was for a child to feel loved, accepted and appreciated. That how we speak to a child matters enormously. And that the labels we give can become a self-fulfilling prophecy – either positive or negative.

Kamil decided that he would only give good labels. That he would look in his children for what was best in them. And that he would support them in that.

Because he knew that what Szymon Grzelak had said was true: "Instead of talking about laziness, let's look for moments when the child took the initiative. Let's appreciate them, give new labels. Persistent, responsible, engaged."

And Kamil knew that was exactly what he was doing. And that it worked.

**THE END**
