## Text

**THE PRICE OF THE VIEW**

**A short story**


When he came on for the night shift, the reception already smelled like an olfactory hell. Freshly cleaned, it smelled like a hospital corridor where someone had spilled perfume from a drugstore shelf. He breathed in and knew right away that this night would be long.

He was twenty-eight years old, had worked at this hotel for eight months, and knew perfectly well that his job was about pretending. Pretending that he had any influence over the situation. Pretending that the problems were temporary. Pretending that the guest in suite 512 was not sleeping in the sauna because the air conditioning had died three days ago and nobody had any idea when it would come back to life.

He rubbed his eyes, pulled a stack of printouts from the cupboard and spread them out in front of him. Reservations. Cancellations. Complaints. Calls from people who had seen the film on the Internet and were now calling with questions. Some wanted to know whether the air conditioning worked. Others — whether the water was yellow. And still others called just to laugh.

"Tell me," he heard in the receiver for the twelfth time that evening, "is it true that you charge 50 percent of the order value for food delivery?"

"Sir," he answered, clenching his teeth, "all charges are clearly stated in the rules available at the reception desk and on the website."

"I see, so it's true."

He hung up before the man could answer. He had no strength left for more explanations anyway.

He took a deep breath and walked over to the glass doors leading onto the terrace. Beyond them stretched the night bay, black as ink, crossed by the dancing lights of the streetlamps. Somewhere in the distance he could hear the murmur of the waves. The hotel stood on a cliff, and he came here every day from a town an hour away, to watch the sea through the glass and pretend that all of this made sense.

But tonight he was in no mood for pretending. Something in him had cracked. Maybe it was the film, which he had already seen twice — once on his break, and once at home, in the small apartment he rented from his aunt on the outskirts of the city. Or maybe it was the yellow water that actually did run from the taps in part of the suites, even though his superiors told him to repeat to the guests that it was the effect of "temporary treatment". Or maybe it was simply 2:37 in the morning, and he felt that his life was like that air conditioning — it seemed to work, but inside it was hot, suffocating, and nobody knew how to fix it.

He opened the doors to the terrace. The cold air hit his face and unfurled his lungs. He should have gone back inside, but he didn't. He stood and watched the sea. And then he heard it.

Footsteps. Quiet, rhythmic, coming closer from the direction of the beach. Someone was walking on the gravel, which echoed off the concrete wall of the hotel. He turned around. In the lamplight he made out a figure — a man in a dark coat, with a bag slung over his shoulder. He walked slowly, as if he did not want to wake anyone, and at the same time as if he knew exactly where he was going.

"Good evening," the man said, stopping a few metres away. He spoke Polish with a foreign accent, but he could not place it. "I'm looking for the reception."

"You're in the right place," he answered, surprised that anyone would come from the beach at this hour rather than through the main entrance. "But the reception is at the front. The side doors are locked for the night."

The man smiled. There was something about that smile that unsettled him — not so much friendly as... expectant.

"I don't need the front reception," he said. "I need someone who listens."

He looked around, as if checking that nobody was watching them.

"Have you seen the film?" he asked, stepping closer. "About your hotel?"

"Who hasn't," he answered, trying to sound indifferent. "The whole country has seen it."

"The whole country," the man repeated. "And what do you think? Do you think the hotel will collapse?"

He shrugged.

"I don't know. People talk. Some say they'll lose millions. Others say it's the best advertising they could have gotten."

"And you? What do you think?"

He hesitated.

"I think it doesn't matter. In a year nobody will remember. People will come here anyway, because the sea is here. Because the views are here. And the rest... the rest doesn't count."

The man nodded.

"Wise words. And naive," he added after a moment. "You see, I'm not a guest. I'm not looking for a room, I'm not looking for a place to stay. I'm looking for an answer to a question that nobody in this hotel wants to ask."

"What question?"

The man stepped even closer, almost touching his shoulder. In the lamplight he saw his face — he had deep wrinkles around his eyes, but his gaze was sharp as a razor.

"How much do you have to pay for someone to finally tell the truth."

He froze.

"What are you talking about?"

"I'm talking about the fact that this air conditioning didn't break three days ago. I'm talking about the fact that it never worked. I'm talking about the fact that someone in your management knew about it from the beginning and counted on hiding it until the end of the season. And now that it has all come out, they're trying to pretend it's the supplier's fault, or the weather's, or anyone else's."

He wanted to deny it, to say it wasn't true, that it was impossible, that it was absurd. But the words stuck in his throat. Because he knew the man was right. He had seen the internal emails circulating between the offices, seen the notes from meetings in which the technicians wrote in black and white that the system was not designed for such high temperatures. And he had seen how those notes ended up in the bin, because "someone upstairs" had decided that it was not a priority.

"How do you know that?" he asked at last.

The man smiled sadly.

"Because I was the one who designed it."

And then everything became clear. This man, this stranger in the dark coat, was the architect. Or the engineer. Someone who knew how this hotel had been built — and how much it had been cut down to save money.

"I came here because I wanted to see whether anyone would finally ask," the man went on. "Whether anyone would ask the question: why? Why did they pay for a system that doesn't work? Why did they save on the parts that were supposed to provide comfort? Why did they prefer to pretend that everything was fine instead of admitting the mistake?"

He fell silent. The wind rustled in the trees, the waves beat against the rocks below.

"And you," he said at last. "You're here, on the night shift. You look at this sea and think to yourself: 'What's the point of all this for me?' And you know what? You're right. The only question is what you're going to do about it."

He turned and headed for the beach, disappearing into the darkness as quickly as he had appeared. He was left alone on the terrace, with the cold wind in his face and a hundred thoughts whirling in his head.

For a moment he thought about going back inside and pretending nothing had happened. Returning to his post, answering the phone, replying to an email, logging a complaint. Doing what he had done every day for eight months.

But something wouldn't let him.

He went back to the reception, opened the drawer and took out a piece of paper. The pen trembled in his hand as he began to write. He didn't yet know what he would do with this letter. Maybe he would send it to the media. Maybe he would leave it on his supervisor's desk. Or maybe he would simply throw it in the bin, the way others before him had done.

He wrote.


_I don't know if anyone will read this. I don't know if it matters. But I'm sitting here, in a hotel that was supposed to be a symbol of Polish luxury, looking at the sea. And I keep thinking that all of this is so absurd it's almost funny._

_5,000 zlotys a night. That's what the suite costs where the air conditioning doesn't work. 315 zlotys for food delivery. That's what you have to pay for someone to bring you a plate from the kitchen._

_And me? I earn 18 zlotys an hour. The same as five years ago at the supermarket. And every week I come here to answer the calls of furious people who have every right to be furious. Because they paid for something they never got._

_I know it's not the fault of the reception staff. I know it's not the fault of the cleaners, the cooks, or the maintenance men. I know the problem is higher up. Much higher up._

_But I also know that this can't go on. That eventually someone has to say: "Enough. We're sorry. It's our fault. We'll fix it."_

_Only nobody has the courage._

_So I'll do it._


He finished writing. He folded the paper, put it in an envelope and wrote on it: "For the management".

Then he stood up, walked to the door and went out onto the terrace. The sky in the east was beginning to lighten. Dawn was coming. And he stood there, watching the sea, knowing that in a few hours everything would change.

Maybe he would lose his job. Maybe the whole affair would go further. Maybe nobody would pay attention to his letter.

Or maybe someone would read it after all. Someone with the power to change things. Someone who would ask the question everyone was afraid to answer.

He went back inside, picked up the phone and dialled a number.

"Good morning," he said when a familiar voice answered on the other end. "It's me. I need your help. I have something that has to be published."

There was silence on the line. Then he heard:

"What do you mean?"

"The true story of this hotel. From beginning to end. Who knew, who pretended, who profited and who lost. Everything."

A pause. Then:

"Are you sure?"

He looked at the envelope lying on the counter.

"I've never been surer."

Outside the window the sun was rising, and he knew that this night had changed everything. Maybe not for the whole world. But for him — certainly.


**Epilogue**

Six months later the hotel was closed for renovation. This time a real one. The air conditioning was replaced, the systems were redesigned, the standards were raised. Half of the management changed. Nobody remembered the affair anymore, at least officially.

And he sat on a train heading for the other end of Poland. In front of him was a ticket for a flight to Japan. The yen was the cheapest it had been in years, and he had always wanted to see Tokyo. The watches sold there were cheaper than in Europe. Apparently for four thousand zlotys you could fly there and back.

He smiled, looking out the window at the fields and forests passing by.

He didn't know what awaited him. Maybe a new job. Maybe a new life. Maybe just a week of the holiday he'd never had.

But he knew one thing — he would never go back to the hotel where he pretended that everything was fine.

And that was the most important thing.


**END**
