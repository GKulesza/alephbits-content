## Text

**IN FULL**

When Ola took her son in her arms for the first time, she felt two things at once. One – an enormous, overwhelming love. The other – fear. Because in that small, wrinkled creature she saw a human being who depended on her completely. And she thought to herself: "I have to shape him. I have to raise him. I have to teach him everything."

Five years passed. Franek, now a preschooler, sat on the floor in the living room and arranged blocks. Ola watched him from the kitchen, sipping cold coffee. She saw in him the same thing she had seen on the day of his birth – a human being. But now she knew something she had not known then.

That she did not have to shape him. That he was already fully himself.

---
It came from a podcast she had heard by chance while washing the dishes. Someone was talking about Jesper Juul – a Danish therapist who had put forward a provocative thesis: "What if we assumed that a child, from the very moment of birth, is a 100% complete human being?"

Ola stopped with the sponge in her hand. Something clicked inside her. She remembered all the situations in which she had treated Franek like a project to be fixed. Like something to be smoothed out, corrected, adjusted. As if he himself did not know who he was. As if his feelings did not matter.

And then she remembered the situation in the café that Juul had described. A parent asks the child what they want to drink. The child chooses. The waitress brings the juice, and the child suddenly changes their mind. The parent gets furious and says: "Shut your mouth, you little brat."

And Ola thought: "I did that too. Just with different words."

She remembered how Franek, when he was three years old, cried that he did not want to eat broccoli. And she said: "Stop whining, broccoli is healthy. If you don't eat it, there's no dessert." Back then she thought it was good parenting. That she was teaching him what is good and what is bad. That she was shaping his habits.

Now she saw it differently: "I made him distrust his own body, which was telling him he did not want to eat it. I taught him that my reality matters more than his."

---
But the hardest part was trust.

In Juul's book she found this sentence: "The child is competent." And that changed everything. Because if a child is competent, it means they know what they need. That they can make decisions. That you do not have to impose ready-made solutions, only accompany them in discovering the world.

Ola decided to test it. When Franek said he did not want to go to the pool because he was afraid of the water, Ola did not say: "Don't be afraid, there's nothing scary about it." Instead she asked: "What do you feel when you think about the pool?"

Franek looked at her in surprise. No one had ever asked him before what he felt.

– I'm afraid – he said. – That I'll sink.

– I understand – Ola answered. – It's normal to be afraid. Is there anything that could help you feel safer?

Franek thought for a moment.

– Maybe if I had armbands?

– We can try.

They went to the pool. Franek got into the water with armbands and after five minutes he said:

– Mom, I want to take the armbands off now. I want to try on my own.

Ola let him. And he swam. Not perfectly, but on his own. And when he came out of the pool, he said:

– Mom, I can do anything, if you let me.

Ola thought then: "It's not that he needs my methods. He needs my trust."

---
Four years later Ola already had two children – Franek and three-year-old Zosia. And she was still learning this philosophy anew every day.

One day Zosia picked up the scissors. Ola saw it and her first reflex was: "No, that's dangerous!" But she stopped. She remembered what Juul said: "A child needs a witness, not a corrector."

– Zosia – she said. – I see you want to use the scissors. This is a sharp object. Do you know how to use it safely?

Zosia looked at her seriously.

– Yes, mom. You have to hold it by the handle, not the blade.

– Good. And what are you going to cut out?

– A heart for daddy.

– That's lovely. Just remember to be careful with your fingers. If you need help, I'm here.

Zosia cut out the heart. Not perfect, but her own. And when she gave it to her dad, she said:

– Daddy, I made it myself. Mom trusted me.

That meant more to Ola than any parenting method.

---
The hardest days were the ones when she herself had no strength. When work, home, children – everything overwhelmed her. Then it was easiest to fall back into old habits: into shouting, into punishments, into "because I said so".

She remembered one evening when Franek did not want to go to sleep. She had had enough, she was tired, she only wanted a moment of silence. And she shouted: "You're going to sleep, end of discussion!" Franek looked at her with tears in his eyes and said:

– Mom, you no longer believe that I know what I need.

Ola froze. Those words hit her like a blow. She apologised. She sat down next to him on the bed.

– I'm sorry – she said. – I'm tired and it's not your fault. But you know what? You're right. I do believe you know what you need. I just forget sometimes.

Franek hugged her.

– It's okay, mom. I forget sometimes too.

---
Ola read about parenting mistakes, about punishments and rewards, about what destroys children. She saw her own past in those descriptions. She saw her mistakes, her clumsiness, her fatigue. And every time she felt that it was too much. That she would never be perfect. That she would always mess something up.

Until one day she came across a quote from Juul that changed everything:

"If today we again treated a child condescendingly, we don't have to beat ourselves up over it. It is simply a signal for us. Information that our own boundaries have been violated and we were unable to defend them in time. It is not a reason for guilt, but rather an invitation to reflect on ourselves."

And then Ola understood: it is not about being a perfect parent. It is about being a real one. About admitting mistakes, apologising, trying again. And trusting – yourself and the child.

---
Zosia was five years old when Ola heard about ADHD. Someone in her circle said that Zosia was "such a difficult child" – inattentive, chaotic, unable to focus on one thing for more than five minutes. Ola went to a specialist.

– Are we suspecting ADHD? – she asked.

– It's too early for a diagnosis – answered the psychologist. – But we can work on certain habits. An orderly space, routines, sleep hygiene, physical activity – all of this helps.

Ola came home and looked at Zosia. Her daughter, who was drawing on the living room wall because she forgot she had paper. Who climbed onto a chair to reach the sweets, and then forgot why she had climbed up. Who fell asleep at eleven in the evening and woke up at six, tired.

Ola thought about what she had read: "The circadian rhythm in children with ADHD is shifted by two hours."

She decided to make changes. Not as punishments, not as "methods". As help.

First of all – space. Zosia's corner was tidied up. Fewer toys in sight, more space. Fewer distracting stimuli.

Then – routine. Fixed meal times, fixed sleep times. She introduced a timer for brushing teeth: "Let's see who brushes their teeth first – you or me?"

Zosia loved races. And suddenly brushing teeth stopped being a fight and became a game.

And before bed – no screens. No blue light, which stimulates the prefrontal cortex and prevents sleep. Instead – reading books. And silence.

Zosia started sleeping better. And functioning better. Not perfectly – she was still chaotic, still forgot things. But she had tools. And she knew she could ask for help.

---
Ola's greatest challenge was planning time. Zosia, like many children with ADHD, had difficulties with executive functions. She forgot tasks, put them off until the last moment, got lost in the chaos of her own thoughts.

Ola introduced a calendar. Sticky notes on the fridge. Reminders on her phone. And a rule: "First things first".

– Zosia, what do you have to do today? – she asked in the morning.

– Brush my teeth, get dressed, pack my backpack.

– Good. What's the most important thing?

– Brushing my teeth.

– So what will we start with?

– With brushing my teeth.

And it worked. Not always, but more and more often. Zosia was learning to plan. Learning that she could have an influence on her life. That she did not have to be chaos.

---
One day Franek, who was already eleven years old, said to Ola:

– Mom, I think you're a good mom.

Ola was surprised.

– Why do you think so?

– Because you let us be ourselves.

Ola felt something tighten in her throat.

– And what does it mean to "be yourself"?

– It means that when I want to do something, you trust me. And even if I do it wrong, you help me instead of shouting. And you apologise when you do something wrong yourself.

Ola hugged her son.

– Thank you – she said.

And she thought to herself that this was the greatest gift she could give a child: not methods, not punishments, not rewards. But trust. And presence. And a love that does not condition, does not demand, does not judge.

A love that says: "You are fully human. From the very beginning. And you always will be."

---
At night, while the children slept, Ola opened Juul's book. She came across a sentence she had read before, but which sounded different every time:

"If we teach children that their dignity is inviolable from the first day of life, then they will not have to fight for it later in adulthood."

Ola closed the book. She looked at the door of the children's room. Through the gap the light of a night lamp was visible – small, warm, calm.

She thought about all the mistakes she had made. About all the moments when she could have done better. About all the times she had failed.

And she also thought about all the moments when she tried again. When she apologised. When she trusted.

It was not a perfect path. But it was hers. And it was real.

And maybe that was exactly the point – not to be a perfect parent, but to be a real one. To show a child that you can make mistakes and fix them. That you can be tired and ask for help. That you can not know, but keep searching.

And that in this whole journey the most important thing is one: to treat another human being – small or big – as your equal. As someone who has the right to their own feelings, their own needs, their own boundaries.

As someone who is fully human.

From the very beginning.

**THE END**
