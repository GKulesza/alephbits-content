## Text

**THE CASE**

Przemek always repeated that he was not a hero. That he simply asked a question. That anyone would do the same in his place.

Not anyone.

He was sitting now on a bench in the park, watching the pigeons fighting over a piece of bread roll, and trying to remember when he had last felt normal. Probably still before the police knocked on his door at six in the morning. Before they dragged him out onto the stairwell in handcuffs – fastened behind his back, like a major criminal, as if he were about to kill someone. Before his photo appeared on the official profile of the Greater Poland police with the caption "detained". Before the neighbours started looking at him differently.

– You're that one with the Ukrainians, right? – the shop assistant in the neighbourhood store asked him yesterday, and he didn't know whether it was admiration or accusation.

And yet he had only asked. Together with Darek, a colleague from work, they went to the Poznań University of Economics, because they had read that some Ukrainian company was renting an office there for a third of the market price. The university was supposed to teach, and in the meantime business was being conducted there. Someone should have checked that. Someone should have asked why a public university was giving away space for next to nothing. And why the same company boasted of "legalisation without refusals and without stress".

They went. They didn't shout. They didn't threaten. They introduced themselves by their full names. She – a young Ukrainian woman, maybe thirty, petite, with a phone in her hand – came out into the corridor and started recording them. She didn't ask them to leave. She didn't call security. They talked. They asked questions, she answered evasively. No recording showed them checking her ID, impersonating officers, doing anything illegal. But later the media wrote that that was exactly what had happened. That they had attacked her. That they had terrorised a poor Ukrainian woman who only wanted to work legally.

And then the police came.

Not a summons for questioning. Not a call from the station. At six in the morning, banging on the door, then handcuffs, then a police car. At the station they didn't even remove his handcuffs while he was giving his statement. He had to ask them to refasten them in front so that he could sign the record. The record of an interrogation in a case concerning an act under Article 212 of the penal code – a private prosecution offence that carries a maximum fine.

– This is madness – Krzysztof, the lawyer from the internet who had first publicised the case, told him afterwards. – They treated you worse than a bandit. For what? For asking a question?

– For the fact that the question was inconvenient – Przemek answered quietly.

He was sitting now on a bench and watching the light of the setting sun reflect in the windows of the dormitory across the street. He was thirty-four, an IT specialist, working remotely for a company from Wrocław. He lived in a village near Poznań, in a house after his grandparents that he was slowly renovating. He had never had any trouble with the law. He had never even received a fine. And now his face was on the official police profile, and people on the internet were calling him "bandit" and "fascist".

– You know what the worst thing about all this is? – he asked, even though there was no one on the bench. – That I would do it again.

---
Paweł Wyżżykowski knew that feeling. Since 2017 he had been living with it every day. A hundred denunciations, zero charges brought, but also zero peace. He once thought that the worst thing that could happen to him was online hate. Then he discovered that he was on a Ukrainian death list. Not some symbolic one, not a blacklist of jokers. On Myrotvorets – a list of people to be killed. Those who "harm Ukraine".

– For what? – his children asked him, when one evening they saw him crying over his laptop.

– For the truth – he answered.

He didn't tell them that a few days earlier a man from the same list had been murdered. That he lived seventy kilometres away from them. That his photo had then appeared on the Myrotvorets website with the caption "eliminated".

Now he was sitting in his house, in the room that served as his recording studio, looking into the camera lens. He was supposed to record another video, another analysis, another proof that the world had gone mad. But the words wouldn't come. Instead he thought about Przemek and Darek – two guys from Poznań who asked one question and were handcuffed for it. He thought about how his own name had ended up on the death list precisely because he had publicised the case of their interrogation. That the more truth he spoke, the more of a target he became.

Someone knocked on the door. His wife? No, his wife was with the children at his in-laws'. He stood up slowly, his heart beating faster. Through the peephole he saw a stranger – a shaved head, a leather jacket. He didn't look like a postman.

– Who is it? – he asked through the door.

– I come from Krzysiek – said the man. – From the video presentation. He sent me to check that everything was all right.

He opened the door. The man was huge, but he smiled gently.

– Krzysiek said you're on the list. That you need help. You've got four kids, man. You can't take such risks.

Paweł was silent for a moment. Then he invited him inside. They sat in the living room, drank tea and talked about what it was like to know that someone wants to kill you only because you tell the truth.

– Are you afraid? – the man asked.

– Every day. But I'm even more afraid of what will happen if I stop.

---
The next day Paweł recorded a video. He talked about the legalisation company, about the mechanism of flooding offices with applications, about the law in Poland having stopped working when politicians began using it to intimidate inconvenient citizens. He talked about Przemek and Darek. He talked about himself.

– Dear ladies and gentlemen – he finished, and his voice broke – I am not a hero. I am an ordinary man who has four children and wants them to live in a free country. But if I can do something, then so can you. Check those companies. Write to the offices. Publicise. Don't let yourselves be intimidated. Because if we give up, who will be left?

He turned off the camera. He sat for a moment in silence. Then he opened his laptop and checked the statistics – the video already had tens of thousands of views. In the comments people wrote: "We're with you", "Don't give in", "What can we do?".

Someone else wrote: "Take care of yourself".

Paweł looked at the photo of his children standing on the desk. Then at his hands – the same ones that had been writing the truth since 2017. They were trembling.

But he didn't stop writing.

---
Przemek answered the phone from Darek late in the evening.

– Did you hear? – asked Darek. – That guy from the death list, Wyżżykowski, recorded a video. About us. He said that what they did to us is a scandal on a European scale.

– I know. I saw it.

– And now what?

Przemek looked out of the window at the dark yard. On the barn behind the house, where he kept his tools, an old flag was waving – white and red, the one his grandfather had hung there. He remembered how his grandfather used to tell him about the times when you could go to prison for telling the truth. He thought it was just history. That it would never repeat itself.

– We're going to court – he said. – We won't give in.

– Alone?

– No. Did you see all those people in the comments? Those who write that they want to help? Those who are paying into the account? We're not alone, Darek. And we never were.

He put down the phone. On the desk lay a printout from the Greater Poland police profile – his photo, the caption "detained". He picked it up and for a moment looked at his own face. Then he crumpled the paper and threw it into the bin.

He didn't know what tomorrow would bring. He didn't know whether he would win in court. He didn't know whether he would ever stop being afraid.

But he knew one thing – he had asked a question. And he would ask it again. Because if not him, then who? If not now, then when?

And somewhere in Poznań, in a dark room, Paweł Wyżżykowski was looking at the death list on which his name appeared, thinking about the same thing.

**THE END**
