**THE SECOND WHEEL**

## I. THE BEGINNING

Magda woke up at 5:47, before the alarm went off. She lay in the darkness of her flat in Wrocław and heard through the window the sounds of the city waking to life. Somewhere in the distance someone was speaking Ukrainian. More and more often.

She got up, drank her coffee and sat down at the computer. On the screen was open a page of comments under a video of President Nawrocki. For the past three hours she had been reading them, trying to understand what other Poles feel. And what Ukrainians feel.

Her grandfather died in Volhynia in 1943. In her drawer she kept the letters he had written to her grandmother before his death. The last one ended with the words: _"My dears, if I survive, I will come to you. If not, remember that I love you more than my own life."_

He never came.

Magda closed her eyes. A childhood memory appeared in her mind – her grandmother, who wept when she talked about Volhynia. And the words she repeated like a mantra: _"Don't forget. You must not forget."_

She opened her eyes and looked at the screen. One of the comments read:

_"Poles are punished in their own country, while Ukrainians feel untouchable."_

She read it three times. Then she got up and walked to the window. Outside it was raining, and she felt something break inside her.

Not because she did not understand what other Poles feel. She understood. But because she knew that the truth was more complicated.

---
## II. THE DEVELOPMENT

A week later Magda sat in a café on Wrocław's market square. In front of her stood a coffee she did not drink, and the words from the comments still echoed in her head.

_"I don't trust any politician – not even the President!"_

_"Talking and doing are two very different things. Theatre for the sheep."_

_"Nawrocki is a false flag. Everything for the polls."_

She closed her eyes and thought about her grandfather. About the fact that he died because he was Polish. About the fact that the Ukrainians who killed him were neighbours with whom he had shared bread. About the fact that some of those Ukrainians had not wanted to kill either – they had been forced.

— Excuse me, is this seat free?

She looked up. In front of her stood a woman in her thirties – dark hair, tired eyes, in her hand a bag with writing in Ukrainian.

— Yes, of course — Magda answered.

The woman sat down, ordered tea and for a moment looked at her in silence.

— I'm sorry — she finally said in Polish, with a distinct accent. — I saw you looking at my bag. I'm from Ukraine. But I'm not a Banderite. I wanted you to know that.

Magda felt something tremble inside her.

— How do you know what I'm thinking?

— Because I see it in the eyes of Poles when they look at me. — The woman smiled sadly. — My name is Olena. I fled Donbas in 2022. My husband died in the war. I have two children who speak Polish better than I do. And I wanted you to know that not all Ukrainians are Banderites. Not all of them hate Poles.

Magda was silent. In her mind appeared the comment she had read yesterday:

_"I don't believe 95% of Ukrainians living in Poland, because that many come from the western region."_

— My family comes from Volhynia — she said at last. — My grandfather died in 1943. At the hands of Ukrainians.

Olena looked at her with pain.

— I'm sorry. Truly. I know what our ancestors did. I know it was terrible. But I am not them. My children are not them. And I wish Poles would understand that.

Magda looked at her for a long moment. Then she took her hand.

— I know — she said. — I know that not all Ukrainians are Banderites. But I'm afraid that history will repeat itself.

Olena squeezed her hand.

— I'm afraid too. But if we are afraid of each other, that is exactly what will repeat itself.

---
## III. THE TURNING POINT

A month later Magda returned to the same place. This time she did not come alone. She came with Olena and her children, who were playing in the playground next to the café.

They sat at the table, watching the children.

— My grandmother always said that one must not forget — Magda said. — But she never said that one must not forgive.

— That's hard — Olena answered. — My father also never spoke about the war. Only at the end of his life did he tell me about his brother, who died at the hands of the Banderites. He said that those who killed were not Ukrainians. They were Banderites. And that the Ukrainians who did not want to kill also died.

Magda looked at her.

— How is it possible that you, a Ukrainian woman, tell me this? That you don't hate Poles?

Olena smiled.

— Because I understood that hatred is a loop. The more you hate, the more trapped you are in it. And I want to be free. For myself. For my children. For Poland, which has become my second home.

Magda took a deep breath. In her mind appeared the memory of her grandmother saying: _"You must not forget."_ And suddenly she understood that her grandmother had not been talking about hatred. She had been talking about memory. And about the fact that memory does not have to be a burden.

— Maybe that's what reconciliation is about — she said quietly. — Not about forgetting. About understanding.

Olena nodded.

— Yes. About understanding that history is not only our grievances. It is also our responsibility.

---
## IV. THE ENDING

A year later Magda and Olena ran workshops for Polish and Ukrainian families in Wrocław. They talked about history, about trauma, about how one can live together without forgetting the past.

At one of the workshops an elderly man, a Pole from Volhynia, said:

— All my life I hated Ukrainians. But when I heard Olena's story, I understood that not all of them are the same. And that hatred will not bring my loved ones back.

Olena took his hand.

— I'm not asking you to forget, sir. I'm only asking you to see that we are different. And that some of us want to be friends.

The man was silent for a moment. Then he said:

— Maybe someday I'll be ready. Maybe not now. But I would like to try.

Magda looked at them and thought about her grandfather. About the fact that he died because he was Polish. About the fact that the Ukrainians who killed him were also victims – victims of an ideology that forced them to do things they did not want to do.

She stood up and walked to the window. Outside the sun was shining, and children were playing in the playground. Polish and Ukrainian. Together.

She smiled.

_"You must not forget"_ – she heard her grandmother's voice.

— I'm not forgetting, Grandma — she whispered. — But I'm learning to forgive.

---
## V. THE EPILOGUE

A few months later Magda received a letter from Olena. She wrote that she was returning to Ukraine to help rebuild her country. But that she would always be grateful to Poland for becoming her second home.

At the end of the letter there was a sentence:

_"Truth is like light. Even if someone tries to put it out, there will always be someone who lights another candle."_

Magda read the letter three times. Then she put it in the drawer, next to her grandfather's letters.

And she understood that memory is not only pain. It is also hope.

---
**THE END**
