## Text

**THE AEROPLANE**

## I. THE BEGINNING

Kamila woke up at 5:47, before the alarm went off. She lay in the darkness, listening to the third radio station in her head – the one that never went quiet, even at night. Plan for today: finish the presentation for the board. Deadline: 12:00. She had over six hours. That should be enough.

She got up, drank some coffee and sat down at the computer. She opened the file. For the first ten minutes she stared at the empty screen, hearing four different voices in her head at the same time: the one saying this was important; the one saying it was impossible; the one reminding her about the shopping; and the one humming a song from the radio that she couldn't stand.

– Pull yourself together – she told herself. – You have to start.

But her brain didn't work that way. It worked like an aeroplane that needs a runway to take off into the air. Without the right momentum – or without adrenaline – it wouldn't move.

She looked at the clock. 6:23.

She closed her eyes. A memory from her childhood appeared in her head – a teacher saying to her mother: _"Kamila is gifted, but lazy. If only she wanted to..."_

_"If only she wanted to"_ – she repeated to herself all her life.

She opened her eyes. 6:45.

She had to do something. She reached for her phone and opened the first game that came to hand. An hour later she was on level five, and the presentation was still empty.

– This isn't really happening – she whispered. – This isn't really happening.

---
## II. THE DEVELOPMENT

7:59. Kamila ran into the office with a cup of coffee she had spilled on the way. She had a stain on her white shirt, but she had no time to worry about it. The presentation was due in four hours, and she had only the title and three sentences of introduction.

– Did something happen? – asked a colleague from the next desk.

– Nothing – she answered, smiling artificially. – Everything's under control.

She sat down at the computer. For a moment she looked at the empty screen. And then, as if by magic, something happened.

Her brain switched into hyperfocus mode.

For the next three hours Kamila didn't exist. She felt no hunger, heard no sounds, saw no people. Only the presentation existed. Slides appeared one after another, perfectly designed, logical, convincing. Her fingers flew over the keyboard as if they knew what to do by themselves.

At 11:47 she finished. For a moment she looked at the screen, unable to believe she had done it. Thirty-two slides. Graphics, data, analyses. Everything ready.

– Wow – said her colleague, looking over her shoulder. – That's amazing. How did you do it in such a short time?

– I just... – Kamila fell silent. She didn't know what to say. She didn't know how to explain that for three hours she had been in a state that others reach maybe once a week, and she every day – but she pays double the price for it.

The rest of the day was different. The energy that had driven her through those three hours had vanished. She felt like a balloon with all the air let out. Every task – even answering an email – demanded ten times more effort than usual.

At 4:00 pm her manager asked her into a meeting.

– Great presentation – she said. – Really great. But I have one small comment for you.

Kamila felt something tighten in her chest.

– I don't quite understand where the data in slide twelve came from. Could you justify it better next time?

– Of course – answered Kamila, trying to make her voice sound normal. – Sure. I'll fix it.

She left the office and only in the bathroom allowed herself to breathe. She felt as if someone had poured boiling water over her. She knew it was only a comment. A small, constructive comment. But in her head she heard other words: _"You're hopeless. You've done something wrong again. Again you're not good enough."_

She remembered what her psychiatrist had once said: _"Rejection Sensitive Dysphoria. It's not your fault. Your brain reacts to criticism as if it were a threat to your life."_

But that didn't help. It hurt just the same.

---
## III. THE TURNING POINT

She got home at 8:00 pm. Her husband was waiting with dinner – pasta with tomato sauce, her favourite. She sat down at the table and for a moment looked at her plate, unable to reach for her fork.

– Did something happen? – he asked.

– Nothing. Just... – she stopped. In her head appeared the memory of today's meeting, when the manager talked about that small fix. And suddenly she understood something she hadn't understood for years.

– All my life I've heard that I'm gifted and lazy – she said quietly. – That if only I wanted to, I could be the best. And I tried. I really tried. But my brain doesn't work that way. It works like an aeroplane. It needs rocket fuel to take off, and then it flies so high that it sees nothing but the target. But when it lands... – she paused, pressed her lips together. – When it lands, there's no fuel left for anything else.

Her husband took her hand.

– And now what?

– I don't know. – She looked at him. – But I think I have to stop pretending I'm a car. And start living like an aeroplane.

---
## IV. THE ENDING

A year passed. Kamila stopped pretending she was neurotypical. She learned to communicate her needs, instead of her diagnosis. At a team meeting she said:

– Listen, if you have comments for me, please give them to me in the morning. In the afternoon my battery is at zero and I can't take them in properly.

And people heard her. Not because she told them about ADHD. But because she told them what she needed.

At work she started working in blocks – three hours of hyperfocus, then a break, then again. She learned that her brain doesn't work like a continuous stream, but like a series of bursts. And that that's all right.

She also started talking about it publicly. At workshops, at trainings, in conversations with other people who felt as lost as she did.

– You're not broken – she told them. – Your brain works differently. And that's your strength, not your weakness. As long as you learn to work with it, not fight it.

One day, after a workshop, a woman with tear-filled eyes came up to her.

– For thirty years I thought I was lazy – she said. – That I wasn't trying hard enough. And now I know... it wasn't my fault.

Kamila took her hand.

– I know. And you're not alone.

She left the room and looked at the sky. Somewhere there, in the distance, an aeroplane cut through the clouds. And for the first time in years Kamila didn't feel that it was a metaphor for her life.

She felt that it was a promise.

---
## V. EPILOGUE

A few months later Kamila received a message from the woman from the workshop:

_"I got my diagnosis. ADHD. I feel as if someone had lifted a weight from my shoulders that I'd been carrying since childhood. Thank you for being the one who told me I'm not broken."_

Kamila read the message three times. Then she put the phone in her pocket and went back to work. Ahead of her was another project, another deadline, another chance to show what she could do.

But this time she didn't feel pressure. This time she felt only determination.

Because she knew she didn't have to be a car. She could be an aeroplane. And what seems chaotic on the ground, in the air arranges itself into a perfect flight.

---
**THE END**
