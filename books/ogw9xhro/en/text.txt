**At the Café**

Morning in Warsaw smelled of coffee. Not the kind from the office machine, not the kind from the home espresso maker — only the kind from a real café, where the barista tamped the ground beans in the portafilter with a surgeon's gravity, checked the milk temperature with a fingertip and poured out the first, failed espresso because it "hadn't run like mouse tails".

Marek came to the Green Caffè Nero on the corner. Not because he liked the coffee there — though, yes, it was good. But because he needed a place. Not an office, not a home, not a stairwell. A place where, for an hour, he could pretend to be someone else. Someone without debts, without an ex-wife, without a child-support case and without exactly three zloty and seventy-eight groszy in his account.

He sat by the window and ordered a cappuccino with oat milk. The barista — a girl in a navy apron, with a tattoo on her forearm — smiled and asked:

– Large or small?

– Large – he said. – Please.

– For here or to go?

– For here.

He paid. The last fifteen zloty he had in his wallet. He'd spent the rest on a bus ticket. But he didn't regret it. Because this wasn't just coffee. It was a ticket to a world where, for a while longer, he could be someone who has time.

---

He sat and watched people. At the next table two girls in suits were talking about a presentation. One of them — a blonde with glasses — was typing something on a laptop, sipping a latte. The other — with short-cropped hair — laughed loudly, telling a story about a boss who "can't tell the difference between a KPI and an OKR". Marek smiled to himself. Two years ago he'd talked like that too. Now he only talked about how much he had left until the end of the month.

At a table at the back sat an older man in a woollen jumper. He was reading the "Gazeta Wyborcza" and every few minutes raised his eyes to look at the door. He was waiting for someone. Maybe his wife. Maybe a grandson. Maybe someone who would never come. Marek knew what it was like to wait for someone who doesn't come. He knew, because he himself had been waiting for his son for three months. Since the conversation in which he'd told him he wouldn't be living with his mom anymore.

– Dad, but you'll come back? – Kuba had asked then.

Marek hadn't answered. Because he didn't know.

---

The waitress — the same one with the tattoo — came over to his table with a smile.

– Sorry, can I get you anything else?

Marek looked at the empty cup. He wanted to say "yes", but he knew he had nothing to pay with.

– No, thank you – he said. – Just the bill, please.

– You already paid when you ordered – she smiled. – That's everything.

She looked at him carefully. As if she could see something others couldn't. Maybe the tiredness. Maybe the emptiness in his eyes. Maybe that his face showed all the conversations he'd never had, all the words he'd never said.

– Is everything all right? – she asked quietly.

Marek wanted to say "yes". He always said "yes". But this time something cracked.

– No – he said. – Not everything.

---

She sat down across from him. For a moment, just a moment. She was on her break.

– I have days like that too, sometimes – she said. – But then someone comes in who orders a plain espresso, no sugar, and drinks it standing at the counter, like an Italian. And then I think: well, there are still people who know what they want.

Marek laughed bitterly.

– And I don't. I don't know what I want.

– Then maybe – she said, getting up – start small. With one thing. With one coffee. Even if it's just the one you've already drunk.

She walked off, leaving a business card on his table. Not from her café. From some foundation. On the back was written: "We help people start over."

Marek put the card in his pocket. He stood up, straightened his jacket. As he was leaving, he glanced once more at the old man with the newspaper. He was still waiting.

---

Outside it was raining. Marek stood under the awning and lit a cigarette — the last one, because he had no money left for another pack. Through the café window he could see the barista with the tattoo, who was just serving someone another coffee. She was smiling. As if she had no problems at all.

But Marek knew that everyone has problems. It's just that some people have learned to hide them in their pocket, along with the rest of their small change.

He took out his phone. He found a number he'd saved long ago but never called. For a moment he held his finger over the "call" button. Then he put the phone back.

Not now. Maybe later.

He crossed the street. Behind him, in the café, someone ordered a double espresso with oat milk. Someone else asked about the cheesecake. Someone took a coffee to go and ran out into the rain.

Life went on. Even if he was standing still.

---

A week passed. Then two. Marek went back to the same café. This time he had no money for coffee, but he sat down at a table — the same one, by the window — and watched. The barista with the tattoo saw him and came over.

– Good morning – she said. – How can I help?

– Nothing today – he answered. – I'll just sit.

She nodded. After a moment she came back with a cup.

– On the house – she smiled. – Same as last time.

Marek looked at her. He wanted to thank her, but the words stuck in his throat.

– Thank you – he managed at last.

– No problem – she said. – Everyone's entitled to one free coffee. Especially those who are starting over.

She smiled and walked off.

Marek took the cup in his hands. It was warm. He felt the warmth spread through his fingers, then through his palms, then through his whole body. He took a sip. It tasted exactly like it had then — when he'd first walked in, thinking it was just coffee. And it turned out to be something more. A ticket to a world where you can start over.

He reached into his pocket. He took out the card he'd been given a week ago. He turned it over. On the back, written in pen, was a phone number. And next to it — a word: "Call".

Marek looked at the barista, who was just serving someone coffee at the counter. She looked up, as if she felt his eyes on her. She smiled. He nodded. He took out his phone and dialled the number.

– Hello? – he heard in the receiver.

– Good morning – he said. – I have a question about that... foundation. About how to start over.

The line was silent for a moment. Then he heard:

– It's good that you're calling. We happen to have a place.

---

He left the café an hour later. The sky was clear, the sun breaking through the clouds. He stopped for a moment in front of the entrance and looked up — at the Green Caffè Nero sign. On the door he saw a notice: "Open daily 7:00–22:00". Below it, in small letters: "For those who are starting over".

He smiled. Maybe it wasn't a real notice. Maybe he was only imagining it. But at that moment it didn't matter.

He went back inside. The barista with the tattoo looked at him in surprise.

– Did you forget something? – she asked.

– No – he answered. – But I wanted to ask. Can you always come here and just sit?

She smiled.

– Yes. Always. Even without ordering.

– Thank you – he said. And he sat down by the window.

Because he knew this wasn't just a café. It was a place where you can start over. Even if only for one coffee.
