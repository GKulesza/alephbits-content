## Text

**THE BREAK — THE RHYTHM OF LEARNING**

## I. THE BEGINNING

Zosia woke up at 5:47, even though her alarm was set for 6:15. She lay in the darkness for a moment, listening to the beating of her own heart and the hum of the radiator. Outside the window, Warsaw was just waking to life – the first buses, the distant barking of a dog, raindrops striking the glass.

She knew that today she had to study. The pharmacology exam was in three weeks, and she had only read the first chapter so far. Thousands of pages, hundreds of drugs, mechanisms of action, interactions, dosages. Her brain felt like a sponge soaked with water – every new drop made something else flow out.

She got up, poured herself some coffee and sat down in front of the computer. She opened the textbook. For the first five minutes she read the same sentence three times without understanding it. Then her hand reached for her phone on its own.

_"Just check the messages"_ – she told herself. _"Only for a moment."_

Forty minutes later she was watching a reel about how to make the perfect fried egg, and she couldn't remember why she had picked up the phone at all.

– One more time – she said to herself, putting the phone down at the other end of the room. – I'll try one more time.

She sat up straight, took a deep breath and began to read. This time she managed to make it to the end of the page. Then the next one. And then, just as she felt she was getting into the rhythm, the kettle went off in the kitchen – an automatic one, set for 7:30, because she had forgotten to switch it off.

She went to turn it off. On the way she noticed that someone had left an unwashed cup on the windowsill. She took it to the sink. There were two more plates in the sink. She washed them. Then she remembered that she should check whether she had enough eggs for breakfast.

She returned to her desk an hour later. The textbook lay open on the same page. Zosia looked at it and felt like crying.

---
## II. THE DEVELOPMENT

That evening, once she had made peace with the thought that she wouldn't get anything done today, she turned on the radio. The programme "Day by Day". Someone was talking about the full moon, about cycles, about how sometimes you have to let go in order to be able to start again.

_"I have a feeling"_ – said the host, Krzysztof – _"that we will soon hear about an amazing breakthrough in the treatment of cancer. But that's not the point. The point is that certain things must die so that others can be born. As in nature, as in our lives."_

Zosia rolled her eyes. More nonsense about fate. But something in this man's voice made her keep the radio on.

_"Getting down to things doesn't look like 'I'm getting down to it'_" – Krzysztof continued. _"Getting down to things looks like this: well, it has to be done. There is no super energy there. It's like clarified butter flowing off the pan. It is something like that, not some enthusiasm."_

Zosia froze. That was exactly what she felt. All her life she had thought that before starting anything she had to feel a _spark_, _motivation_, _enthusiasm_. And yet he was saying that it didn't work that way. That motivation comes _after_, not _before_.

She got up and walked over to the window. Outside, the moon was almost full – bright, round, like an eye looking straight at her. She remembered what a classmate who was interested in astrology had once told her: _"During the full moon it is best to start new things. The energy is greatest. But it is also easiest to get distracted."_

Zosia smiled bitterly. _Easiest to get distracted_ – that was her middle name.

The next day she decided to do something different. Instead of trying to study at home – where something to do lurked on every shelf, something to watch on every screen – she went to the medical library.

The hall was huge, filled with the whisper of turning pages and the quiet clatter of keyboards. Dozens of other students sat around her, each immersed in their own world. No one cared what she was doing. No one expected tea from her, no one left unwashed cups.

She sat down by the window, took out her textbook, a pen and some paper. She set a timer for 25 minutes – the Pomodoro method she had read about on the internet. She decided that for these 25 minutes she would only read and take notes. And then she would take a 5-minute break, but a break _without her phone_.

The first block was hard. After ten minutes her thoughts began to drift: _"Maybe I should check whether anyone has replied to my message? Maybe make myself some tea? Maybe clean out my bag?"_ But she persevered. She looked at the words, forced herself to read them, to write them down in her own words.

When the timer rang, she couldn't believe that 25 minutes had passed. She felt like after running her first kilometre – tired, but strangely satisfied.

During the break she stood up, walked around the library, looked at people. She didn't pick up her phone. When she sat back down, her head felt lighter.

The second block went more easily. The third even more so. After four blocks (1 hour 40 minutes of study in total) she felt that her brain was starting to sweat. She took a longer break – half an hour. She went outside, walked through the park, breathed in the fresh air.

When she came back, she had the feeling that something inside her had changed. Not because she had suddenly become a genius. But because she had stopped fighting.

---
## III. THE TURNING POINT

A week later Zosia walked into the same library with the feeling that it was already her second home. She found her favourite spot – by the window, with a view of the old trees, away from the noisy groups. She had learned that her best time for studying was the morning, right after breakfast, before the world started sending out its signals.

But it wasn't always easy. There were days when she sat down at her desk and felt that nothing was coming. Then she remembered Krzysztof's words from the programme: _"When I really don't feel like it, I should go away and sleep it off."_

Sometimes she really did go to bed. Or she went for a walk. Or she simply sat and looked out of the window, letting her thoughts flow. And every time she came back, she felt that something had stirred.

On Friday evening, as she was getting ready for another session, a notification from YouTube appeared on her phone: _"YUI – again (Full MV)"_.

She stopped. YUI – the Japanese singer she had listened to in middle school. The song "again" – the opening of "Fullmetal Alchemist: Brotherhood". She remembered how, back then, as a teenager, she would stare at the screen, feeling that this music was saying something she couldn't put into words.

She clicked.

The first chords of the guitar hit her like a wave. And then YUI's voice – clear, strong, full of emotion:

_"Yume no tsudzuki o otteita natsu no hi..."_

_(I was chasing the continuation of a dream on a summer day...)_

Zosia closed her eyes. She remembered the times when she believed everything was possible. When she dreamed of becoming a doctor, of saving people, of changing the world. And how much harder that path had turned out to be than she had thought.

She opened her eyes and looked at the textbook. On its cover was the name of the subject that gave her the most trouble. And then she understood.

It wasn't that she couldn't study. It wasn't that she was lazy. It was that all along she had been trying to _control_ the process instead of _trusting_ it. She had been trying to force her brain to work in a way that didn't serve it. She needed silence. She needed rhythm. She needed breaks that were not an escape but regeneration.

She played the song from the beginning and began to read. This time she didn't fight. She let the words flow through her, wrote down the most important things, repeated them aloud when something was unclear. When she felt her attention waning, she stood up, stretched, looked out of the window. And came back.

Over the next three hours she read more than in the entire past week.

---
## IV. THE ENDING

The pharmacology exam took place on Monday at 9:00. Zosia had slept well the night before – for the first time in months. She woke up calm, drank her coffee, ate breakfast. She didn't open the textbook even once. She knew that at that moment she wouldn't absorb anything new.

In the exam hall she sat down at a desk, took a pen in her hand and looked at the first question. For a moment she thought about all those days when she couldn't get started. About all the breaks that had been an escape. About all the nights when she stared at the ceiling, thinking she wouldn't make it.

And then she began to write.

The answers flowed out of her almost by themselves. Not all of them were perfect – she felt that some details had escaped her attention. But she knew she had done everything she could. That there was no "if only". There was only "I did it".

When she handed in her work and left the hall, the sun was shining outside. She took a deep breath, caught the smell of spring and smiled.

In her pocket she had her earphones. She put on a YUI song – this time "Rolling Star", another song that had accompanied her through hard times.

_"Mou ichido, mou ichido..."_

_(One more time, one more time...)_

She remembered Krzysztof's words from the programme she had heard weeks earlier: _"Sometimes you have to let go in order to be able to start again."_

And she understood that it wasn't just advice about studying. It was advice about life.

She went home, taking her time. In the kitchen she found an unwashed cup – the same one that had been standing there for weeks. She took it, washed it, put it on the drying rack. And then she felt that something inside her had closed.

She didn't have to be perfect. She didn't have to control everything. She only had to do what she could, at her own pace, listening to herself.

She sat down in front of the computer, opened her notes file and added one sentence at the end:

_"The best break is the one that lets you come back."_

She closed her laptop, lay down on the bed and fell asleep.

This time without any guilt.

---
## V. EPILOGUE

Three weeks later Zosia received her exam results. She opened them with a mixture of fear and curiosity.

**Passed. Grade: 4.5.**

She read it twice. Then three times. And then she burst into laughter that was half laughter and half tears.

She called her mother.

– I passed.

– Darling, that's wonderful! I'm so proud of you.

– Mom... I thought I wouldn't make it. I really thought it was the end.

– But you did make it. And you always will, remember. Even if sometimes it seems to you that you won't.

Zosia put down the phone and looked out of the window. Outside the sun was shining, and in the distance children were playing on the playground. Somewhere on the radio a song was playing that she didn't know, but which had something familiar about it – a rhythm that reminded her that everything has its time.

She opened her laptop and typed into the search engine: _"YUI again lyrics meaning"_.

She read the translation. The last verse read:

_"Kono yo de ichiban taisetsu na koto wa / Tamerau koto naku susumu koto"_

_(The most important thing in this world is / to move forward without hesitation.)_

She smiled. She closed her laptop, took her bag and left the house. She had a whole day ahead of her, a whole summer, a whole life.

And she knew that if she ever again felt she couldn't get started, she would know what to do:

She would take a break.

And then she would come back.

---
**THE END**
