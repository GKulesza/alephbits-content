## Text

**CURIOUS ZOSIA AND THE TREASURE OF FRIENDSHIP**

In a small town, where houses stood tightly side by side and the streets were narrow and winding, there lived a little girl named Zosia. She had fair hair, blue eyes, and she was very, very curious.

Curious – that's not saying enough. Zosia had to know everything. What is hidden in the closed wardrobe? What lies at the bottom of the shopping basket? What did the maid bring from the market? What does Mama hide on the top shelf of the cupboard? Nothing could hide from her.

– Zosia, don't peek into parcels that aren't for you – said Mama.  
– Zosia, don't touch things that aren't yours – repeated Grandma.  
– Zosia, don't poke your nose into other people's business – sighed the maid.

But Zosia peeked, touched and poked anyway. And it often ended badly.

Once, when the maid had brought a basket from town and had gone to fetch firewood, Zosia couldn't hold back. She went up to the basket, carefully lifted the lid... and suddenly something big, snapping its claws, grabbed her finger! It was a crayfish that had escaped from the market and hidden in the basket. Zosia screamed, cried, ran all over the kitchen, and the crayfish clung to her finger as if bewitched. Luckily the maid came back and freed the girl. But Zosia had enough fright to last a year.

Another time she found a jar of tar in the wardrobe, which Mama had hidden from the children. Curious Zosia reached for it, put her finger in, tasted it... and for a week she couldn't get rid of the black, sticky mark on her tongue and hands.

Mama was worried. How to teach her little daughter that curiosity can be dangerous?

---
One summer Mama decided to take Zosia to Grandma's in the countryside. It was a great honour – Zosia went there rarely, because Mama was afraid that her curious daughter would cause trouble for Grandma. This time, however, Zosia promised: I'll be good! I won't touch anything! I'll listen!

And indeed – the first days in the country passed calmly. Zosia ran through the meadow, swung on the swing, fed the swans on the pond and marvelled at how nature changes. She was so delighted with the countryside that she almost forgot about her curiosity.

Grandma, seeing that her granddaughter was good and obedient, decided to reward her. She led her to a big, old cupboard.

– You see, Zosia, all these toys? – she asked, opening the doors.

Zosia jumped for joy. Inside stood: a beautiful doll in a silk dress, a jumping jack in a colourful outfit, a doll's pram and a big, colourful ball.

– They are for you, my child – said Grandma. – You will receive them when I am convinced that your curiosity has passed. Don't touch anything that doesn't belong to you, and always be obedient. Then all the toys will be yours.

Zosia promised. For a few days she kept her word. She played in the garden, helped in the kitchen, read books. But the toys in the cupboard called to her as if under a spell.

One day Grandma went to visit a neighbour. Zosia was left alone in the house. She sat down in the armchair in Grandma's room and looked at the cupboard.

Maybe I'll get those toys tomorrow – she thought. Or maybe today, when Grandma comes back? Oh, why doesn't Grandma come back already?

She got up, went to the cupboard, then to the window, then back to the cupboard. Curiosity fought with the promise. In the end curiosity won.

– I'll only look – she whispered to herself. – I'll only see if the doll has real hair. I won't touch anything. Grandma won't find out.

She brought a stool, climbed on it, opened the doors a crack and...

And then a white mouse jumped out of the cupboard. It darted across Zosia's hand, jumped to the floor and raced into the depths of the room.

Zosia froze. Then she jumped off the stool, tried to catch the mouse, but it vanished somewhere behind the chest of drawers. The girl looked for it until evening. When Grandma came back, Zosia was afraid to admit what she had done. She went to bed with a heavy heart.

The next morning she woke up early. She knew she had failed. Instead of enjoying the countryside, she waited for punishment. And then she heard Grandma talking to Mama on the phone. Zosia didn't know what they were saying, but she was afraid it was about her.

When she returned to her room, a note from Grandma was waiting on the table:

_"My dear Zosia. I know what happened. I know about the mouse. And I know that you're ashamed to admit it. But don't worry – doing something wrong doesn't mean you're a bad girl. What matters is that you learn from it. And the lesson is this: curiosity is a good thing, but only when it doesn't hurt others and doesn't break promises. The toys will wait. And you wait, until you learn to master your curiosity."_

Zosia read the letter several times. Then she went to Grandma, hugged her and said:

– I'm sorry, Grandma. I'll never do that again. And I don't want those toys, until I truly deserve them.

Grandma smiled and hugged her granddaughter.

– That's good, sweetheart. Because I can see you're beginning to understand.

---
A few days passed. Zosia stopped thinking about the toys. Instead, she began to take an interest in flowers. Every day she went out to the meadow and gathered different plants – colourful petals, interesting leaves, fragrant herbs. She learned their names from Grandma, and then dried them between the pages of books.

One day Grandma led her to her old cupboard, but not to give her toys. She took out of it old, yellowed books about herbs and plants.

– I think this will be useful to you, Zosia – she said. – You have a good eye for nature. And curiosity, when it is well directed, can bear beautiful fruit.

Zosia looked at the books, then at Grandma, and then at her own collection of pressed plants.

– Grandma – she said – I think that instead of toys, I would like to take up drying flowers. And maybe make presents from them for others.

– That's a wonderful idea – answered Grandma.

And so Zosia discovered her passion. She dried flowers, made pictures from them, bookmarks, ornaments. She gave them to Mama, Grandma, the neighbours. She was happy that she could bring joy to others.

And curiosity? It stayed. But it was no longer dangerous. It became a curiosity about the world, which helped her discover new things and share them with her loved ones.

And although she never got that doll or that jumping jack, she knew she had gained something far more precious – the ability to look at the world with open eyes, but with hands kept closed to other people's secrets. And that was the greatest treasure she could ever find.

**THE END**
