**WE'VE GOT A CORPSE, NOW WHAT?**

When Tomasz Kowalski answered the phone from the hospital, he knew it had happened. His mother, whom he had spoken to only yesterday, who had laughed that her legs had finally stopped swelling, that maybe next week she could come home – she was gone. Quickly, peacefully, in her sleep.

He sat in his apartment in Warsaw, holding the receiver in his hand, and felt the world around him slow down. For a moment he didn't know what to do. Then he remembered that he had to call his sister. That he had to arrange something. That he had to... but he didn't know what.

– For the first time in my life – he said to himself – I have no idea what to do.

**The funeral home**

The next day Tomasz stood at the entrance to a funeral home in the centre of Warsaw. The sign was elegant, gold letters on a black background. "Kamienica żałoby" – he read. It sounded serious. Dignified. He believed it was a good place.

Inside, he was greeted by a man in a dark suit, with a tie tied so perfectly that Tomasz felt, next to him, like someone who couldn't get his own life together.

– Welcome, welcome – said the man, offering his hand. – My sincere condolences for your loss. Please, have a seat.

Tomasz sat down. A conversation began that was supposed to last an hour, and that changed everything.

– What are your preferences? – asked the man. – Oak or pine coffin? Lacquered or natural? Wreaths, flowers, musical accompaniment... we can arrange everything.

Tomasz listened, trying to understand what was important and what wasn't. The man spoke quickly, confidently, as if he knew the answers to all the questions. And then he suggested something that seemed necessary to Tomasz:

– I recommend embalming. Thanks to it, the body keeps a fresh appearance for a longer time. Your mother will look natural, peaceful. It costs about 1500 złoty.

Tomasz nodded. 1500 złoty? That was a lot, but all in all... he wanted his mother to look good. He wanted the people who came to the funeral to remember her as she was. He agreed.

Then came the coffin. The man showed him several models. The cheapest cost 800 złoty, but it looked cheap. The most expensive – 4500 złoty – was mahogany, with a silk lining. Tomasz chose the middle one. 2800 złoty. He placed in it his mother's favourite dress and her silver bracelet, which she always wore.

– And flowers? – asked the man. – We have beautiful wreaths, from 300 to 1200 złoty. And music – an organist, a violinist, we can arrange everything.

Tomasz agreed to everything. Together with his sister they split the costs. It came to about 12 000 złoty. He thought it was normal. He thought that was how it had to be.

**A few weeks later**

Tomasz was sitting in a café with his friend Magda. He was telling her about the funeral, about how hard it had been to arrange everything, about the costs.

– You spent 12 000 złoty? – asked Magda in disbelief.

– Yes. Why?

– Tomek, I've heard of something called a green burial. My aunt did that. It cost her about 3000 złoty. And the body wasn't embalmed, and the coffin was made of unpainted wood.

Tomasz looked at her, surprised.

– But I had to do everything right. Mum deserved a dignified farewell.

– And she deserved it. But does that mean you had to spend that much money? Did anyone tell you that you could choose cheaper options? That you don't have to embalm?

Tomasz fell silent. Nobody had told him that. Nobody had told him he could choose a cheaper coffin, that he could skip the embalming, that he could arrange the funeral differently.

Nobody had told him that he didn't have to be ripped off.

**The meeting**

A few months later, Tomasz found himself at a meeting of the Funerarium foundation. It was organised by Agnes Tołoczmańska – a woman who had devoted her life to education about death. Tomasz came out of curiosity, but he left with regret.

Agnes talked about how funeral homes often exploit families' ignorance. About how embalming is not necessary in most cases. About how you can arrange a green burial, in which the body is wrapped in linen and buried in the ground without a coffin. About how you can choose a biodegradable urn and pour the ashes into the soil in a memorial park.

– The biggest mistake – said Agnes – is not fighting for your rights. A funeral is a service. And a service can be performed badly. If it's performed badly, you have the right to demand your money back. Nobody will tell you that in a funeral home, but it's true.

Tomasz listened and felt the regret growing inside him. If he had known this earlier, he could have arranged his mother's funeral differently. He could have spent less money. He could have chosen something more in keeping with her character – she had always said she didn't want to be a burden, that she didn't want them to spend a fortune on her.

And he had spent it. Because nobody had told him he could do it differently.

**The change**

Tomasz began reading books about death, about grief, about funerals. He read "Mamy trupa i co dalej" – a book written by Agnes together with Małgorzata Węglarz. He learned about what green burials look like, about what you can put in a coffin and what you can't, about the symbolism on gravestones, about how children should be taken to funerals rather than protected from death.

He started talking about it with his sister. With his friends. With his own children. He decided he wouldn't let his children go through what he had gone through. That they would know that death is a natural process, and a funeral is a service that can be negotiated.

And one day, when his son asked him what would happen when his grandfather died, Tomasz sat down with him and told him about death. About the fact that it's normal. About the fact that you can say goodbye. About the fact that there's no need to be afraid.

The son listened seriously. Then he asked:

– Will grandpa go to heaven?

Tomasz smiled.

– I don't know. But I hope he'll find peace there.

**Reflection**

Tomasz often thought about that day when he arranged his mother's funeral. About how lost he had been, how he had let himself be led, how he had spent money he didn't have to spend. But he also thought about what he had learned. About the fact that death is part of life. That you can prepare for it. That you can talk about it.

And that the most important thing is not to be afraid to ask.

Because when you don't know what to do – ask. When someone offers you something that seems expensive – ask whether there's an alternative. When you don't understand the process – ask how it works.

And remember that you have the right to choose.

Because as Agnes said at the meeting:

– You can do more than you think.

And Tomasz finally understood that.

**THE END**
