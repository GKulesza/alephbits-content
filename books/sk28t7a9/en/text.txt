**THE LITTLE HOME**

## I. The room that waited

When Marta came home from work, the apartment was dark. Not because the light had gone out – simply because the windows were covered by blinds she couldn't remember last raising. The living room was bathed in half-light, where the outlines of furniture merged into one another, forming a single grey, shapeless mass.

She took off her shoes in the hallway but didn't move them aside. They stood where they had fallen, as every day. Three coats hung on the rack, though there was only one of her. On the floor lay a shopping bag from last week that had never been unpacked.

Marta was thirty-five, had a job that exhausted her and an apartment that overwhelmed her. She couldn't remember the last time she had felt good in it. Maybe never.

She sat down on the sofa, the only free spot in the room. Around her things piled up. Books she had meant to read but never started. Clothes she had bought because she thought they would suit her but never wore. Boxes she hadn't opened since the move, even though three years had passed.

– I'll do it tomorrow – she said to herself. – Tomorrow I'll clean up.

But she knew it wasn't true. Tomorrow would be just like today. And the day after too.

She picked up her phone. She hesitated for a moment, then typed into the search bar: _"how to overcome clutter"_.

There were thousands of results. Guides, videos, articles. But one title caught her eye: _"Clutter is the physical representation of your emotional blocks"_.

She clicked. It was a podcast. The voice was calm, warm, and the words hit her like waves:

_"There is no bad or good clutter. It wants to tell you something."_

Marta put down her phone and looked around the room. And then she saw something she hadn't seen before. Every thing lying around her was part of something bigger. Part of her. Each had a story that she herself had put into it. Each held her in the place where she was, instead of letting her move on.

And then she knew she had to start.

---

## II. The first wardrobe

Marta chose the wardrobe in the bedroom. It seemed like the easiest target – there were no personal things in it, only clothes she rarely wore anyway. She opened the doors and looked inside for a moment. It was full. Right to the brim.

She started taking things out. One thing after another. Dresses she hadn't worn in years. Trousers that were too small, but she kept them because "maybe she'd lose weight someday". Shirts she had bought on sale, though she knew she wouldn't like them.

When the pile on the floor grew to half her height, she felt tired. But not physically. Something else. Deeper. As if every thing she took out was carrying away a piece of her energy.

She picked up a red dress. She remembered when she had bought it. She was twenty-five then, she was in love and thought her whole life would be the same. The dress was meant for her first trip with Marek. But the trip never happened. They split up a week before it. The dress stayed. For ten years.

– Why do I keep it? – she asked aloud.

The answer came quickly. Because if she threw it away, it would mean that time really was over. That Marek would never come back. That she – the Marta of ten years ago – no longer existed.

She looked at the dress closely. It was pretty, but no longer her style. Someone else's memories, not hers.

– Thank you – she said quietly. – You were important. But now move on.

She put it into a bag labelled "to give away".

And she felt something inside her loosen. As if the stone she carried on her chest had suddenly grown smaller.

---

## III. The box from her parents

In the corner of the room stood a box she hadn't opened since the move. It was large, cardboard, sealed with tape. Someone had written on top: "Mum's things".

Marta sat down next to it and looked at it for a long time. Her mother had died five years earlier. Cancer, fast, too fast. Marta hadn't had time to say goodbye. Hadn't had time to say everything she wanted. Only this box was left.

She peeled off the tape. Inside were photographs, letters, jewellery, a few books. And a small, worn notebook in which her mother had written down recipes.

Marta took it in her hands and opened it to the first page. Her mother's handwriting was slanted, a little clumsy. The first recipe: _"Tomato soup – 1 kg of tomatoes, 2 onions, a spoonful of butter..."_

She smiled through tears. Her mother made the best tomato soup in the world.

She went through the rest of the things. There were also little keepsakes her mother had saved: a ticket from their trip to Zakopane, old jewellery of little value but which her mother wore every day, a few photographs from Marta's childhood.

She stopped at a photo in which she was five and her mother was holding her in her arms. They were both laughing. Marta remembered that day – they were on the beach, the wind blowing through their hair, and her father was taking the picture.

– Mum – she whispered. – Why can't I let go of this?

And then she heard in her head the voice from the podcast she had listened to a few days earlier: _"If this thing is important to you, why do you hide it in a box instead of putting it on display?"_

Marta looked at the photograph. It was important to her. Truly important. So instead of putting it back in the box, she decided to frame it. And place it on the shelf in the living room. Where she would see it every day.

She went through the rest of the things once more. She kept a few: the notebook with recipes, the ring her mother always wore, and a small rose-shaped brooch. The rest went into the bag.

She didn't cry. She felt calm. And she knew her mother would be proud of her.

---

## IV. The desk

The desk in her study was the worst. It was covered with papers from two years ago, unread letters, invoices that had long expired. In the middle lay a laptop, and next to it a coffee mug that had already grown a life of its own.

Marta sat down at the desk and started sorting. Most of the papers ended up in the bin. The letters that didn't matter followed them. Invoices – into a separate folder, but only those that might still be useful.

She also found a notebook in which she had once started writing a story. A tale about a girl who runs away from the city to find herself. Only a few pages had been written. Marta read them and smiled. It was good. Really good. Back then, a few years earlier, she had decided it was silly, that she had no talent, that it wasn't worth the time. And she had hidden the notebook in a drawer.

Now she took it out and placed it on the desk. Next to the laptop.

– I'll finish you – she said. – Someday. Now I'm ready.

And then she felt that this wasn't just a promise. It was a decision. And that this decision had a power she hadn't understood before.

---

## V. The storage room

Once she had dealt with most of the things, Marta found a few cardboard boxes in the basement that she had completely forgotten about. They were marked with dates: 2015, 2017, 2019. She opened the first one.

Inside were clothes from the time she had worked at a corporation. Suits, shirts, elegant shoes. Things she had worn every day but hated. She had kept them "for a rainy day", in case she had to go back to that life.

She picked up a suit. It was black, perfectly cut, expensive. And completely wrong for her.

– I'm not going back – she said. – I don't want to go back.

She put it in the bag. And did the same with the rest.

In the second box were books from her student days. Textbooks that were supposed to help her career but which she had never opened after the exams. She had kept them because she thought they proved how smart she was. But really they proved how lost she had been.

She kept only one. A book about writing. About how to find your voice.

The rest went to be given away.

The third box was the hardest. Inside were letters from Marek. The ones he had written to her at the beginning of their relationship, and the ones they had exchanged after the breakup. They lay neatly arranged, as if someone wanted to preserve the memory of something that no longer existed.

Marta took them in her hands. She read a few first lines. Then she closed the envelope.

– This is no longer your story – she said. – Your story is now. And you're writing it yourself.

She put the letters into a separate bag. Not into the rubbish bag, but into a bag she intended to burn. Not out of hatred. Out of gratitude. For teaching her how to love. And for teaching her how to leave.

---

## VI. The evening

When she finished, the apartment grew bright. Not because she had turned on the light – because she had opened the blinds. The sun poured through the windows, illuminating every corner. And suddenly she saw that this apartment was beautiful. She just hadn't seen it before.

She sat down on the sofa, which was finally free. Around her lay bags of things to give away, but she felt they weren't her burden. They were her choice.

She picked up her phone and texted her friend:

_"I did it. I cleaned up. And you know what? It wasn't clutter. It was me waiting for change."_

Her friend replied after a moment:

_"You're amazing. Now change the rest."_

Marta smiled. She knew the rest was already changing. That every box she had opened, every thing she had let go of, was a step towards herself. Her real self.

And tomorrow, in the morning, instead of saying "I'll do it tomorrow", she would say: "I did it". And she would move on.

Because home isn't a place where you live. Home is a place where you are. And where you become.

---

**THE END**
