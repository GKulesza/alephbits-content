## Text

**THE CONCRETE HOUSE**

## I. THE BEGINNING

Anna came home at 9:47 pm. The workshop had run an hour over, as usual. People didn't want to leave; they asked the questions they asked at every workshop: "But how am I supposed to do it? Where do I find time for myself? Why doesn't he understand me?"

She answered patiently, with a smile that cost her more energy than anyone could have guessed. She was an expert on relationships. A psychologist. An author of books about boundaries, love, psychological games. She knew what to say, how to say it, when to say it.

She knew everything about relationships.

And that was exactly why her own relationship was a ruin she couldn't repair.

In the hallway she took off her shoes and heard a soft sizzle from the kitchen. Paweł was frying something in a pan. It smelled of garlic and thyme. Her mother's favourite dish, which she hadn't eaten in years, ever since her mother stopped coming to see them.

— Hi — he said without turning around. — I thought you'd be back earlier. I made your favourite...

— You didn't have to — she cut him off. — I told you I didn't know when I'd be back.

Paweł finally turned around. In his hand he held a wooden spoon and a smile that was more of a question than an answer.

— I wanted to.

Anna felt something break inside her. Not in a bad way — in a way she couldn't name. Somewhere inside, in her concrete house, a small crack appeared.

She sat down at the table, rested her head on her hands and said:

— Today at the workshop we talked about games. That woman was telling us about her husband... that he always wants to help her, and she feels that it's not love, just... rescue work. That he rescues because he's afraid that without it he won't be loved.

Paweł set a plate in front of her. Pasta with sauce, finely chopped basil. A smell she remembered from childhood, from before the time her mother stopped cooking.

— And do you think that's how it is? — he asked quietly. — That I rescue you?

Anna raised her eyes. For a moment she looked at his hands. Hands that were always ready to help. To hug. To reach out when she was falling.

Hands that had never hurt her.

And yet, when she looked at them, she saw other hands. Hard, worn hands that hit and yanked. Her father's hands.

— I don't know — she said quietly. — I guess I don't know how to accept help.

---
## II. THE DEVELOPMENT

The next morning Anna woke up at 5:47, before the alarm went off. Paweł slept beside her, breathing evenly, calmly. She looked at him for a moment — at his face, which at night seemed younger, as if the everyday worries drifted away along with consciousness.

She remembered what she had said yesterday at the workshop, when one of the participants asked about trust:

_"If I carry the belief inside me that I am not enough, then I will never in my life tell you what I need."_

She got up quietly, poured herself some coffee and sat down in the living room with a laptop. She opened the file she had been working on for months — a book about relational games, whose title was "Prisoners of Relational Games".

On the screen was the last paragraph she had written yesterday before the workshop:

_"The 'gotcha' game is when one person looks for evidence that the other is not enough. It's a defence mechanism that protects against closeness — because if I catch you failing, I don't have to be afraid that you'll be the first to reject me."_

She read it three times. Then she closed the laptop and looked out the window. It was raining outside, and she was thinking about how many times over the past year she had caught Paweł failing. How many times she had proven to him that he wasn't good enough. How many times she had said:

— You forgot again. You were late again. Again you didn't do what I said.

And every time he apologized, she felt a strange satisfaction. Not malicious — more like she was proving to herself that she didn't have to rely on him. That it was better to count on herself.

_"You can count, child, so count on yourself" — she heard her mother's voice.

She went back to the bedroom. Paweł was still asleep. She sat on the edge of the bed and placed her hand on his shoulder.

— Paweł — she whispered. — We need to talk.

He opened his eyes. In his gaze she saw the same thing as always — readiness. He was ready for anything. For her anger, her sadness, her silence. He was ready to accept, to understand, to forgive.

And that was the worst part. That he was always ready.

— About what? — he asked, rubbing his eyes.

— About the fact that... — she stopped. In her head appeared a memory from yesterday's workshop, when one of the participants talked about her therapy. About how she had discovered that all her life she had been playing the game "if it weren't for you". That she blamed others because she was afraid to look in the mirror.

— About the fact that I was playing a game — she said at last. — All the time. With you. With my mother. With everyone.

Paweł sat up in bed. For a moment he looked at her in silence, and then asked:

— What game?

Anna took a deep breath.

— "Gotcha". And "I must be brave". And "if it weren't for you". All at once.

---
## III. THE TURNING POINT

They sat in the kitchen over a breakfast that nobody ate. Their coffee was going cold in front of them, and the rain outside had stopped, giving way to a grey, still sky.

— Tell me about your father — said Paweł.

Anna froze. It was a question she had been avoiding for fifteen years. Ever since she met Paweł, from the first dates, when she said her father was dead, even though she knew he was alive — somewhere out there, in another city, with another family.

— I don't want to.

— I know. — Paweł took her hand. — But if you want to stop playing, you have to see where those cards came from.

Anna felt like crying. For so many years she had pretended to be strong. That she needed no one. That she would manage. And now this man, who had always been by her side, who had never left, was asking her to open the door to the room in which she had locked her past.

— He had hard hands — she said quietly. — He worked in a factory. He brought home the smell of metal and grease. And sometimes... — she stopped, pressed her lips together. — Sometimes those hands hit.

Paweł didn't let go of her hand.

— And what did you do then?

— I hid. Under the bed. In the wardrobe. Anywhere. And I told myself that when I grew up, I would be stronger. That no one would ever hurt me. That I wouldn't need anyone, because if I need someone, I give them power over me.

— And that's why you never ask me for help.

— Yes.

Anna looked at her hands, resting on the table. Hands that wrote books, ran workshops, helped others. But she never reached them out for help.

— I remembered something you once said at a workshop — said Paweł. — You said that children play games because they don't know how else to get attention. That if they don't get love, they try to get at least attention. Even negative attention.

— Yes, that's true.

— And you, Anna? What attention are you trying to get?

Anna was silent for a long moment. And then, for the first time in years, she said something she had never confessed to anyone:

— That I'm not my mother. That I'm not a woman who lets herself be hit, who endures, who stays silent. That I'm stronger. That I'll manage.

Paweł stood up and walked over to her. He sat down on a chair beside her, still holding her hand.

— But you don't have to be stronger — he said quietly. — It's enough that you are yourself. And that you let me love you.

Anna felt something break inside her. The concrete house she had been building all her life began to crumble. And for the first time, she didn't want to save it.

---
## IV. THE ENDING

A month later Anna returned to the same place — her kitchen, at the same table. But this time something was different. Paweł stood at the stove, frying pasta with garlic and thyme. She sat at the table with a notebook in which she was writing down her thoughts.

— Do you know what I discovered? — she asked.

— What?

— That all my life I was afraid that if I stopped being strong, no one would choose me. That if I showed weakness, I would be left alone. And yet... — she stopped, looking at him. — And yet you stayed, even though I was weak. Even though I played those silly games. Even though I often hurt you.

Paweł set a plate in front of her.

— I knew it wasn't you. It was your monster.

Anna smiled. For the first time in a long while, she felt she could breathe.

— Thank you for not running away.

— There was nowhere to run — he answered. — Your walls were too high. But I knew that once you hid behind them, you would eventually come out.

She took his hand and pressed it to her cheek. A hand that had never hurt her. A hand that was ready to help, but not to rescue.

— Paweł — she said. — I'm sorry. And thank you.

— For what?

— For waiting. And for seeing me, even when I couldn't see myself.

He sat down beside her. The rain outside started again, but this time it was different — gentler, as if it were cleansing the air.

And then Anna understood that she had left the game.

Not because she had won. But because she had stopped playing it.

---
## V. EPILOGUE

A few weeks later Anna ran workshops for women who felt just as lost as she had been. She told them about the concrete house she had built around herself. About how all her life she had thought she had to be hard in order to survive. And then she discovered that real strength isn't hardness. It's the ability to open up to another person.

At one of the workshops, a woman raised her hand and asked:

— But how am I supposed to open up, when all my life people have let me down? How can I believe that this time it will be different?

Anna looked at her hands. Hands that had once been fists. Hands that were now open.

— You don't have to believe — she answered. — You only have to try. And let someone help you break up that concrete. Piece by piece.

After the workshop she went home. Paweł was waiting for her with dinner — pasta with garlic and thyme again. And again the smell that reminded her of childhood.

But this time she no longer felt sadness.

She felt only gratitude.

She walked up to him, took his hands in hers and said:

— Thank you for being here.

And he answered:

— Thank you for coming back.

And then she understood that she had left the game.

Not because she had won. But because she had stopped playing it.

---
**THE END**
