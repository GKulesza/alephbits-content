## Text

**THE STONE OF MEMORY**


## I. Berlin, Unter den Linden, 2026

Marta entered the new building of the Polish embassy for the first time. It was huge, bright, all glass – completely different from the previous one she remembered from childhood. Back then, the Polish embassy of the PRL era in the GDR was a grey, gloomy block that nobody wanted to visit.

Now life was teeming here. Several hundred people, businessmen, politicians, journalists, crowded into the main hall, waiting for the conference to begin. Marta was among them, in a navy blazer, notebook in hand. She had come from Warsaw to see it with her own eyes. Her grandfather had been one of those who had fought in the east in the forties and then been swept away by history. Grandfather never talked about the war. Only on his deathbed, at the age of ninety, did he say:

_"Marta, over there, in Berlin, there is a stone. Not for me, not for us. But I'd like someone to see it someday. So they'd know that Poles were there."_

She didn't know what he meant. She thought he was delirious, that these were the last mists before the end. Until now.

At the conference, the floor was taken by a tall, slender man in a dark suit. Mateusz Fałkowski, director of the Pilecki Institute in Berlin. He spoke calmly, without pathos, but Marta heard something in his voice that moved her – a kind of quiet, unshakeable certainty.

_"For many years Poland did not exist in German media"_ – he said. _"We were that country nobody talked about. Now this is changing. But that doesn't mean we're already where we want to be. We're still building ourselves up. We still have to build institutions that will represent us. And we still have to remember those whose voices were drowned out."_

Marta wrote quickly, but at some point she stopped. She noticed that someone was standing at the side of the hall, leaning against the wall. An older man, around seventy, with a grey beard and eyes that looked with attention, but without surprise. As if he had heard all of this before.

After the conference Marta went up to him.

— Excuse me, are you from the Institute?

The man smiled.

— No. I'm just a witness.

— To what?

— To what was said here. To this change. Once, when I came to Berlin, nobody wanted to talk to me. I was a Pole, meaning someone inferior. And now... — he looked around the hall — now I have the feeling that they're the ones curious about us.

Marta introduced herself. The man said his name was Tomasz, that he was a retired historian who had lived in Berlin for years.

— My grandfather told me about a stone — Marta said. — That it lies somewhere around here. That I should find it.

Tomasz looked at her carefully.

— Your grandfather was right. The stone exists. But it isn't easy to find. It's not a monument, it has no inscription. It's just a boulder, on which someone long ago carved a date. And initials.

— Whose?

— Of Polish soldiers. Those who died in Berlin in forty-five. They're on no list, they're in no museum. They exist only on that stone. And in the memory of those who know where to look for it.

---

## II. Warsaw, 1989

When Marta was little, her grandfather, Colonel Władysław Kowalski, used to take her for walks in Łazienki Park. He walked slowly, leaning on his cane, and talked about a world that no longer existed.

— Do you see that monument? — he would say, pointing at some sculpture. — A German once stood here. And then we removed him. And put up our own.

— Grandfather, were you in Berlin?

Grandfather was silent for a moment.

— I was. There, where the war ended. I saw buildings burning, people fleeing. And I saw the stone.

— What stone?

— An ordinary one. Grey. But someone had written our names on it. The soldiers who died there. I don't know who did it. Maybe one of ours, maybe one of the Germans. But I know that stone exists.

Marta didn't understand then. She thought her grandfather was talking about some forgotten monument. Only many years later, when she read documents from the archives, did she understand that it wasn't a monument. It was an act of memory. And that her grandfather, though he had survived the war, had spent his whole life looking for that stone, to see whether his comrades had been remembered.

He never found it before he died.

---

## III. Berlin, Mitte district, a week later

Tomasz led Marta to an old, neglected courtyard between two tenement houses. It was cramped, with bottles and cans lying around. Nobody cleaned up here, nobody looked in.

— It's here — he said, pointing to a corner of the courtyard, where a grey, unremarkable boulder was set into the ground.

Marta knelt down and read the carved letters:

_"K. Kowalski, J. Nowak, S. Wiśniewski, 1945. They fell for freedom."_

Below, in a small, barely visible hand, someone had added:

_"Don't forget them. They were there too."_

Marta touched the cold stone with her fingers. A long silence caught in her throat.

— Grandfather talked about them — she whispered. — About all of them. About Janek and Staszek. He said they were his friends.

Tomasz stood beside her, silent.

— For years nobody remembered them — he said at last. — The Germans didn't want to talk about them, because it was an uncomfortable history. We didn't want to talk about them, because it was Berlin, the city of the enemy. And they lay here, in this stone, without a word.

Marta stood up.

— What can we do?

— We can tell their story. We can make sure the stone is no longer forgotten. We can make sure that the Germans who live here learn that Polish bones lie under this courtyard. And that we, the Poles, remember.

Marta nodded. She knew what she would do. She would return to Warsaw, write an article, tell the story of the stone, of her grandfather, of his friends. Not as a journalist — as a granddaughter. As someone who wants memory to survive.

And then she would come back to Berlin. She would find a way to put a plaque beside the stone. So that nobody could ever say they didn't know.

---

## IV. Berlin, six months later

The plaque was in place. A small one, in bronze, with an inscription in two languages: Polish and German. Someone had placed it on the initiative of the Pilecki Institute, with the consent of the district authorities.

Marta stood beside it, watching the little group of people who had come for the unveiling. There were Polish diplomats, German officials, journalists, and also a few older men who stood silently at the side. Veterans. Those who had survived.

Tomasz came up to her.

— I thought you wouldn't come.

— I had to — said Marta. — Grandfather would have wanted it.

A young man she didn't know approached her. He was around thirty, with dark hair, and held an old, worn photograph in his hand.

— Excuse me — he said in Polish with a foreign accent. — I'm looking for someone who knew these men. My grandfather was German. He lived in this district in forty-five. When he saw the Polish soldiers who had died, he went to them to bury them. It was he who placed the stone.

Marta looked at him, surprised.

— Your grandfather placed the stone?

The young man nodded.

— Yes. He always talked about them. He said they weren't enemies. That they were people. And that they deserved to be remembered.

Marta didn't know what to say. For a moment she looked at him, then at the stone, then at the rising sun that lit up the courtyard.

And then she understood.

The stone wasn't only for Poles. It was for everyone who believed that memory matters more than divisions. For the grandfather who didn't want his friends to be forgotten. For the German soldier who, in the middle of war, found in himself enough courage to bury enemies. For her, who years later had put up the plaque. And for this young man, who had come to say: _"I haven't forgotten."_

— Can we hug? — asked the young man.

Marta nodded, and he embraced her gently, as if he wanted to pass on to her more than just a gesture — his grandfather's whole story, the whole weight of what had happened.

— Thank you — she said. — Thank you for not forgetting.

— Thank you to you. For reminding others.

---

## V. Warsaw, a year later

Marta sat in her small flat in Mokotów and read a letter from Mateusz Fałkowski. He wrote that the stone had become a symbol. That the German media had started writing about it, that school trips had appeared, that the Pilecki Institute now ran workshops for German teachers who wanted to teach about the Polish contribution to World War II.

_"The change in how Poland is perceived doesn't happen through politicians"_ – he wrote. _"It happens through stories like yours. Through people who remember. Through stones that have names. Through doing our own thing, without asking what others think of us."_

Marta put down the letter and looked out of the window. Outside was Warsaw, her city, her country. Once she had thought the Germans were foreign, that that world was closed to her. But today she knew there are no closed worlds. There are only stories that must be told. And stones that must be discovered.

She picked up a pen and wrote on a piece of paper:

_"Don't forget them. They were there too."_

Then she put the paper into a drawer, next to a photograph of her grandfather. In it he was smiling, in uniform, cane in hand. As if he knew that his friends would finally be found.

---

## VI. Berlin, the courtyard in Mitte, now

The stone still lies there. The plaque still stands. Sometimes people who have heard about it come here. They leave flowers, small candles, sometimes just a card with the words: _"Thank you."_

And somewhere out there, in Warsaw, in the flat in Mokotów, Marta is still writing. About her grandfather, about the stone, about the German who placed it, and about the grandson who came years later to see it.

And she knows this isn't the end. That every story that gets told changes something in the world. Even if only a little. Even if only for one person.

Because stones don't speak. But the people who find them can speak for them.

And that is the most important thing.

---


**THE END**
