## Text

**THE MUG**

## I. 14 years and 17 days

When Wojtek turned fourteen, Ewa thought the worst was behind her. Nappies, sleepless nights, teething, the terrible twos – all of that was behind her. So were the first school years, with their endless projects, parent-teacher meetings and nights spent over exercise books.

She was wrong.

She sat in the living room with a mug of tea that had long gone cold, staring at her phone. Wojtek had shut himself in his room an hour ago, after she had told him he couldn't go to the party that, as he claimed, "the whole class is going to".

– You don't understand me! – he shouted, slamming the door.

And that was basically it. She didn't understand. And she felt that the harder she tried, the worse it got.

Ewa was a psychologist by training, but what was happening between her and her son didn't fit in any textbook. She knew she should stay calm, keep her distance, not let herself be drawn into the spiral of emotions. And yet every day she ended up in the same place – furious, helpless, exhausted.

She looked at her reflection in the window. She saw a woman who no longer resembled the one she used to be. Eyes shadowed, shoulders drooping, lips pressed into a thin line. She had stopped smiling. She had stopped being herself.

And then, as if by a higher power, a phone appeared in her hand. And on the screen – a post. By a woman who called herself "a supportive mother of a teenager".

_"When your child screams that they hate you, it doesn't mean they hate you. It means they don't have the words to say what they really feel."_

Ewa read that three times. Then she started scrolling on.

_"Children are not against us. They are for themselves."_

_"Punishment teaches four things: resentment, rebellion, revenge and withdrawal. Nothing else."_

_"You don't have to be perfect. You have to be present."_

She stopped at that last one. Present. When had she last been present? Not physically present – that she always was. But truly present? Listening without judging, without ready-made solutions, without the conviction that she knew better?

She couldn't remember.

She scrolled on and found information about a workshop. She was so absorbed that she didn't even notice Wojtek leaving his room and coming into the kitchen. She only noticed him when he stood beside her.

– What are you doing? – he asked suspiciously.

– Looking for something – she answered.

– For what?

Ewa hesitated.

– For a way to be a better mum.

Wojtek looked at her in disbelief.

– You're fine.

– I'm not. I shout at you. I don't listen to you. And then I feel awful.

Wojtek shrugged.

– All parents do that.

– That doesn't mean I have to.

The teenager was silent for a moment.

– You can try – he said at last. – But don't expect me to trust you right away.

Ewa smiled.

– I don't.

---

## II. The first workshop

The room was full. Ewa sat in the back row so that no one could see her trembling hands. In front of her stood Kasia – a woman with a calm voice who looked as if she knew exactly what each person in that room was feeling.

– How many of you feel like helpless parents? – she asked to begin with.

Several dozen hands went up. Ewa raised hers too.

– That's good – said Kasia. – Because if you feel helpless, it means you want to change something.

For the next three hours Ewa listened about mistaken goals of behaviour, about children seeking a sense of belonging, about the difference between punishment and consequences. Kasia talked about the lizard brain, about mirror neurons, about the fact that shouting breeds shouting, and calm breeds calm.

What struck her most was a sentence Kasia said at the end:

– Positive discipline is not for children. It's for you. Children are as they are. It's you who have to change.

On the way home Ewa thought about what she had heard. About how often she reacts instead of responding. How often she says "no" before she has even heard what it's about. How often her own emotions obscure what really matters.

She came home. Wojtek was sitting in the living room, staring at his phone screen. He looked up when he heard the door.

– So? – he asked.

– Weird – she admitted. – But good.

– Did they teach you how to raise us?

– They taught us how to work on ourselves.

Wojtek looked at her carefully.

– And are you going to?

– I am.

– We'll see.

Ewa smiled.

– We'll see.

---

## III. A month later

Ewa came home after another workshop. In her bag she had a set of cards that Kasia recommended to every parent. On each card was one tip, one exercise, one principle.

Wojtek noticed it right away.

– What's that?

– Cards. Tools.

– Can I see?

– Sure.

Wojtek took the deck and started going through it. After a moment he divided the cards into three piles.

– These you already use – he said, pointing at the first pile. – It's middling, but you're trying.

Ewa looked at the second pile.

– And these?

– These are what you still need to work on.

– And the third?

Wojtek smiled.

– The third is what you have no idea about.

Ewa laughed. And then she realised she was laughing sincerely – for the first time in weeks.

– You know what? – she said. – Thank you.

– For what?

– For telling me.

Wojtek shrugged.

– Someone has to.

That evening Ewa took out one of the cards. On it was written: _"Before you react, ask yourself: what do I want to teach my child in this situation?"_

She memorised it. And she decided that from tomorrow she would use it. Even if she didn't always get it right.

---

## IV. The day she didn't shout

It happened three days later. Wojtek came back from school with an F in maths. He was angry, closed off. He threw his backpack on the floor and went to his room.

Ewa felt a wave of emotion rising inside her. She wanted to shout, to go in after him and tell him that this wasn't right, that he had to study, that he would have problems in life.

She held herself back. She took out of her pocket the card on which she had written the principle: _"Before you react, ask yourself: what do I want to teach my child?"_

The answer came quickly. She didn't want to teach him fear of failure. She wanted to teach him that mistakes are an opportunity to learn. That you don't have to be perfect to be valuable.

She knocked on his door.

– Come in – she heard.

Wojtek sat on the bed, staring at the floor.

– I know you've had a bad day – said Ewa. – Do you want to talk about it?

– No.

– Then we won't talk. But I'm here if you want to.

Wojtek looked at her, surprised. He had expected a lecture, criticism, punishment. Instead he heard: _"I'm here."_

– Thank you – he said quietly.

Ewa left, closing the door. She knew this was only the beginning. That there would be worse days, that she would lose her temper, that she would make mistakes. But at that moment she felt something she hadn't felt for a long time – that she was on the right track.

---

## V. Self-esteem and worth

A few weeks later Wojtek came to her with another F. This time in Polish. Ewa took a deep breath.

– I know you're disappointed – she said.

– It's not fair! – he burst out. – The teacher doesn't like me. She always gives me worse grades!

Ewa sat down beside him.

– And what if it were true? What if you really got a grade that doesn't reflect your effort? What then?

– It would mean I'm worthless.

– Does your worth depend on one grade?

Wojtek was silent.

– Look – said Ewa. – Imagine you have two things: a sense of worth and self-esteem. A sense of worth is the conviction that you are valuable, no matter what you do. Self-esteem is how you judge yourself in a particular situation.

– And so what?

– If your self-esteem is low, it doesn't mean your worth has fallen. You can be bad at maths and still be a valuable person.

Wojtek looked up.

– Do you really think so?

– I think it's not that I think so, but that that's how it is. No matter what I think.

The teenager was silent for a moment.

– So I don't have to be the best to be OK?

– You don't have to be the best to be loved. And you don't have to be perfect to be enough.

Wojtek nodded. He didn't say anything, but Ewa could see that something in him had stirred.

---

## VI. The other side

Ewa started running workshops. First for a few friends, then for larger groups. Kasia supported her, shared materials, answered questions.

At one of the workshops a woman stood up. She was about forty, her eyes red from crying.

– Everything you say sounds beautiful – she said – but my child doesn't listen to me. I'm nobody to him.

Ewa walked over to her.

– I understand you. I thought the same. But the truth is that children do listen to us. Just differently. They don't listen to our words, but to our emotions. If you're calm, they feel it. If you're angry – they feel that too.

The woman looked at her uncertainly.

– And how do I change that?

– You can't change it from one day to the next. It's a process. But it's worth starting with one step: taking care of yourself.

Ewa reached for a cup.

– Imagine that your resources are a cup. Every day something is taken from it. Arguments, duties, worries. If you don't fill it up again, in the end it will be empty. And then you have nothing to give your child.

– So how do I fill it?

– First: let yourself rest. Second: ask for help. Third: don't strive for perfection. Perfection is the enemy of presence.

The woman smiled through her tears.

– That's hard.

– I know. But I'm with you.

---

## VII. A year later

Wojtek turned sixteen. He still had bad days, he still shut himself in his room, he still sometimes shouted. But Ewa was no longer the same woman either.

One evening, when he came back from a party, he saw her in the living room with a mug of tea and the positive discipline cards.

– You're still doing that? – he asked.

– All the time – she answered. – It's not something that ends.

Wojtek sat down beside her.

– You know you're a good mum?

Ewa looked at him, surprised.

– How do you know?

– Because you try. And you don't pretend everything is perfect.

– Is that enough?

– It's everything I need.

Ewa took his hand.

– I love you, Wojtek.

– I know, Mum.

And then Ewa understood that what she had been learning over the past year wasn't only about parenting. It was about life. About being present, being authentic, being yourself.

And what mattered most came to her from the person she loved most. From her son. Who, it turned out, had been seeing her all along.

Even when she couldn't see herself.

**THE END**
