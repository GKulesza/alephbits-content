## Text

**SEVEN MOUNTAINS AND ONE OCEAN**


When Marek first heard that South Korea had bought a thousand tanks from Poland, he thought it was a mistake. It was 2022, he was sitting in his apartment in Warsaw, drinking coffee and reading headlines on his phone. "Poland, the fastest-growing country in Europe" – that he could still understand. But Korea? Tanks? Koreans, after all, made him think of K-pop, dramas and face masks from Rossmann. Not the defence industry.

– What do they want to do with them? – he asked himself, scrolling through the article.

He didn't know then that a few years later he would be standing at Incheon airport himself, looking at giant screens with writing in Hangul and wondering how on earth he had ended up there.


**The reason for the trip**

Marek was an architect. He worked in a design office in Warsaw that had won the tender for the modernisation of one of the Korean museums. It was a huge contract – the first of its kind for a Polish company in Asia. His boss told him plainly: "You're going for a month. You'll see how they work, you'll get to know their style, you'll learn something new. And while you're at it – you'll see whether it even makes sense".

Marek had no idea what to expect. He knew as much about Korea as the average Pole: that it is divided into two parts, that it has a powerful economy, that Koreans are great programmers and that their women wear beautiful, colourful dresses. And also that they like K-pop. That was all.

Before leaving, he decided to do a little research. He typed into Google: "What do Koreans think of Poles". The results were surprising.

"Most Koreans don't know where Poland is."  
"They associate Chopin, Auschwitz and Warsaw."  
"Lewandowski is the most famous Pole."  
"Poland is the land of the Witcher."  
"Poland is seen as the fastest-growing country in Europe."  
"Poles are considered friendly and hard-working."

Marek smiled. That wasn't a bad image. But was it true? He decided to find out for himself.


**First days in Seoul**

When he got off the plane, he felt the humid, hot air. Seoul welcomed him with a grey-blue sky and the smell of exotic spices. He caught a taxi to the hotel. The driver, an older man in sunglasses, looked at him in the rear-view mirror.

– Poland? – he asked in broken English.

– Yes – Marek answered.

– Lewandowski! – the driver smiled, giving a thumbs up. – Good footballer.

– Thank you – Marek replied, surprised.

– Witcher too! – the driver added. – I played all three parts.

Marek laughed. His first encounter with Korea was far friendlier than he had expected.


**Meeting the team**

At the design office in central Seoul Marek met his team. There were eight Koreans – all young, smiling, dressed in elegant suits. They introduced themselves, handed him business cards with both hands, which impressed him. In Poland no one attached that much importance to etiquette.

The first conversation was a little stiff. Marek spoke English, they did too, but with a distinct accent. For the first few days he felt like a stranger on a planet where everyone speaks a code he doesn't fully understand. But then, during a shared lunch in a small restaurant with kimchi and bulgogi, they started talking about Poland.

– Is it true that it's very cold in Poland? – asked one of the designers, Young-hee.

– It depends – Marek replied. – In winter it can be freezing, but in summer it's pleasant. And in your country?

– We have seasons too – she said. – But our winters are shorter and the summers very hot. And humid.

– Just like now.

They laughed.

– You know – added one of the engineers, Min-ho – I've always wanted to visit Warsaw. I've heard it's beautiful after the reconstruction. And that it has lots of green spaces.

– That's true – said Marek. – And I think you'd like it. But I think you have a beautiful city too.

Young-hee nodded.

– We do. But it wasn't always like that. After the Korean War everything was destroyed. I think Poles understand what it means to rebuild a country after a war.

Marek felt something break inside him. That was the first deeper connection. Not through tanks, not through K-pop. Through history.


**Language as a key**

One evening, after work, Marek walked around the Hongdae district. He saw graffiti, record shops, groups of teenagers dancing to K-pop. At some point he stopped in front of a small bookshop. In the window lay a book for learning Korean.

He bought it. Not because he wanted to learn – but because he wanted to understand how Koreans see the world. He saw that Korean has its own alphabet – Hangul – and that it's a language in which the verb always stands at the end of the sentence. That was completely different from Polish. But the more he read, the more he was fascinated by this way of thinking – logical, orderly, yet full of contexts and subtleties.

Later, during a conversation with Min-ho, he asked:

– Is it true that in Korean there are different levels of politeness?

Min-ho laughed.

– Yes. And it's very important. If you speak to an older person, you have to use formal language. To a friend – informal. And to a boss – yet another.

– It must be difficult.

– For us it's natural. But for foreigners – yes, probably difficult. But it's not impossible. Poles are very smart, right?

– I think so – Marek replied with a smile.

– Besides – added Min-ho – I've heard Polish is difficult too. It has seven cases, right?

– Seven. Or more. We're not sure ourselves.

They laughed, and Marek felt that these two languages – Polish and Korean – were like two distant worlds that had suddenly found a common point. Both difficult. Both rich. Both full of history.


**Poland through Korean eyes**

At the end of his stay, during a farewell dinner, Marek asked his colleagues directly:

– What do you really think about Poland?

There was silence. Then Young-hee began to speak.

– I think Poland is a country that has strength in it. You know, we Koreans have also been through hard times. War, occupation, poverty. But now we're one of the richest countries in the world. And I think Poland is following the same path.

– And Poles? – asked Marek.

– Poles are... interesting – said Min-ho. – I've heard they're very hospitable, but a bit closed at first. That's similar to us. We don't smile at strangers on the street, but once we get to know someone, we become very warm.

– And one more thing – added another engineer. – Poles are very proud of their history. I see it in your films, in your music. I respect that. In Korea we're also proud, but sometimes maybe too much.

Marek listened and felt that what he was hearing was sincere. There was no lie or political correctness in it. Only observation.

– And what will you tell your children about Poland when you go back to your country someday? – he asked.

– That it's a beautiful country – answered Young-hee. – And that it's worth getting to know.


**The return**

A month later Marek returned to Poland. At Warsaw airport he felt a strange relief. He was home. But something inside him had changed. Korea was no longer just a country on the map with which Poland had trade relations. It had become a place where he had friends. A place where people spoke about him with respect. A place that looked at Poland as at its former self – a country that rises from its knees and walks into the future.

He picked up the Korean language book he had bought in Seoul. He opened it to the first page. There he found the words he remembered from the conversation with Min-ho:

_"Czułda" – to give something to someone informally.  
"Czuła jo" – to give politely.  
"Tyrmn" – to give very formally._

He smiled. In the Korean language, as in life, everything depended on context. On who you give something to, in what situation, with what respect.

And then he thought about Poland. About how much it had changed in recent years. How it had gone from a country nobody wanted to talk about to a country Koreans think of with warmth.

Maybe that was the key. Not the tanks. Not K-pop. Not Chopin. Just that both countries know what reconstruction is. And that both countries believe in the future.

Marek closed the book. Outside the window the sun was shining. Somewhere out there, in Korea, his friends from the design office were just finishing work. And maybe, at this very moment, they were talking about Poland.

Not about a poor country in the East.

About a friend.


**THE END**
